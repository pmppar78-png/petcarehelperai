Strengths and Tradeoffs

Decision Guidance for

Ninety minutes and a duck dog, or a coat you brush to the skin

The Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retriever is the smallest retriever on this site's card: 17 to 21 inches, 35 to 50 pounds, often living 12 to 14 years. The breed was developed in Nova Scotia to toll ducks, which means to play and flash along the shore until birds swam in. The modern profile still wants that engine. It asks for 90 minutes or more of vigorous exercise and lists dock diving, agility, flyball, and obedience. Beginner-friendly is scored 3 out of 5. Housing is an active home with outdoor access and a fenced yard. Water is a bonus, and it is also a clue: this is a wet-dog plan.

The Old English Sheepdog sitting on the other side of the comparison is 21 inches and up, 60 to 100 pounds, and listed at 10 to 12 years. The job was driving cattle and sheep to market in the early nineteenth century, which is younger as a breed story than the "old" in the name suggests. Exercise is moderate to high, about one to two hours. The temperament words are adaptable, gentle, and smart, and children are rated excellent. The line that dominates the budget is grooming. Professional grooming is published at $1,200 to $2,400 a year, against the toller's grooming band of $100 to $400. If you remember one dollar from this page, remember that gap.

A toller week is brushing two or three times, daily when the coat is blowing, a monthly bath, clean ears, and trimmed nails. Food for a working toller is described as a sport or active formula, about two to three cups, $400 to $900 a year, with extra calories when the work is heavy. The sheepdog week is a brush that reaches skin, or a pelt, and the profile is plain that shaving a neglected coat damages the skin underneath. Food for that heavier dog is $600 to $1,000. Routine veterinary care lands at $300 to $600 on both cards. The time is what the toller charges. The salon invoice is what the sheepdog charges.

Say the diseases in the toller's order. Progressive retinal atrophy and an annual eye exam. Addison's disease, with lethargy, vomiting, and weight loss as the signs the page prints. Other immune-mediated disease. Collie eye anomaly. Hypothyroidism, so thyroid function gets monitored. Hip dysplasia is also on the toller materials. None of those is a home diagnosis. They are the list you take to the litter and later to the clinic. The sheepdog's order is different: hips and elbows, with an OFA hip figure of about 15.8 percent among evaluated dogs at the 60 to 100 pound size, then PRA, primary ciliary dyskinesia, deafness in some individuals, autoimmune disease that includes immune-mediated hemolytic anemia, and bloat. Thyroid testing is on the sheepdog screening sentence too.

A dull toller who vomits or drops weight is the Addison's appointment, the same day, with the warning box in mind. A sheepdog who retches and produces nothing, or whose abdomen swells, is the bloat appointment, the same hour, because the page lists gastric dilatation-volvulus for this deep-chested herder. A sheepdog puppy who does not respond unless you step into view needs a hearing check, because deafness is named. A toller puppy's eyes need the certificate, because collie eye anomaly and PRA are named. Mixing those scripts is how people buy the wrong follow-up.

Mileage is the other script. Ninety vigorous minutes will ruin a sheepdog puppy's growing hips and will bore an adult toller if you cut it down to a sheepdog's amble. One to two hours of moderate to high work will leave a toller inventing games with the trash, the fence, and the other dog's ears. Build the toller's day from retrieves and a class. Build the sheepdog's day from a long walk, training, and coat work that counts as contact time of its own. Summer heat sits harder on the coated drover. Move that dog early. The toller still needs water and shade, and the profile's love of swimming is a reason to rinse and dry ears after the pond.

Insurance planning is $350 to $700 a year on the toller table and about $40 to $65 a month in the shorter note, against $500 to $900 for the sheepdog. Ask whether Addison's and hereditary eye disease are paid on the toller policy. Ask whether hip disease, elbow disease, and bloat are paid on the sheepdog policy. The yearly totals on the toller page, about $1,800 to $4,000, can sit under a single year of sheepdog grooming plus food if you are paying a salon $2,400 and feeding a 90 pound dog. That arithmetic is why coat skill comes before temperament adjectives.

The fence is shared and the reason differs. The toller profile puts the fence in the housing paragraph because a 35 to 50 pound retriever with a 90-minute motor will leave. The sheepdog needs the fence because a 60 to 100 pound drover is a lot of dog to recover from a road, and because the hair over the eyes does not improve traffic judgment. Measure the crate for the adult body. The puppy measurement goes stale: a toller who finishes near 50 pounds, or a sheepdog who may pass 80 and keep growing coat. Buy the second crate when you know which number you got. A toller bed can be a simple washable pad. A sheepdog bed disappears under hair and has to be something you can clean, or the coat you just brushed rolls in it.

Children who do well with either dog are children who have been taught that a gentle sheepdog is still heavy and a playful toller is still fast. Excellent-with-kids is on both cards. It assumes supervision. The toller's sports list rewards speed. The sheepdog's bear-like shuffle, which the profile mentions, still covers ground when a child is carrying food. A place command is cheaper than a collision, and it is easier to install at 20 pounds than at 80.

Growth feeding stays inside the bands. A toller on heavy work can need the top of $400 to $900 and the upper end of two to three cups. A toller on the couch gets fat on that cup, and extra weight is a hip problem you added to a breed whose page already mentions hip dysplasia. A sheepdog puppy piled with food until the coat looks "prosperous" is how a 15.8 percent hip conversation becomes a surgery conversation. Feel the ribs. On the toller that takes a hand on the chest. On the sheepdog it takes a part in the coat all the way down. If you cannot find a rib, you cannot claim the dog is in working condition.

Choose the toller when you want the smallest retriever, you will spend 90 minutes, you will keep the Addison's signs and the eye calendar, and the grooming you want is a retriever shed. Choose the sheepdog when you want the 60 to 100 pound herder, you will brush to the skin or pay $1,200 to $2,400, and you will collect hip, elbow, eye, and thyroid papers and learn the bloat habit. Twelve to fourteen years of a duck dog is a long sports calendar. Ten to twelve years of a sheepdog is a long grooming calendar. The profiles already wrote both prices. The household only has to pick which invoice it will still open when the dog is old.

Write a toller day on paper before you write a check. Morning retrieve or a run, an evening training rep, and a brush if the coat is shedding. That is the 90 minutes in ordinary clothes. On a class night, agility or obedience replaces one of the walks and still leaves the dog wanting a job. A sheepdog day on the same paper is a long walk, a training rep, and a date with the comb that may take as long as the walk. One to two hours of movement plus coat care is a full evening. People who can do the comb and dislike the retrieve already know which line they will keep. People who light up at dock diving and go quiet at the word "mats" already know the other line.

Puppy months are when the bands are easiest to miss. A toller who eats the full two to three cups while doing puppy-class mileage gets heavy before the adult muscle arrives. Cut to the ribs. A sheepdog puppy who is praised for looking like a stuffed animal is being praised for the fat the 15.8 percent hip figure does not need. Keep growth food until the veterinarian says the big dog's plates are ready for adult food, and keep the toller on a sport cup only when the 90 minutes are real. Treats from the class bag count against both cups. A week of "only a little cheese" moves a 40 pound toller and a 70 pound sheepdog off the condition you wanted.

Ears, eyes, and thyroid are the shared chores with different stakes. After a toller swims, dry the ears. After a sheepdog walks in burs, comb them out before they felt. Book the toller's eye look because PRA and collie eye anomaly are on the list. Book the sheepdog's eye look because PRA is on that list too, and ask about breathing if primary ciliary dyskinesia was mentioned in the pedigree. Thyroid testing is written into the sheepdog screening sentence and into the toller's hypothyroidism line. One blood draw can be on the calendar for either dog. The reason you ordered it should match the page you adopted from.

Over a decade the invoices separate. Twelve to fourteen toller years of $400 to $900 food, $100 to $400 grooming, $300 to $600 routine veterinary care, and $350 to $700 insurance can live near the $1,800 to $4,000 yearly total already printed, until an Addison's workup or a dental cleaning under anesthesia steps outside it. Ten to twelve sheepdog years of $600 to $1,000 food and $1,200 to $2,400 grooming dominate before insurance at $500 to $900 is added. A lean old sheepdog with a combed coat is the cheap version of that breed. A matted one is a medical shave and a skin bill. A toller who still gets the 90 minutes at age ten is the sound version of that breed. A toller who gets the food and skips the work is the heavy version, and the hips were already on the page.
