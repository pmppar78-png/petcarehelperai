Hand-stripping, eyes, and the Lakeland's first season

A Lakeland terrier stands about 13.5 to 14.5 inches and often lives 12 to 15 years. The coat is a wire jacket. Clipping it ruins the texture and the color the breed is known for. Hand-stripping on a schedule, or a groomer who still strips, is part of owning the dog. Ask the clinic who in town does that work before you book a pet-store shave "to make it easy."

The health questions to put on the intake form are primary lens luxation, loose kneecaps, and Legg-Calve-Perthes disease. The breed card also names von Willebrand's disease. Ask the breeder for a DNA result on lens luxation, an eye certification, and a patella evaluation. Put those papers in the folder you take to the first veterinary visit, next to the vaccine record.

A suddenly painful, cloudy, red eye is a same-day emergency. The lens can slip, and the pressure can blind the eye overnight. Skip the photo and drive. Tell the clinic you have a Lakeland and that lens luxation is the worry, so the ophthalmology plan starts at triage. The veterinarian who can see the eye sets the pain control and the referral.

A young Lakeland that goes lame in one hind leg needs the same urgency for a different reason. Legg-Calve-Perthes disease deteriorates the femoral head. A slipping kneecap is a separate exam. Note which leg, whether the dog will bear weight, and when it started. Small terriers hide pain until the leg is carried. Waiting a weekend because "puppies tweak things" is how a surgical conversation starts late.

They dig, they bark, and they will take a small animal if they can reach it. A yard without a dug-in fence is a lost dog. A recall that works in the kitchen fails when a squirrel is involved. Use a leash and a long line while the first month of training is still food in a quiet room. Five minutes of paid attention beats a lecture. They are bold and friendly with their people and independent the moment the work gets dull.

Food is measured for a small wire-coated frame. Free-choice kibble and a couch lifestyle put weight on knees and hips you are already screening. Weigh the dog monthly. A stripping schedule and a lean waist are the two home jobs that change what the veterinarian sees at the yearly exam.

Startup costs on a dog this size are the crate, the food, the bed, and the stripping tools, in the same bands a small-terrier checklist uses: crate and setup often $50 to $300, a month of food $60 to $120, a bed $30 to $150, and a stripping knife or comb $20 to $80, with a first-supply total that commonly lands between $290 and $980. The money that matters more is the emergency fund for a red eye. Book the veterinarian before the puppy comes home, and ask for the lens-luxation conversation on day one.

The first veterinary hour should cover weight, vaccines still due, a patella check, a look at the eyes, and a plan for the lens-luxation DNA result if the breeder folder is empty. Ask who trims wire coats without shaving them. Ask what a red eye means for this breed so the night clinic already knows why you will not wait until morning. At home, walk the fence line and fill any gap a 14-inch dog can dig under. A Lakeland in the street is an emergency of a different kind, and the eye fund does not help a dog that is gone.
