Feeding Guidelines for Shih Tzu

What to Look For

  • Quality protein: A named meat (not "animal protein") as ingredient #1 ensures your Shih Tzu gets bioavailable amino acids.
  • No junk fillers: Corn, wheat, and soy are cheap bulk ingredients that add calories without much nutritional value for most dogs.
  • Right formula for the life stage: Growing, adult, and senior Shih Tzus have different caloric and nutrient requirements. Match the food to the stage.
  • Omega fatty acids: Look for omega-3 and omega-6 sources (fish oil, flaxseed) that support skin, coat, and joint health.
  • Proven digestibility: Choose brands with feeding trial data rather than those that only meet formulation standards on paper.

Best Food by Category

  • Best Overall: A complete, balanced formula with named animal protein and no unnecessary additives — the reliable everyday choice.
  • Best Value: Solid nutrition at a lower price point. Look for store brands that meet AAFCO standards without the marketing markup.
  • Best for Allergies: Single-protein or limited-ingredient formulas that reduce the chance of triggering food sensitivities.
  • Best for Aging Shih Tzu: Lower-calorie recipes with added glucosamine, chondroitin, or omega-3s for joint and mobility support.

Shih Tzu Nutritional Profile

Feeding a Shih Tzu well means accounting for their Small (9-16 lbs) frame and energy requirements. Smaller breeds need calorie-dense foods in manageable portions to fuel their faster metabolism. Protein quality matters more than protein quantity — look for whole animal proteins rather than processed concentrates.

Life-Stage Feeding Guide for Shih Tzu

Shih Tzu nutritional needs shift meaningfully across life stages. Young Shih Tzus need nutrient-dense food with higher protein and fat to support growth — typically 20-40% more calories per pound than adults. The transition to adult maintenance food should happen gradually around the time growth slows. As your Shih Tzu enters the senior phase (roughly the last third of their 10-18 years lifespan), a lower-calorie formula with added joint support becomes appropriate. Fresh water should always be available alongside meals.

Common Dietary Sensitivities in Shih Tzu

Watch for signs that your Shih Tzu's food is not agreeing with them: frequent scratching, red or waxy ears, inconsistent stool quality, or a dull coat. These can all point to dietary sensitivities. Rather than guessing by switching brands randomly, work with your vet on a structured elimination diet. It takes patience — typically two to three months — but it gives you a definitive answer about what your Shih Tzu can and cannot tolerate.

Ideal Portion Control for Shih Tzu

Measured meals beat free-feeding for virtually every Shih Tzu. Use the manufacturer's guidelines as a starting point, then adjust based on your Shih Tzu's body condition — you should be able to feel the ribs without seeing them, and there should be a visible waist from above. Weigh your Shih Tzu monthly and nudge portions up or down by 10-15% if weight trends in the wrong direction. Split daily food into two meals for adults, three to four for growing Shih Tzus, and keep treats under 10% of total daily calories.

Best for Weight Management

If your Shih Tzu needs to lose or maintain weight, choose a formula with L-carnitine and higher fiber content. These help Shih Tzu feel satisfied while consuming fewer calories, which is especially important given their low-moderate (30-60 min daily) activity level.

Understanding Shih Tzu's Dietary Heritage

A Shih Tzu's dietary needs are not arbitrary — they are rooted in what the breed was developed to do. With their typical energy level, this Shih Tzu burns calories differently than breeds of a similar size with lower drives. Understanding that context helps you choose food that genuinely matches your Shih Tzu's biology rather than defaulting to whatever is popular or heavily advertised.
