The bills a French lop insurance quote has to name

A French lop is a 10 to 15 pound rabbit, and the pen has to let that weight hop. Wire under a heavy body is how hocks go bald and then sore. Use a solid floor, a large enclosure, and hay in front of the rabbit all day. Pellets and a handful of greens are the side of the diet. They do not stand in for grass hay. A lop that fills up on pellets gets heavier than the 10 to 15 pounds the breed already carries, and the extra weight lands on feet that were already working hard.

The dollar lines already on this page are the ones to set next to a policy. Lifetime veterinary costs quoted here reach $15,000 to $45,000. Comprehensive premiums across the same years add up to $5,000 to $12,000. One $3,000 emergency, paid at 80 percent, covers most of what a year of premiums cost. Read those three lines against the three problems lop owners actually file: a damp ear, cheek teeth, and a gut that stops.

The ear canal under a lop does not air out. Head shaking and a sour smell are infection or mites until a veterinarian looks in the ear with a scope. Waiting a week turns a cleaning into a culture, a pain plan, and sometimes surgery. Ask the insurer whether ear disease is excluded as "breed typical" before you enroll. The exclusion is worth more than a five-dollar difference in the monthly quote.

Cheek teeth keep growing. Hay is what wears them. A rabbit that lives on pellets grows spurs, drops food, and then stops eating. A dental grind under anesthesia is one of the claims that makes the $3,000 line real. A policy that drops dental disease leaves that visit unpaid. So does a policy that pays the extraction and refuses the anesthesia.

Gastrointestinal stasis is the quiet emergency. A French lop that has produced no droppings by evening, sits hunched, and will not take hay needs an exotic veterinarian the same day. The clinic will want a weight, a temperature, and a decision about fluids and pain control. Insurance that starts the illness wait on the day you adopt is late for a rabbit that arrived from a show barn with a soft stool.

Long nails change how a heavy rabbit stands, and then the hocks take the load at the wrong angle. Trim on a schedule, or have the clinic do it at the same visit as the weight check. Sore hocks on a 10 to 15 pound body are a housing failure and a medical bill at the same time. Photograph the pen floor when you call, because the veterinarian will ask what the rabbit stands on.

Before the rabbit comes home, book a veterinarian who already treats lop rabbits and ask which of the three they see most: ears, teeth, or stasis. Match the policy to that answer. Stock hay, a solid-floor pen, a carrier, and a gram scale in the same week you request the quote. The first month goes badly when the hay runs out, the nails click on the wire, and the only clinic that will see a rabbit is forty minutes away after the gut has already stopped.
