Expert Feeding Tips for Hermit Crab (Marine) Owners

Experienced Hermit Crab (Marine) owners and species specialists recommend several feeding best practices. First, establish a consistent feeding schedule; Hermit Crab (Marine) fish thrive on routine and predictable mealtimes support healthy digestion. Second, rotate between two or three high-quality food brands quarterly to provide nutritional variety and reduce the risk of developing sensitivities to specific proteins. Third, supplement with species-appropriate fresh foods where safe: small amounts of cooked lean meat, safe vegetables, and occasional fruits provide additional micronutrients. Fourth, invest in appropriately sized feeding stations or slow-feeder bowls to improve eating posture and reduce gulping. Finally, track your Hermit Crab (Marine)'s dietary intake and any reactions in a simple log to share with your aquatic veterinarian during wellness visits.

What to feed this Hermit Crab

Marine hermit crabs scavenge in a reef. Land hermits from a souvenir shop are a different animal with a different diet. They pick at algae, leftover frozen mysis, and a sinking pellet, and they will take a bit of mussel. A reef of 10 gallons or more can hold a small hermit if there is food besides a spotless rock. They need a spare shell larger than the one they wear. A crab stuck in a too-small shell stops feeding well and cannot molt cleanly.

Offer a pinch of meaty food and an algae clip if the rocks are bare. Pull the mussel the same day. A stalled molt kills a hermit: the crab is half out of the old skin, or it dies inside the shell after a salinity swing. Empty shells in the tank are not automatically deaths. Hermits leave shells behind when they move. A smell and a body hanging out of the shell means a death.

They will also eat snails and pick at coral if they are hungry. Feed them a pellet and a bit of mysis so they have a meal of their own. Larger species such as the white-spotted hermit need more food and more room than a tiny blue-leg. Match the ration to the crab you actually have. A hermit that patrols, changes shells, and finishes a pellet is eating. One that never leaves a crevice and gets thin needs the food placed at the entrance after lights out.

Calcium from the saltwater and from a varied diet keeps the claws hard. A soft, pale crab in the open may be freshly molted. Leave it. Do not poke it into eating until the new skin has hardened. Older hermits eat the same algae, mysis, and pellets in smaller amounts.

A marine hermit needs a shell it can change into as it grows, and a crab in a too-tight shell eats less and molts badly. Keep two or three empty shells slightly larger than the one it wears, on the sand where the crab walks. Feed a sinking pellet and a small piece of mysis or mussel at dusk, and an algae clip if the live rock has been scrubbed clean. Small blue-leg hermits eat less than a large white-spotted hermit. Match the pinch to the animal in the tank. A hermit that switches shells and then patrols is feeding. A crab that never emerges, with a smell from the shell, has died. Leave a freshly molted crab alone until the new skin hardens, and put the food at the mouth of its crevice so it can eat without crossing the open sand.
