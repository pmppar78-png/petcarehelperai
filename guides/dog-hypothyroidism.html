Hypothyroidism in Dogs

Understanding hypothyroidism in dogs including symptoms, blood test diagnosis, thyroid medication, and long-term management of this common condition.

Causes and Risk Factors

Multiple factors can contribute to the development of this condition.

  • Genetic predisposition: Certain breeds are at higher risk due to inherited traits
  • Age: Some conditions are more common in puppies/kittens or senior pets
  • Environmental factors: Diet, toxin exposure, stress, and living conditions play a role
  • Underlying health issues: Other conditions may increase susceptibility
  • Immune system status: Compromised immune function can predispose to disease

Symptoms to Watch For

Early detection significantly improves treatment outcomes. Watch for these signs.

  • Changes in appetite, water consumption, or weight
  • Behavioral changes including lethargy, restlessness, or hiding
  • Physical symptoms specific to the affected body system
  • Changes in bathroom habits (frequency, appearance, straining)
  • Visible discomfort, pain response to touch, or changes in posture
  • Respiratory changes, coughing, or labored breathing

Diagnosis

Canine hypothyroidism is a hormone measurement, not an imaging-first disease. The Merck Veterinary Manual and VCA start with a total T4. A clearly normal total T4 makes hypothyroidism unlikely. A low total T4 is not enough, because other illness lowers T4 in a dog whose thyroid is working. Confirmation pairs a free T4, measured by equilibrium dialysis, with a TSH. A low free T4 together with a high TSH supports primary hypothyroidism.

Treatment Options

Primary hypothyroidism in dogs is treated with levothyroxine. The veterinarian chooses the dose and rechecks thyroid levels after the dog has been on a steady plan. This is lifelong replacement, not a surgery and not a short antibiotic course. A dog who is sick from another disease should not be started on thyroid hormone from a single low total T4.

Prevention and Management

  • Regular veterinary checkups (as recommended by the AVMA for all companion animals) for early detection (annually, or semi-annually for seniors)
  • Maintain a healthy weight through proper nutrition and exercise
  • Keep vaccinations and preventive medications current
  • Breed-specific health screenings recommended by your veterinarian
  • Provide a low-stress, enriched environment
  • Follow your vet's recommendations for diet and lifestyle modifications

Is this condition curable?

Canine hypothyroidism is replacement, not recovery of the gland. Levothyroxine, adjusted to a post-pill total T4, holds weight, coat, and energy only while the tablets continue. Signs return if they stop. A single low total T4 does not prove the disease, because sick dogs of any kind can test low. A TSH or free-T4 panel is what separates a true hypothyroid dog from a dog that is sick with something else.

How can I afford treatment?

The costly mistake in hypothyroidism is a year of levothyroxine for a dog whose low T4 came from another illness. Pay for a TSH or free-T4 before you pay for a year's tablets. Once the diagnosis is real, the ongoing cost is the drug plus a post-pill T4 recheck, which is modest next to orthopedic surgery. The veterinarian sets the microgram dose from that recheck.
