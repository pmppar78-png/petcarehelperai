Hypothyroidism in Dogs

Understanding hypothyroidism in dogs including symptoms, blood test diagnosis, thyroid medication, and long-term management of this common condition.

What hypothyroidism is

Hypothyroidism in dogs is a shortage of thyroid hormone, almost always because the thyroid gland itself has failed. The two common paths are lymphocytic thyroiditis, an immune attack on the gland, and idiopathic atrophy, in which the working tissue is replaced by fat. Either way the dog cannot make enough thyroxine. Thyroid hormone sets the metabolic idle speed. When it is low, the dog gains weight, slows down, seeks heat, and grows a dull, sparse coat. Primary hypothyroidism is the disease this page is about. A low thyroid number during some other severe illness is a different situation called sick euthyroid, and treating it with hormone can be the wrong move.

The disease is common, chronic, and usually undramatic. Myxedema coma, with stupor, low body temperature, a slow heart, and facial swelling, is the rare emergency form. Most dogs arrive because of a coat change or a weight problem that diet alone did not fix. Once the diagnosis is real, replacement is lifelong and the outlook is good.

Which dogs get it

Middle-aged dogs of medium and large breeds are typical. Golden Retrievers, Labrador Retrievers, Doberman Pinschers, Dachshunds, Boxers, Cocker Spaniels, Irish Setters, and Beagles are named often. Toy breeds develop it less often. Sight hounds such as Greyhounds and Salukis normally run a lower total T4 than other breeds, so a number that looks low on a generic reference range can be normal for them. Spayed and neutered dogs are the patients clinics see, partly because that is who lives in the waiting room. A young dog with classic signs still deserves testing. A dog who is sick with pancreatitis, heart failure, or Cushing's disease can have a low total T4 while the thyroid gland is working, which is why one number on a crisis blood panel should not start a prescription.

Signs owners notice

Weight creeps up without a huge appetite. The dog seems lazy, sleeps more, and picks the warmest bed. The coat thins along the trunk, the tail becomes a "rat tail," and the skin may darken or grow a recurrent infection. Some dogs develop a tragic facial expression from thickening of the skin. The heart rate may be slow, and cholesterol on a screening panel is often high. A mild anemia can show up on the complete blood count. Nerve signs, such as weakness or a droopy face, happen in a minority. The dramatic emergency is myxedema coma: a dog who is dull to the point of stupor, cold, and puffy. That dog needs a hospital. A tablet started at home from an old bottle delays care.

  • Weight gain and low energy with a normal or even modest appetite.
  • Symmetric hair loss, a sparse tail, and a dry or dark coat.
  • Recurrent skin infections that partially respond and then return.
  • Seeking heat, and reluctance to walk in cool weather.
  • A very dull, cold, swollen dog, which is an emergency pattern.

How a veterinarian diagnoses it

Canine hypothyroidism is diagnosed with thyroid hormone testing. Testing starts with a total T4. A clearly normal total T4 makes hypothyroidism unlikely. A low total T4 cannot stand alone, because other illness lowers T4 in a dog whose thyroid is working. Confirmation pairs a free T4, measured by equilibrium dialysis, with a TSH. A low free T4 together with a high TSH supports primary hypothyroidism.

Free T4 by equilibrium dialysis is the free-hormone method least fooled by other illness and by some drugs. TSH rises when the pituitary is shouting at a failed thyroid. Thyroglobulin autoantibodies help explain odd results in a dog with thyroiditis and can interfere with some assays. A dog on prednisone, a sulfonamide antibiotic, phenobarbital, or a recent illness can show thyroid numbers that look low for reasons outside the gland. The clinician reads the panel against that history. Imaging of the thyroid is rarely needed. A therapeutic trial of levothyroxine, given to "see if the coat improves," muddies the diagnosis and can be harmful if the dog was sick from something else.

Treatment categories

Primary hypothyroidism in dogs is treated with levothyroxine. The veterinarian chooses the dose and rechecks thyroid levels after the dog has been on a steady plan. This is lifelong replacement. A dog who is sick from another disease should not be started on thyroid hormone from a single low total T4.

Levothyroxine is a tablet given on a consistent schedule, often twice daily at the start, and the amount is adjusted to a post-pill total T4. Many clinics draw that recheck sample four to six hours after the morning tablet, several weeks after a dose change, so the number reflects a steady plan. The target is a clinical dog who has energy and a returning coat, with a lab value in the range the veterinarian set. Food, calcium, and some other drugs can change absorption, so the clinic may ask you to give the tablet apart from the meal. There is no surgery that restores the gland. Supplements marketed as thyroid support cannot stand in for levothyroxine once the diagnosis is confirmed, and they should not be stacked on top of the prescription without telling the clinician.

Home care

Give the tablet at the same times every day. If a dose is missed, follow the instruction you were given. Do not invent a double dose. Keep the recheck appointment even if the dog seems brighter, because "brighter" can still be an over-replaced dog with a heart that is being pushed. Watch for restlessness, panting, a racing appetite, or diarrhea after a dose increase. Those can mean the amount is too high. Watch for a return of weight gain and a dull coat months later, which can mean a missed tablet or a need to recheck. Tell any new clinic, including a dentist or an emergency service, that the dog takes levothyroxine. Recheck sooner if the dog starts a long course of another drug that might interact.

When it is an emergency

When to See the Vet Immediately

Ordinary hypothyroidism is a scheduled diagnosis. Myxedema coma belongs in an emergency hospital the same hour: stupor, collapse, a very low temperature, a slow heart, or facial puffiness in a dog with a history of untreated thyroid disease. So should a dog who becomes extremely restless, vomits repeatedly, or seems overheated after someone gave a large extra dose of thyroid hormone. Chest pain is hard to read in dogs. Sudden weakness with a pale gum color or a racing heart after a dose mix-up deserves the same urgent visit. A gradual weight gain can wait for a regular appointment and a proper panel.

What to ask about the bill

Canine hypothyroidism is treated with lifelong levothyroxine. Levothyroxine, adjusted to a post-pill total T4, holds weight, coat, and energy only while the tablets continue. Signs return if they stop. A single low total T4 does not prove the disease, because sick dogs of any kind can test low. A TSH or free-T4 panel is what separates a true hypothyroid dog from a dog that is sick with something else.

The costly mistake in hypothyroidism is a year of levothyroxine for a dog whose low T4 came from another illness. Pay for a TSH or free-T4 before you pay for a year's tablets. Once the diagnosis is real, the ongoing cost is the drug plus a post-pill T4 recheck, which is modest next to orthopedic surgery. The veterinarian sets the microgram dose from that recheck.

Key Questions

Why was the thyroid test low at the emergency clinic?

Severe illness lowers total T4 even when the thyroid gland is healthy. That sick-euthyroid pattern is a reason to wait, treat the real crisis, and repeat a free T4 and TSH when the dog is stable. Starting hormone from the emergency printout can mask the underlying disease and push the heart of a fragile patient. Ask the regular veterinarian to interpret the number in context before any tablet is dispensed.

How soon should the coat improve?

Energy often improves within a couple of weeks. Hair regrowth is slower and can take a few months, because the coat cycle has to turn over. A recheck total T4, drawn a few hours after the tablet, tells you whether the amount is in range while you wait for the hair. If the number is right and the coat is still unchanged months later, the clinician looks for a second skin disease such as allergy or infection.

Do Greyhounds use the same T4 range?

Often they do not. Many sighthounds have a lower total T4 than the reference range printed for other breeds, and a low number in a bright, well-muscled Greyhound may be normal. Free T4 by equilibrium dialysis and TSH, plus the actual signs, keep a retired racer off a drug he does not need. The same caution applies to other sight hounds. Bring breed information to the visit so the lab result is read against the right range.
