Dog Urinary Tract

Evidence-based guide to UTIs in dogs covering symptoms, causes, diagnosis, treatment options, prevention, and when UTIs indicate a more serious condition.

What a urinary tract infection is

A urinary tract infection in dogs is bacteria in the urinary tract causing inflammation, most often in the bladder. That is bacterial cystitis. Escherichia coli is the organism clinics see most, followed by Staphylococcus, Proteus, Enterococcus, and Klebsiella. The bacteria usually climb from the skin and the lower genital tract. A kidney infection, pyelonephritis, is the same family of germs higher up, and the dog is sicker. Subclinical bacteriuria is bacteria in the urine of a dog who has no urinary signs. ISCAID guidelines do not tell clinicians to treat every positive culture in a dog who feels fine, because antibiotics have a cost and some of those dogs are better left alone.

A first, sporadic infection in an otherwise healthy dog is a short problem if the drug matches a culture. A relapse means the same infection never really left, often because a stone, a prostate, or a pocket of urine is protecting the bacteria. A reinfection is a new organism after a cure. Those two words change the workup. Clearing germs while a stone stays in the bladder is why the same signs return a month later.

Which dogs get

Female dogs, especially those with a recessed or hooded vulva, get bladder infections more often because the opening sits where skin bacteria live. Males are less often infected and more often obstructed, and an intact male with urinary signs needs the prostate examined. Dogs with Cushing's disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, incontinence, or long courses of steroids grow bacteria in dilute or immunologically quiet urine. Dogs who have had a urinary catheter, a recent cystotomy, or a polyp in the bladder are repeat customers. Any breed can be affected. A young dog with a first infection and a normal exam is a different patient from an eight-year-old with a third infection this year.

ISCAID separates sporadic bacterial cystitis from recurrent infection. Recurrent usually means three or more episodes in a year, or two in six months. The second and third episodes are when imaging and a search for a driver earn their keep. Treating each one as if it were the first, with a longer guess of an antibiotic, misses the stone.

Signs owners notice

The dog asks to go out constantly, squats and passes only a little, strains, or has accidents in the house. The urine may be cloudy, bloody, or strong-smelling. Some dogs lick the vulva or the prepuce. A kidney infection adds fever, a poor appetite, back pain, vomiting, and a dog who looks sick, far past merely inconvenienced. The emergency pattern, more often in males, is straining with little or no urine, a hard painful belly, and restlessness or vomiting. That can be a urethral obstruction, and it can be fatal. It is a different disease from a simple cystitis even though the posture looks similar from across the yard.

  • Frequent squatting, small puddles, and urgency.
  • Blood in the urine, or urine that looks cloudy.
  • House accidents in a dog who was previously reliable.
  • Licking at the urinary opening.
  • Fever, vomiting, or back pain, which point higher than the bladder.
  • Straining with no urine produced, which is an obstruction until proven otherwise.

How a veterinarian

A dog urinary tract infection is diagnosed with a urinalysis and a urine culture, ideally from a sample taken before antibiotics. White cells and bacteria on the dipstick and the sediment are clues. The culture names the organism and the drugs that can kill it. Culture is the test that should drive the prescription. Radiographs or ultrasound are added when infections keep returning, to look for stones, masses, or an anatomic problem.

Cystocentesis, a needle sample from the bladder, is the cleanest specimen for culture because a free catch picks up skin bacteria and overcalls infection. A dipstick and a spun sediment show blood, white cells, and crystals. Crystals and stones are different findings, and struvite crystals can appear in a cooled sample that sat in the car. The culture and sensitivity report is what names the drug. Imaging enters at the second or third infection, or at the first if the dog is a male, is systemically ill, or has a breed history of stones. Radiographs find many struvite and calcium oxalate stones. Ultrasound finds radiolucent stones, bladder-wall thickening, polyps, and prostate changes. A urine protein and a culture collected from the kidney's point of view, plus bloodwork, join the plan when pyelonephritis is suspected.

Treatment categories

The antibiotic for a dog UTI comes from the culture when one was obtained, and the veterinarian sets the drug and the length. Pain control is separate and is also dosed by the veterinarian. The antibiotic leaves stones behind: some dissolve on a stone-specific diet, and some need a cystotomy. A dog who is straining and producing no urine needs emergency care for a blockage.

Sporadic cystitis in an otherwise healthy dog is often a short antibiotic course chosen from the culture, with pain relief such as a veterinary anti-inflammatory if the kidneys and the gut allow it. Recurrent infection needs the driver fixed. Infection-associated struvite stones in dogs may dissolve on a dissolution diet plus an antibiotic that matches the culture, with radiographs to prove they shrank. Calcium oxalate stones stay put on a diet and usually need surgical removal or another removal method the surgeon offers. A recessed vulva sometimes needs a vulvoplasty after infections keep returning. Prostatic disease in a male needs its own plan, often including culture and, when appropriate, neutering once the infection is understood. Treating subclinical bacteriuria in a dog with no signs is a conversation. A reflex refill skips that conversation.

Home care

Give the antibiotic for the full number of days, even after the urine looks normal. Offer water and give the dog chances to empty the bladder. Finish the recheck culture if the clinic asked for one, because a clear puddle can still grow bacteria. If this is a repeat infection, collect the next sample before any leftover tablet is given. Wipe a hooded vulva if the clinician showed you how, and keep the fur short around the opening. A urinary diet only helps if it was prescribed for a stone type you actually have. Do not start one from a shelf tag. Watch for a return of straining, especially a male who postures and produces nothing. That dog goes to a hospital. Keep the follow-up imaging appointment if stones were seen, because a diet that "should be working" still needs a film to prove the stone is smaller.

When to See

Straining with little or no urine, a hard abdomen, vomiting, collapse, or extreme pain is an emergency. Urethral obstruction is more common in males and can cause life-threatening potassium changes within hours. A dog with fever, back pain, and vomiting may have a kidney infection and also needs same-day care, fluids, and a culture. Blood in the urine of a bright dog who is still passing a stream can be booked promptly the same day with a regular veterinarian. It should not be watched for a week, and it is still a different urgency from the dog who cannot pass urine at all.

What to ask

A first, sporadic bladder infection usually clears when the antibiotic matches a culture. ISCAID urinary guidelines separate that simple cystitis from a relapse. Relapse means a stone, prostatic disease, incontinence, Cushing's disease, or a recessed vulva is still driving the bacteria. Clearing the germs without finding that driver is why the same infection is back a month later.

Spend the urinary budget on a culture and, if this is the second infection, on imaging. A longer course of an untested antibiotic is the purchase to skip. One culture costs less than months of the wrong tablet followed by a cystotomy for stones. Ask for the culture fee as its own line before you accept a refill. The surgical estimate matters only if radiographs show stones.

Key Questions

Why culture the urine before antibiotics?

Because the drug that worked for a neighbor's dog may be useless against this organism, and a few days of the wrong tablet can make the culture unreadable. A cystocentesis sample taken first lets the lab name the bacterium and list the drugs that can kill it. ISCAID guidance puts that result at the center of the prescription, especially when the infection is recurrent or the dog is ill. A dipstick alone cannot choose the antibiotic.

Do crystals mean my dog has stones?

No. Crystals can form in a sample that cooled on the way to the clinic, and they can be present without a stone. Stones are found on radiographs or ultrasound. Struvite stones in dogs are often tied to infection and sometimes dissolve with diet and the right antibiotic. Calcium oxalate stones do not dissolve that way and usually need removal. The crystal name on a urinalysis is a hint. The image is the diagnosis.

When is a bladder infection actually a blockage?

When the dog strains and little or no urine comes out, especially a male dog who is restless, vomiting, or painful in the belly. That is a urethral obstruction until the clinic proves otherwise, and it is an emergency. A dog who passes many small bloody puddles and still empties is more consistent with cystitis or stones that have not blocked the urethra. If you are unsure whether any urine is coming out, treat it as a blockage and go in.
