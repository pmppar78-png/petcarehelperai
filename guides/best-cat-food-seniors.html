Senior Cats

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Top senior cat food brands reviewed. Covers kidney support, weight management, hydration, and transitioning older cats to appropriate nutrition.

What "senior" means on a cat food bag

Cat-food companies often print "senior" on recipes aimed at cats around seven years old, and a second wave of labels shows up for cats around eleven. AAFCO does not define senior as its own life stage the way it defines growth and adult maintenance. A healthy older cat can legally eat a food that says complete and balanced for adult maintenance. The senior badge is a recipe style: sometimes fewer calories, sometimes added fiber, sometimes a joint additive, sometimes a phosphorus number the company wants to highlight. Read the calorie line and the phosphorus number, and match them to the cat you have, because a seven-year-old indoor cat who still hunts toys is a different patient from a fifteen-year-old who has lost muscle over the spine.

Therapeutic diets for kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, or urinary stones are a separate aisle. Those foods are tools a veterinarian prescribes after bloodwork and a urine test. Buying a "kidney support" grocery formula for a cat who has never had a chemistry panel can underfeed protein or change minerals the cat did not need changed. Schedule the lab work first. Then choose the bag with the results in hand.

Protein, muscle, and calories

Older cats lose muscle when the diet is short on protein and when they eat too little because the food smells dull or the mouth hurts. . A maintenance food with a solid animal-protein base supports the thighs and the back muscles you should still be able to feel. Some senior recipes drop protein sharply to look "gentle." That move can speed muscle loss in a cat who does not have a diagnosed reason for a protein cap. If your veterinarian has restricted protein for a specific disease, follow that plan. For a healthy older cat, keep protein adequate and judge the bowl by muscle and weight, every month, on a scale that reads ounces.

Calories go both directions. A less active lap cat gains fat on the same cup that kept a three-year-old sleek, and the fat hides muscle loss until the cat looks round and weak at the same time. Another senior cat, especially one with early hyperthyroidism or a disease that raises metabolism, melts away on a "weight control" senior formula. Feel the ribs. You want a light fat cover, a waist, and thighs that still have substance. Weigh monthly and write it down. A loss of several ounces without a diet change is a clinic visit, even when the cat still asks for breakfast.

Water is the feeding choice that helps almost every older cat. Cats evolved to get moisture from prey and many of them drink poorly from a bowl. Wet food, added water in a pate, and a fountain placed away from the litter box all raise intake. That extra moisture supports kidneys and also dilutes urine for cats with urinary crystal histories, again under a vet's plan. Dry food can stay in the mix for convenience if the cat eats it and the calories are counted. If the cat is a kibble-only eater, a fountain and several water stations matter more, and you can still warm a spoon of wet food on top as a bridge.

Mouth pain, smell, and texture

Dental disease makes a hard kibble feel like a punishment. A cat who circles the bowl, drops pieces, or chews on one side may need a dental procedure more than a new flavor. Until that visit, a pate or a softly mashed chunk-in-gravy lets the cat keep eating. Very large chunky stews frustrate a cat with missing teeth. Warm the wet food slightly so the aroma carries. Cold food straight from the fridge is a common reason an older nose turns the meal down. Wipe the chin after wet meals. Residue there mats the fur and can inflame the skin of a cat who no longer grooms the folder of the mouth.

Appetite changes deserve a log. Note which texture disappeared in ten minutes and which texture is still there at dusk. Bring that log, plus the labels, to the clinic. Sudden refusal is a medical sign in cats, who hide illness and then develop hepatic lipidosis if they stop eating for more than a day or so. Do not wait out a total hunger strike over a weekend because you just bought a case of a new senior flavor.

Changing foods without a standoff

Older cats imprint on texture. Plan on ten to fourteen days for a switch, longer if the cat has walked away from new foods before. Offer the new food in a separate dish beside the old one at the usual meal time, and let the cat choose while you still control calories. A hunger strategy that works on a young dog can be dangerous in a senior cat. If you need a therapeutic diet, ask the clinic for a transition trick they trust, such as a temporary topping, and track how many grams actually go in. Powdered toppers and fish oil add calories and can unbalance a therapeutic recipe if you pour freely. Measure them.

Raise the bowls slightly if the cat has arthritis and crouches awkwardly to eat. A low box under the dishes is enough for many living rooms. Keep the station quiet and away from a dog's traffic path. A cat who is bumped at breakfast will graze at 2 a.m. and you will think the food is wrong. Multi-cat homes need one station per cat plus one extra, or the senior loses the meal to a younger housemate and you misread the weight loss.

Labels, additives, and typical cost

Joint additives such as glucosamine, and fatty acids from fish, show up on a lot of senior cans. The amounts in a grocery formula are often modest compared with a veterinary product, and they are optional extras on top of a complete maintenance food. Green-lipped mussel and similar ingredients are the same kind of extra. They can be reasonable if the cat likes the food and your veterinarian agrees they fit the medical picture. They are a weak reason to buy a calorie level the cat does not need. Compare kilocalories per can alongside the price per can. A cheap can with twice the calories of the neighbor can is the expensive one once the cat gains weight.

Wet senior foods commonly sell in a typical range of about $1.50 to $4 a can, and dry foods often land around $2 to $6 a pound. Store wet food in the fridge after opening and discard it after two days if the cat has only eaten half. Dry food should be finished within about six weeks of opening so the fats stay palatable to a nose that is already picky.

Senior cat food questions

Does every cat need a senior formula at age seven?

Seven is a marketing milestone on many bags, and AAFCO still treats a healthy cat that age as an adult maintenance eater. Match calories to the body you can feel, and keep protein adequate unless a veterinarian has capped it for disease. A senior label helps when its calorie and texture fit. It is optional when a regular adult food already keeps muscle and weight steady.

Should I buy a kidney diet before bloodwork?

Phosphorus and protein changes are tools for cats with documented kidney disease, chosen after lab work and an exam. A healthy older cat can lose interest or lose muscle on a food that restricts those nutrients without a reason. Book the panel, then pick the recipe with the numbers in front of you. Moisture from wet food and several water stations helps older cats in the meantime.

What if my older cat refuses the new food?

Offer it beside the familiar food for up to two weeks, slightly warmed, in a texture the mouth can manage. Track grams eaten and body weight. Do not fast a senior cat to force a swap. A cat who quits eating entirely needs a veterinarian promptly, because appetite loss in cats becomes a liver problem faster than most owners expect.
