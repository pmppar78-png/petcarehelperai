Small Animal Health & Symptom Guide

Small animals such as rabbits, guinea pigs, hamsters, ferrets, chinchillas, and hedgehogs have unique health needs that differ dramatically from dogs and cats. Because these prey animals instinctively hide illness, recognizing subtle changes in behavior, appetite, and droppings is essential for catching problems early. This comprehensive guide covers the most common health issues across small pet species, emergency warning signs every owner must know, and best practices for preventive care that keeps your pocket pet thriving.

Why Small Animal Health Requires Special Attention

Small animals are prey species, which means they evolved to mask signs of weakness. In the wild, a sick animal that looks vulnerable becomes a target for predators. This survival instinct carries over into domestic life, making it critically important for owners to recognize the earliest, most subtle indicators of illness. By the time a rabbit, guinea pig, or hamster visibly appears sick, the condition is often advanced and potentially life-threatening.

Another key challenge is that many general-practice veterinarians have limited experience with exotic small mammals. Finding an exotic veterinarian who specializes in small animal medicine before an emergency arises is one of the most important steps a new owner can take. The Association of Exotic Mammal Veterinarians (AEMV) maintains a directory of qualified practitioners.

Rabbit Health Problems

Rabbits are prone to several serious conditions that require prompt veterinary attention.

  • GI stasis: The most common and dangerous rabbit emergency. The gut slows or stops, causing gas buildup, pain, and potential organ failure. Caused by insufficient hay intake, stress, dental disease, or dehydration. A rabbit not eating or producing droppings for more than 12 hours needs immediate exotic vet care.
  • Dental disease: Rabbit teeth grow continuously at a rate of approximately 2-3 mm per week. Without adequate hay to wear them down, molars develop sharp spurs that lacerate the tongue and cheeks, causing severe pain and inability to eat.
  • E. cuniculi: A parasitic infection causing head tilt, hind-leg weakness, kidney damage, and cataracts. Treated with fenbendazole under veterinary supervision.
  • Pasteurella (snuffles): Bacterial respiratory infection causing nasal discharge, sneezing, and abscesses. Requires antibiotic treatment from a vet.
  • Fly strike (myiasis): Flies lay eggs on soiled fur, and hatching maggots eat into the rabbit's flesh. This is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate veterinary intervention.
  • Bladder sludge and stones: Rabbits excrete excess calcium through urine. Thick, sludgy urine or gritty deposits can cause pain and urinary blockages.

Guinea Pig Health Problems

  • Respiratory infections: The most dangerous common illness in guinea pigs. Bacteria such as Bordetella and Streptococcus cause pneumonia rapidly. Symptoms include sneezing, nasal discharge, labored breathing, and lethargy. Requires same-day veterinary treatment with appropriate antibiotics.
  • Scurvy (vitamin C deficiency): Guinea pigs, like humans, cannot synthesize their own vitamin C. Deficiency causes swollen joints, rough coat, lethargy, dental problems, and immune suppression. Supplement with 25-50 mg of vitamin C daily through fresh vegetables or liquid supplements.
  • Bumblefoot (pododermatitis): Painful bacterial infection of the foot pads caused by wire cage floors, rough bedding, obesity, or unsanitary conditions. Requires topical and sometimes systemic antibiotics.
  • Ovarian cysts: Extremely common in unspayed female guinea pigs over two years old. Causes bilateral hair loss on the flanks, crusty nipples, and behavioral changes. Spaying is the definitive treatment.
  • Dental disease: Similar to rabbits, guinea pig teeth grow continuously and require proper wear from hay and fibrous foods.

Hamster Health Problems

  • Wet tail (proliferative ileitis): Serious bacterial infection causing severe diarrhea, lethargy, dehydration, and a wet, soiled tail area. Most common in young Syrian hamsters. Requires immediate veterinary treatment; can be fatal within 48 hours.
  • Respiratory infections: Drafts, cedar or pine bedding, and dirty cages predispose hamsters to pneumonia. Symptoms include sneezing, wheezing, nasal discharge, and labored breathing.
  • Cheek pouch impaction: Food or bedding material becomes stuck in the cheek pouches, causing swelling and potential infection. A vet may need to flush the pouches under anesthesia.
  • Tumors: Common in older hamsters, especially Syrian hamsters over 18 months. Both benign and malignant tumors occur frequently along the mammary chain and on internal organs.
  • Diabetes: Particularly common in Campbell's dwarf hamsters. Signs include excessive drinking, urination, weight loss, and lethargy. Managed with diet modification.

Ferret Health Problems

  • Adrenal disease: Extremely common in ferrets over three years old. Causes progressive hair loss (usually starting at the tail), enlarged vulva in females, prostate enlargement in males, and aggression. Treated with surgery or hormone implants.
  • Insulinoma: Pancreatic tumors that cause dangerously low blood sugar. Signs include staring into space, pawing at the mouth, wobbliness, and seizures. Managed with diet, medication, or surgery.
  • Lymphoma: The most common cancer in ferrets. Can affect the lymph nodes, spleen, liver, and intestines. Symptoms vary by location and may include weight loss, lethargy, and enlarged lymph nodes.
  • Gastrointestinal foreign bodies: Ferrets are notorious for swallowing rubber, foam, and fabric objects that cause intestinal blockages requiring emergency surgery.
  • Canine distemper: Nearly 100% fatal in ferrets. Vaccination is essential. Symptoms include nasal discharge, eye discharge, rash on chin and groin, and hardened foot pads.

Chinchilla and Hedgehog Health Problems

  • Chinchilla heat stroke: Chinchillas cannot tolerate temperatures above 75°F and cannot sweat. Heat stroke causes rapid breathing, drooling, and collapse. Keep enclosures in temperature-controlled rooms below 72°F.
  • Chinchilla dental disease: Overgrown teeth and molar spurs cause drooling, weight loss, and selective eating. Requires veterinary dental work under anesthesia.
  • Chinchilla fur slip: A defense mechanism where patches of fur release when the chinchilla is stressed or roughly handled. The fur grows back but indicates handling issues.
  • Hedgehog mites: Extremely common, causing flaky skin, quill loss, and itchiness. Treated with Revolution (selamectin) prescribed by an exotic vet. Never use over-the-counter mite products designed for other species.
  • Wobbly Hedgehog Syndrome (WHS): A progressive, degenerative neurological disease causing increasing wobbliness, muscle wasting, and eventually paralysis. There is no cure, but quality of life can be managed in the early stages.
  • Hedgehog hibernation attempts: African pygmy hedgehogs are not adapted to hibernate safely. Temperatures below 68°F trigger a dangerous torpor. Keep the enclosure between 72-80°F at all times.

Emergency Warning Signs in Small Animals

Any of the following symptoms warrant same-day or emergency veterinary care. Do not wait to see if the animal improves on its own.

Seek Immediate Veterinary Care If You Observe:

  • Not eating for 12+ hours (especially rabbits and guinea pigs — can become fatal)
  • No droppings for 12+ hours (indicates gut shutdown in herbivores)
  • Labored or open-mouth breathing (sign of severe respiratory distress)
  • Head tilt (indicates inner ear infection or neurological issue)
  • Seizures, tremors, or collapse
  • Blood in urine or stool
  • Bloated or hard abdomen
  • Sudden hind-leg paralysis (spinal injury or severe calcium deficiency)
  • Fly strike (visible maggots on rabbit — extreme emergency)
  • Severe diarrhea (especially in hamsters — may be wet tail)
  • Eye bulging or proptosis (hamsters are prone to eye issues from rough handling)
  • Ferret pawing at mouth or staring blankly (possible insulinoma low blood sugar episode)

Preventive Health Care for Small Animals

The best approach to small animal health is prevention. Many of the most common and dangerous conditions are directly linked to improper husbandry.

Diet as the Foundation of Health

  • Rabbits and guinea pigs: Unlimited timothy hay must comprise 80% or more of the diet. Hay provides essential fiber for gut motility and dental wear. Without it, both GI stasis and dental disease become almost inevitable.
  • Guinea pigs: Daily vitamin C supplementation is non-negotiable. Bell peppers, kale, and parsley are excellent natural sources.
  • Hamsters: A balanced commercial hamster food supplemented with small amounts of fresh vegetables, occasional protein (mealworms, cooked egg), and limited seeds.
  • Ferrets: Obligate carnivores requiring high-protein, high-fat, grain-free food. Poor diet directly worsens insulinoma risk and body odor.
  • Chinchillas: Unlimited timothy hay, limited pellets, and extremely limited treats. No fresh fruits or vegetables, which cause dangerous bloat.

Environmental Health Factors

  • Bedding: Use paper-based bedding (Carefresh, Kaytee Clean & Cozy) or kiln-dried aspen. Never use cedar or pine shavings — the phenol oils cause liver damage and respiratory irritation. Fleece liners are excellent for guinea pigs and rabbits.
  • Temperature: Chinchillas require temperatures below 75°F, hedgehogs need 72-80°F, and all small animals should be protected from drafts, direct sunlight, and extreme temperature swings.
  • Ventilation: Ammonia buildup from urine in poorly ventilated enclosures is a primary cause of respiratory infections. Clean litter areas frequently and ensure adequate airflow.
  • Cage hygiene: Spot-clean daily, full bedding change weekly, and deep-clean the enclosure monthly with pet-safe disinfectant.

Regular Health Checks at Home

Perform a brief health check on your small pet weekly.

  • Weigh your pet on a kitchen scale — sudden weight loss is often the first sign of illness
  • Check eyes for discharge, cloudiness, or swelling
  • Examine ears for wax buildup, redness, or head tilting
  • Inspect teeth for overgrowth, misalignment, or drooling
  • Feel the body for lumps, bumps, or areas of pain
  • Check feet for sores, swelling, or overgrown nails
  • Monitor droppings daily for changes in size, shape, consistency, and frequency
  • Observe breathing for wheezing, clicking, or labored respiration

Finding and Working with an Exotic Veterinarian

Not all veterinarians have training in small exotic mammal medicine. Before an emergency occurs, locate a qualified exotic vet in your area. Ask potential veterinarians about their experience with your specific species, whether they can perform dental procedures and surgery on small animals, and what their emergency or after-hours protocols include. Building a relationship with an exotic vet before a crisis will ensure your pet receives the best possible care when it matters most.

Medication Safety for Small Animals

Small animals metabolize medications differently than dogs and cats, and many common pet medications are lethal to certain species.

  • Never give antibiotics without veterinary guidance: Amoxicillin and penicillin-based antibiotics are fatal to rabbits, guinea pigs, hamsters, and chinchillas because they destroy essential gut bacteria, causing fatal enterotoxemia.
  • Avoid over-the-counter flea and mite treatments: Products designed for dogs and cats can be toxic to small animals. Only use treatments specifically prescribed by an exotic vet.
  • Pain management: Small animals experience pain but cannot tell you. Meloxicam (Metacam) is commonly prescribed for rabbits and guinea pigs, but dosing must be precise due to their small body weight.
  • Critical care feeding: For animals that stop eating, Oxbow Critical Care is a syringe-fed recovery food that can sustain gut motility while the underlying cause is treated. Keep a bag on hand for emergencies.
