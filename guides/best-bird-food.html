Best Bird Food by Species

Top bird food brands for parrots, cockatiels, finches, and canaries. Covers pellets, seed mixes, fresh food, and nutritional requirements by species.

Pellets, seed, and the job of each

Hookbills, from budgerigars through cockatoos, hull their food with the beak and can live for decades on what you put in the cup. A formulated pellet is the staple most avian veterinarians want those birds to eat, because the vitamin and mineral levels are mixed into every piece. A seed mix lets the bird pick sunflower, millet, and safflower and leave the rest. That selection is tasty and chronically short on vitamin A and calcium while running high in fat. The result, over years, shows up as dull feathers, a heavy body, and respiratory or reproductive trouble that an avian vet then has to untangle. Pellets look boring next to a colorful seed bag. The boring cup is the one that matches a long life.

Seed still has a place. A measured sprinkle works as a training reward and as a bridge while a bird learns to eat pellets. Finches and canaries are a different case from macaws. They are passerines with faster metabolisms, and a fortified finch or canary mix, plus greens and a mineral source, is the ordinary base many of them do well on. Pellet crumbs sized for finches exist and some birds take them. Stuffing a macaw pellet into a finch cup wastes the food and can frustrate a bird who cannot crack it. Read the bag for the group it was made for: budgie, cockatiel, conure and small parrot, large parrot, finch, or canary. A single "pet bird" sack with no species on the label is a guess.

How the common pet species actually eat

Budgerigars eat a surprising amount for their size and will live on spray millet if you allow it. Offer a budgie-sized pellet as the morning cup, and keep millet as a short training spray a few times a week. Cockatiels share the budgie's love of seed and add a tendency toward fatty lumps and a quiet weight gain you miss under the crest. Weigh a cockatiel weekly on a gram scale. A climb of even a few grams over a month is a reason to cut the seed portion and book an avian visit, especially if the bird is less keen to fly. Conures and other small parrots can eat a small-parrot pellet, chopped vegetables, and a few nuts as training bites. Too many nuts turn a conure into a picky, oily eater.

African greys and large macaws need large-parrot pellets and a calm routine around calcium-rich foods such as cooked squash and dark greens, because greys in particular are often discussed for low blood calcium when the diet is mostly seed and peanuts. Skip the peanut pile. A few unsalted nuts a day for a large macaw is a treat, and the pellet cup is still the meal. Canaries and finches do better with a mix labeled for them, a separate egg-food or soft-food offering during molt and breeding, and fine chopped greens. Lories and lorikeets are nectar and fresh-fruit birds. Seed and dry pellets made for amazons will sicken the feeding plan you think you copied from a parrot article. If you share a house with a lory, buy a nectar diet made for that group and clean the wet cups daily so the sweet food does not ferment.

Fresh foods that earn a place on the chop

A daily handful of vegetables adds moisture, fiber, and carotenoids that even a good pellet program benefits from. Useful, widely fed choices include kale, dandelion greens, carrot, bell pepper, broccoli, snap peas, and cooked sweet potato or winter squash. A small piece of apple, blueberry, or melon works as variety. Rinse produce, chop it to a size the species can hold, and pull the dish after a couple of hours, sooner in hot weather. Birds dunk food and drop husks into water, so the fresh cup and the water cup both need a wash the same day. Cooked plain grains or a bit of cooked egg can add interest during a molt. Keep the portions small enough that the bird still finishes the pellet cup.

Skip avocado entirely, including guacamole traces on a plate. Skip chocolate, coffee, tea, alcohol, and anything sweetened with xylitol. Onions, garlic, and salty chips inflame the digestive tract or load a tiny body with sodium. Fruit pits and apple seeds carry compounds birds do not need a curiosity bite of. Do not share a bite of dog or cat food, even as a joke. Those formulas are built for mammals, with vitamin levels and seasonings aimed at a different liver. Houseplants and bouquets are a separate hazard. If a plant is new, look it up on an avian toxic-plant list before it sits where a beak can reach.

Moving a seed eater onto pellets

A cold-turkey swap can starve a stubborn bird who would rather wait you out than eat a tan pellet. Weigh the bird the morning you start, at the same time, on a gram scale, and write it down. Offer pellets first thing, when the bird is hungry, in the usual cup. Later in the day, add a measured, smaller portion of the familiar seed, and shrink that portion over two to four weeks if weight holds. Some birds need longer. Mixing a few pellets into seed often fails because the bird flicks the pellets out and eats every seed. A second cup that contains only pellets, placed where the bird already looks for breakfast, teaches faster.

If weight drops more than a small fraction of body weight, or the bird sits fluffed and quiet, pause the conversion and call an avian veterinarian. A budgie can decline in a day. Conversion tricks that sometimes help include crumbling a moist pellet and rolling it in a dust of the old seed, offering a warm mash of crushed pellet for cockatiels, or using a flavored pellet only as a bridge and then returning to a plain complete pellet. Watch the droppings. A bird who is actually swallowing pellets produces different feces from a bird who only grinds and spits them. The spit-out crusts under the cage tell the truth when the cup looks empty.

Minerals, grit, and laying hens

A cuttlebone or a mineral block gives a bird something legal to scrape the beak on, and it offers calcium to birds who choose to nibble. Plenty of pellet-fed parrots ignore it, and that is fine when the pellet is complete and the bird is outside a laying cycle. A hen on an all-seed diet who starts laying is in a different situation. Egg production pulls calcium from the body, and a seed-only hen can become egg-bound or lay soft shells. That is an avian emergency pattern, and the fix is a diet change plus a veterinary exam. Human calcium tablets and dog joint powders are the wrong tools. Bring the current food labels to the clinic and let the avian vet decide whether a short medical supplement belongs in the plan.

Grit is widely misunderstood. Parrots hull seed with the beak, so a bowl of sand is unnecessary and a binge can impact the crop or gizzard. Canaries and finches swallow more seed whole, and some keepers offer a pinch of mineral grit. Keep that offering tiny, fresh, and separate from a toy full of loose sand a bored bird will eat. If you are unsure which group your bird falls into, skip loose grit until an avian vet confirms it. Soluble mineral and insoluble quartz are different products. A cute package at the pet store does not spell out the difference on the front.

Storage, dishes, and what a month of food costs

Pellets lose potency after the bag is open and after the best-by date. Buy a size you will finish in about six to eight weeks, fold the bag closed, and park it in a cool cupboard away from the stove. Refrigerating pellets can add moisture and clumping in a humid kitchen, so a cool dry shelf is usually better than a damp fridge. Seed moths arrive inside otherwise sealed bags. Freezing a new seed bag for a couple of days, then thawing it before you open it, kills the eggs. Store seed in a hard container. A fabric sack in the pantry is how you discover webbing six weeks later.

Use stainless cups you can lift out daily. Plastic cups scar and hold biofilm, the same way a scarred dog bowl does, except a bird's water is small and turns foul faster. Wash with hot water and dry the cup before it goes back, because a wet cup dilutes the first sip and grows slime at the rim. Hopper feeders save a day of fill for finches and can clog when humid chop is stuffed inside them. Keep hoppers for dry mix only. As a spending picture, small pellet bags often land around $8 to $22, and larger parrot bags scale up from there. Fortified finch mixes are frequently cheaper per bag and still need greens and egg food beside them. Those figures are ordinary shelf ranges. A hand-feeding formula for an unweaned chick is a separate purchase with its own mixing instructions from the breeder or the avian clinic. Do not improvise a hand-feeding diet from pantry pellets.

Questions at the food cup

Can a parrot live on a seed mix if I add vitamins to the water?

A seed mix plus drops in the water still lets the bird pick the fattiest seeds and skip the nutrients sprayed on the dish. Pellets put the balance inside every piece, and vegetables add moisture and variety. Vitamin drops also change the taste of water and can cut drinking. Talk with an avian veterinarian before you add any liquid supplement.

How do I switch a cockatiel from seed to pellets without a hunger strike?

Weigh the bird in grams each morning. Serve pellets as the first cup, then a shrinking measured portion of the old seed later in the day over several weeks. A second cup of pellets alone works better than stirring pellets into a full seed bowl. If weight falls or the bird sits fluffed and still, stop and call an avian veterinarian the same day.

Do finches need the same pellets as a macaw?

No. Finches and canaries eat a fortified mix made for small passerines, with fine greens and egg food during molt. Macaw pellets are too large and are formulated for a parrot. Lories need a nectar diet and daily cup cleaning. Match the bag to the group printed on it.
