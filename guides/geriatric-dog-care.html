Geriatric Dog Care Guide

Caring for senior dogs in their final years. Covers cognitive decline, mobility support, comfort care, end-of-life decisions, and quality of life assessment.

How an older dog shows mobility trouble

An aging dog often tells you about joints before anyone says the word arthritis. The dog hesitates at the car door, takes stairs one at a time, or stands up from a nap with a rock-back that used to be a bounce. Morning stiffness that walks off after a block is a classic pattern. Slipping on kitchen tile, worn nails that click and catch, and a tail that sits lower during a previously easy walk belong on the list you take to the clinic. Film a short clip of the dog rising and walking toward the camera. A thirty-second video beats a description given while the dog is standing still in the exam room, looking brave.

Home changes are mechanical. A ramp or a low step replaces a jump into the car. Rugs on slick floors give the hind feet a purchase. A raised bowl saves a sore neck. Nails trimmed short stop the foot from torquing sideways. A harness with a handle lets you steady the dog on stairs without lifting by the collar. Keep walks frequent and shorter if the old route now ends with panting and a long nap. Swimming or a controlled walk on soft ground can be kinder than repeated ball chasing on pavement. Ask the veterinarian before you add a joint supplement, because some products interact with prescriptions the dog already takes.

Pain in dogs is easy to miss because stoicism looks like good manners. Watch for a dog who stops greeting people at the door, who licks one joint, who pants at rest in a cool room, or who growls when a grandchild hugs the hips. Those are pain behaviors as much as they are mood. A pain trial, chosen and monitored by the veterinarian, is a fair question when the video and the history line up. Do not give human pain tablets. Ibuprofen and naproxen can ulcerate the stomach and injure the kidneys of a dog, and acetaminophen carries its own risks. The clinic has dog-safe options and will match them to bloodwork.

Appetite, teeth, and the slowing metabolism

A senior dog who leaves breakfast once may simply want dinner more. A senior dog who leaves meals for two days, drops weight, or starts drinking and urinating more needs an exam. Dental pain hides inside a normal-looking appetite: the dog approaches the bowl, chews on one side, drops kibble, and walks away. Bad breath that changed recently, pawing at the mouth, and preference for soft food are clues. A dental procedure in an older dog is a real anesthetic event, so the clinic will talk about bloodwork and heart status first. Ignoring the mouth because of age leaves the dog in pain that surgery or an extraction might have ended.

Weight creeps up when the walk shortens and the biscuit habit stays the same. Extra pounds load sore joints. Measure the food. Use part of the daily ration as training treats so the calorie total holds still. A dog who is losing weight while eating well needs a different conversation: kidney disease, diabetes, intestinal disease, and cancer can all increase appetite or waste muscle. Body condition is something you can photograph monthly. Ribs should be felt under a thin fat cover. A sunken belly and a dull coat deserve a scale number and a clinic visit.

Water intake matters as much as food. Fill the bowl at the same time each day and notice whether you are refilling it twice as often. Increased thirst with increased urine, accidents in a previously housetrained dog, or a urine stream that seems weak all belong in the appointment notes. Bring a first-morning urine sample if the clinic asks. Do not restrict water to stop accidents. The accident is information.

Cognitive change in an older dog

Canine cognitive dysfunction can look like stubbornness. The dog stands at the hinge side of the door, stares at a corner, or paces after dark. Sleep flips: long daytime naps and nighttime wandering, sometimes with barking at nothing you can see. Some dogs forget a cue they have known for years, or they get lost in a familiar yard. A new anxiety about being alone, when the dog handled solitude for a decade, can be brain change, pain, or hearing loss. The clinic sorts those by exam, because a painful hip and a failing memory need different help.

You can support a confused dog with a predictable floor plan. Leave furniture where it is. Night lights in the hall reduce startling. A potty trip on a leash late in the evening catches the dog who can no longer signal. Short training games, scent work in the living room, and a food puzzle the dog can still solve keep the day structured. Punishment for accidents or for forgetting a cue adds fear to confusion. If nights become unsafe, ask the veterinarian about medical options and about whether pain control alone settles the pacing. Some dogs improve when the joints hurt less, even when the original complaint was sleep.

Quality-of-life questions to ask the veterinarian

Bring notes from a full week. A single bad afternoon can mislead both of you. Note good moments: interest in a walk, appetite, greeting, comfort at rest. Note hard moments: inability to rise, vomiting, confusion that lasts hours, breathing effort, or pain you cannot touch through. Ask the veterinarian which signs would change the plan this month. Ask what pain control, mobility help, and diagnostics are still useful, and which tests would not change what you do at home. Ask how you will know the plan is working, and what failure looks like in this particular dog.

Questions that help a decision stay concrete: Can this dog stand to eat and to toilet with the help we can actually give? Is there a pain plan we have tried? Are the good hours outnumbering the hard ones across a week? What would an emergency look like so we do not wait through it? Who do we call after hours? A quality-of-life conversation is a medical visit. It can happen while the dog is still having good days, which is the easiest time to hear the options. Hospice-style home care, when the clinic offers it, focuses on cleanliness, pain, appetite, and calm. Euthanasia is discussed as a medical procedure with a plan for the body afterward, including what your county allows for burial and what the clinic can arrange for cremation.

Hearing, vision, and coat care in a slower dog

Hearing loss shows up as a dog who startles when you appear at the shoulder, who misses the recall in the yard, and who sleeps through the doorbell. Approach from the front, use a light stamp on the floor, and keep a leash on for toileting so a missed cue does not become a loose dog in the street. Cloudy lenses can be normal aging or they can be cataracts or glaucoma. A red eye, a sudden bump into furniture, or a cloudy eye with pain needs a prompt visit. Blind dogs often map a stable room well and struggle when chairs move.

The coat mats where the dog can no longer reach: behind the ears, the feathering on the legs, and under the tail. Mats pull the skin and hide hot spots. A slicker brush used for a few minutes after a walk, and a sanitary trim by a groomer who will let the dog stand or lie down, prevents a full shave that startles an old dog. Check the feet for long hair that turns into ice balls or for nails that curve into the pad. Those small jobs change the walk more than a new bed does.

  • Film the dog rising, walking, and navigating stairs before the senior exam.
  • Add rugs, a ramp, a raised bowl, and shorter walks when joints slow the day.
  • Treat mouth pain, thirst changes, and weight loss as exam reasons.
  • Track nighttime pacing, door confusion, and new accidents as possible cognitive change.
  • Skip human pain tablets and ask the clinic for a dog-specific plan.
  • Write a week of good hours and hard hours before any end-of-life decision.
  • Ask which emergency signs should send you in the same day.

Questions about an aging dog

What mobility changes should I record before a senior dog visit?

Hesitation at stairs or the car, morning stiffness, slipping on floors, slower rising, and any growl when the hips are touched. A short video of the dog walking toward you gives the veterinarian more than a still exam can show.

How does cognitive change show up in an older dog?

Night pacing, staring at walls, missing the door hinge, forgotten cues, and new house soiling in a dog who was reliable for years. Pain and hearing loss can mimic this, so the clinic should sort the cause before you assume it is only age.

Which quality-of-life questions are worth asking the veterinarian?

Ask whether the dog can stand to eat and toilet with the help you can give, whether a pain plan has been tried, which signs mean same-day care, and how you will judge a good week versus a hard one. Bring notes from several days. One evening is too thin a sample.
