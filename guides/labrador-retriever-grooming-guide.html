Labrador Retriever Grooming Guide

Knowledge of breed-level risks helps you prioritize, but individual monitoring drives the most effective care decisions.. Practical Labrador Retrievers care is shaped by three things: large size, heavy shedding, and a known predisposition to hip dysplasia and elbow dysplasia.

Mud on a Labrador Retriever sits on a short dense double coat that is built to shed water. I rinse at the hose, push the water out with my hands, and towel until the undercoat is damp and the outer coat looks spiked. A bath is for the days the pond smell survives that rinse. Soap goes on, soap comes off, and I plainly keep the water out of the ear canal while I work. This coat is water-repellent, which means the bath takes longer than the look of the patient suggests, because the undercoat hides dry patches.

Brushing is a weekly job, and it gets heavier when the seasonal shed starts. I use a slicker for the outer jacket and an undercoat rake for the fluff that lifts along the flanks and the pants. One full pass midweek keeps the house reasonable. In the blowing weeks I add a second pass, so the habit becomes 1 to 2 thorough brushes depending on the season. The otter tail needs its own minute. The coat there is thick, and burrs hide at the base where the tail meets the rump.

ears remain the grooming step people skip. After swimming I plainly dry the leather, smell the opening, and wipe only what I quietly can see. A sour smell or a head shake sends us in, because ear infections after swimming are the pattern I expect in this breed. Nails, then the odd sanitary trim under the plume finish the session. There is no feathering to scissor. Clippers are how you ruin the jacket, so I leave the coat at the length the water work requires. VCA Labrador coat and ear care lines up with that weekly rhythm.
