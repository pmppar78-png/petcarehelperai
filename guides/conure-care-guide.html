Conure Care Guide

Complete care guide for Sun Conures, Green Cheek Conures, and other conure species. Covers diet, housing, noise levels, and health care.

Housing and Enclosure

Conures are a group, and the cage has to match the bird you actually have. A green-cheek conure is smaller and quieter than a sun conure, and both still need a cage at least 24 inches wide, 24 inches deep, and 30 inches high, with bar spacing around 5/8 to 3/4 inch. Wider beats taller. These birds fly in bursts and climb, and a narrow tower leaves them clinging to one wall. Medium and large conures, such as patagonian or nanday, need a bigger flight cage and heavier bars. Check the species before you trust a "conure cage" label at the store.

Bar stock should be sturdy. Conures are vigorous chewers and will unscrew a cheap toy and then test the perch bolt. Use stainless dishes, natural wood perches of several diameters, and a rope perch you inspect for frayed loops that can trap a toe. Toys must be shreddable and replaceable: pine blocks, paper, palm, and leather they can destroy. A single plastic baby toy will be rubble, and then the bird will start on the windowsill during out-of-cage time. Site the cage in the family room and well clear of the stove. Nonstick fumes and strong aerosols kill parrots, and conures spend time low in the house where those fumes pool.

Sun conures are loud. Green-cheeks are quieter and still shriek at dawn, at dusk, and when you leave the room. Plan the housing around that sound. An apartment with thin walls and a shift-work neighbor is a poor match for a sun conure. Night frights happen. A dim night light and a cage placed away from a door that slams will prevent some of the thrashing. Cover the cage for 10 to 12 hours if the room stays bright late. A full-spectrum bird lamp can set day length in a dark apartment. It supports the clock. Ask an avian veterinarian whether this bird also needs that lamp for vitamin D, because window glass filters UVB (Merck Veterinary Manual, Nutrition in Pet Birds).

Diet and Nutrition

Pellets should be the majority of the diet, with a large daily share of vegetables and a smaller share of fruit. Conures often accept a wider range of fruit than some parrots, and that enthusiasm is how they get too much sugar. Peppers, squash, broccoli, and leafy greens belong in the bowl every day. Nuts such as a piece of almond or walnut stay in the training pocket. A handful is too much fat. Seed mixes are conversion tools and rewards. A seed-only conure develops fatty liver and a vitamin gap the same way other parrots do.

Avoid avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, and salty snacks. Fresh water in a dish that gets washed, because conures dunk food and splash until the bowl is soup. Calcium comes from the pellet and from a cuttlebone or mineral block. Breeding hens need an avian veterinarian's plan. A nest box in the living room and a hope that eggs will stop is a poor substitute. Chronic laying is common in pet conures that are bonded and stimulated, and it drains calcium fast.

Foraging feeds the mind. A paper-wrapped pellet, a vegetable skewer, and a destroyable box turn breakfast into a twenty-minute job. Pull leftovers the same afternoon. In a warm room a pepper rots, and a conure will keep chewing it. Weigh the bird monthly. A green-cheek is a small animal, and a sun conure can hide weight loss under feathers until the keel is sharp.

Health and Veterinary Care

Proventricular dilatation disease, psittacine beak and feather disease, feather destruction, and, in some birds, conure bleeding syndrome are the frightening end of the list. PDD can show as weight loss and undigested food in the droppings. PBFD affects feathers and the immune system. Feather plucking has medical and behavioral causes, and an avian veterinarian should sort them before you buy a spray. Conure bleeding syndrome is a reported problem in which a bird bleeds from routine injuries or without an obvious wound. Any bleeding is a same-day emergency. Do not wait to see if a broken blood feather clots while the bird sits fluffed.

Respiratory infections, bumblefoot from bad perches, and reproductive disease fill out the ordinary caseload. Annual exams with an avian veterinarian, a gram weight, and a look at the choana and the wings are the maintenance. Conures hide illness behind noise. A quiet conure is a sick conure until proven otherwise. Find the clinic before the night fright that breaks a blood feather at 11 p.m.

Out-of-cage time only happens in a bird-proof room. Ceiling fans, open toilets, hot stoves, and other pets are the injuries clinics see. Teach step-up early. A conure that is allowed to chase and bite as a baby becomes a painful adult, and sun conures in particular need a consistent "down" cue. Wash hands. Parrot dust and dander bother some people, so spend real time with the species before you commit.

Noise, Chewing, and Years

  • Expect contact calls when you leave the room, louder in sun conures than in green-cheeks.
  • Budget for toys you are willing to see destroyed every month.
  • Offer several hours out of the cage with supervision.
  • Keep a carrier ready and an avian veterinarian's number on the fridge.
  • Plan for 20 to 30 years depending on the species. A conure outlasts a school project by many years.

A healthy conure plays with its feet, hangs upside down, and eats a pellet and a piece of pepper with equal interest. A bird on the floor of the cage, bleeding, gasping, or suddenly silent needs help immediately. Match the species to the house, feed pellets and vegetables, and give the beak something legal to destroy.

Species Volume and a Destroyed Toy

Name the conure you have. A green-cheek and a sun conure share a family and differ in size, color, and how far the scream travels. Jenday, nanday, and patagonian conures add more volume and need more cage. If you bought "a conure" without the species, look at the belly color, the size, and the band papers before you decide the cage is big enough. The 24 by 24 by 30 inch starting cage fits the smaller birds. A larger species needs a longer flight and thicker bars, and it will demonstrate that fact by bending a thin perch pin.

Budget toys as consumables. A conure that finishes a pine block in a week is using its beak correctly. A conure with nothing left to destroy will audit your picture frames during out-of-cage time. Keep stainless quick-links, and cut off frayed rope before a toe finds the loop. Supervise every minute outside the cage. Fans, toilets, and hot pans are the emergencies avian clinics see on weekends, along with a broken blood feather from a night fright. A dim light and a cage away from the door prevent some of those crashes.

Teach a down cue while the bird is still impressed by a sunflower seed. Sun conures in particular become shoulder birds that lunge once hormones and habit agree. Step-up, a station on the stand, and a consistent bedtime of 10 to 12 hours keep the relationship trainable. Pellets and vegetables stay the bowl. Nuts stay small. An avian veterinarian should meet the bird in the first month, before a quiet day tricks you into waiting. A quiet conure is a reason to call, because the healthy ones narrate the house.

Quick Answers

Are conures good for first-time bird owners?

Green-cheek conures are a possible first parrot for someone ready for noise, chewing, and a cage at least 24 by 24 by 30 inches. Sun conures are louder and a harder fit for apartments and first-time owners.

How much does conure care cost?

A chew-proof cage is the large opening purchase, often several hundred dollars, and the avian exam is separate. Clinic fee roundups published for 2026 place that bird wellness visit near $95 to $185 before lab work (findanexoticvet.com). AAV's owner page asks you to keep wellness visits regular for the life of the parrot (aav.org). Toys, pellets, and produce continue for decades.
