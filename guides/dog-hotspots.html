Hot Spots on Dogs

Causes and treatment of hot spots (acute moist dermatitis) in dogs. Covers home treatment, when to see the vet, and preventing recurrence.

What a hotspot is

A hotspot is pyotraumatic dermatitis, a surface skin infection that a dog creates by chewing, licking, or scratching one spot until it is raw. The lesion is hot, moist, painful, and it spreads in hours as the hair mats over a weeping sore. Bacteria from the skin move in. The itch or the pain that started the chewing is usually somewhere else: a flea bite, an allergic itch, an infected ear the dog cannot reach cleanly, a mat that stayed wet, or an anal-sac problem. If you only dry the circle and ignore the reason the dog went after it, a new circle opens next to the scar.

Deep pyoderma can hide under the same hair loss. That infection goes into the follicles and the dermis, and it stays unfinished when the surface dries. Cytology is how a clinic tells a superficial hotspot from a deeper infection that needs a longer drug course.

Which dogs get

Thick-coated, water-loving, and allergic dogs lead the list. Golden Retrievers, Labrador Retrievers, St. Bernards, Newfoundlands, German Shepherd Dogs, and Collies are classic. The season is warm and humid, or any time a dog stays in a wet coat after a swim or a bath. Dogs with fleas chew the rump and the tail head. Dogs with ear disease chew the neck under the ear. Dogs with atopic dermatitis chew the flank, the hip, and the face. Puppies can develop a hotspot over a minor scrape. A dog who was clipped short for summer can still get one where a mat was left against the skin. Any breed with an itch and a mouth can make a hotspot in an afternoon.

Signs owners notice

You find a patch of wet, stuck hair and, underneath, a red, oozing, foul area that is bigger than it was that morning. The dog lunges to lick it and may cry when it is touched. The edge is sharp, and satellite red bumps may sit nearby if the infection is going deeper. Common sites are the cheek below an ear, the rump, the hip, the flank, and the neck. Fever, lethargy, and several lesions at once mean this is more than a surface sore. A swelling of the face with hives is a different emergency, an allergic reaction, even if the dog has also rubbed a hotspot into the skin.

  • A rapidly growing wet sore under matted fur.
  • Pain, a sour smell, and frantic licking.
  • A location that hints at the driver: under the ear, on the rump, or along the flank.
  • Fleas or flea dirt found with a comb at the same visit.
  • A dog who is dull or febrile, which suggests a deeper skin infection.

How a veterinarian

A hotspot, pyotraumatic dermatitis, is diagnosed on the skin exam. The lesion is a painful, moist, rapidly spreading patch, usually where the dog has been chewing. Cytology of the surface looks for a deep infection that a surface ointment will miss. Skin scrapings are added when mites are plausible. Fleas, an ear infection, or an allergy still have to be looked for.

The hair is clipped wide so the true margin is visible. An impression smear or tape prep shows cocci, rods, or yeast and shows whether white cells are diving into a deeper infection. Scrapings look for Demodex and other mites when the pattern is odd or the dog is young. The ear canals are examined because a neck hotspot is often an ear infection the dog is trying to scratch. A flea comb is part of the exam even in a "flea-free" house. Culture and biopsy are for a lesion that does not match a hotspot, that fails to heal, or that looks like a fungal disease or a skin tumor. Most first hotspots never need a biopsy.

Treatment categories

Treatment of a hotspot is clipping the hair, cleaning the skin, and treating infection and pain. The veterinarian chooses the drug and how long it is used. A single hotspot that is clipped and treated can dry in days. It returns if the flea, the ear, or the allergy is left alone. Biopsy and culture are for a lesion that does not match a hotspot or does not heal.

Clipping has to go beyond the red edge, or the infection simply walks under the remaining wet hair. Cleaning with a veterinary antiseptic, often a chlorhexidine solution the clinic sends home, dries the surface. A short course of an antibiotic, a topical product, and something for itch and pain are chosen from the smear and from how sick the dog is. A steroid, if used, is the veterinarian's decision for a few days so the dog can stop chewing. An Elizabethan collar is treatment. Without it the tongue undoes the clip. Flea control for every pet in the home, ear medication if the canal is infected, and a plan for allergic skin disease are what keep the next hotspot from forming. A thick ointment from a cupboard can seal bacteria against the wet skin. Skip it unless the clinic named that product.

Home care

Keep the collar on except when you are watching the dog eat, if the clinic allowed that break. Clean the lesion as demonstrated, then let it dry. Do not cover it with a greasy salve or with a bandage that stays damp. Keep the dog out of lakes and sprinklers until the skin has closed. Dry the coat thoroughly after any bath, and brush out mats so skin can breathe. Continue flea prevention on the date it is due, including the cats. If the dog has a history of allergic skin, this hotspot is a reason to book that workup. Write down whether the dog is still frantic to lick once the collar is briefly off at the recheck. A dog who is calm and a lesion that is dry and smaller are the signs the short course worked.

When to See

A hotspot the size of a coin in a bright dog can be seen the same day by a regular veterinarian, and it should be, because the circle grows. Go to an emergency clinic if the dog is febrile, dull, unwilling to eat, or has a lesion that is spreading across a large area within hours. Facial swelling that closes an eye, hives, or noisy breathing is an allergic emergency and may be separate from the hotspot. Deep, painful, draining tracts with a sick dog are deep pyoderma or an abscess and need an exam before a leftover ointment is tried. A lesion that smells like rot and sits over a puncture could be a bite wound, which is a different cleaning and a different drug choice.

What to ask

A single hotspot is a surface infection that usually heals after the fur is clipped away, the skin is cleaned, and the dog finishes the short course a veterinarian chooses. Pyotraumatic dermatitis is moist, painful, and fast-growing. The circle comes back when fleas, allergic skin disease, or a coat that stays wet are still present, so healed skin can sit next to an unfinished allergy. Deep pyoderma under the same hair loss is a different diagnosis and stays unfinished when the surface dries.

Treat today's hotspot, pyotraumatic dermatitis, as an exam, a clip, and a short medication course, and ask what a summer of repeat hotspots would cost if fleas or an allergy are the driver. The first lesion is a modest invoice. Monthly new lesions become a dermatology workup. Get both figures at the first visit so the cheap visit cannot be mistaken for the whole problem.

Key Questions

Can I clip and clean a hotspot at home?

A tiny, first lesion in a bright dog still benefits from a clinic visit, because the clip has to be wide enough and the smear decides the drug. Home clipping often stops at the matted edge and leaves infection underneath. If you cannot get in for several hours, an Elizabethan collar to stop the licking helps more than a kitchen ointment. See a veterinarian the same day if the dog is painful, the patch is growing, or you cannot tell whether it is a hotspot.

Why did another hotspot appear after this one healed?

The surface healed and the driver stayed. Fleas, atopic dermatitis, a food reaction, an ear infection, or a coat that never fully dries will send the dog back to the same chew. Flea control has to include every animal in the house. If the skin is itchy between hotspots, that is an allergy workup. Another week of the same spray skips that workup. Cytology on the new lesion confirms whether you are still dealing with bacteria, a mite, or a yeast problem.

How is a hotspot different from a lick granuloma?

A hotspot is an acute, moist, spreading infection measured in hours. A lick granuloma is a chronic, raised, firm plaque, often on a foreleg, that a dog worries at for weeks. They are treated differently. A granuloma needs a search for joint pain, allergy, or a behavioral driver, and it rarely dries up after one clip. Calling every wet sore a hotspot misses that slower lesion and delays the right plan.
