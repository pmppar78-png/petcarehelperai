Ball Python Complete Care Guide

Complete ball python care including enclosure setup, humidity, feeding schedules, handling, common health issues, and morph overview.

Housing and Enclosure

Ball pythons are heavy-bodied snakes that need floor space and snug hides. A tall tree cage wastes the footprint they use. An adult does well in an enclosure with at least 4 square feet of floor, and a 4-foot by 2-foot footprint is the size many keepers move to once the snake is past the juvenile tub. A 40-gallon glass tank can hold a young adult and often runs too dry and too open. PVC or a well-sealed tub holds the humidity these snakes need, around 60 to 70 percent, with higher spikes during shed. Measure humidity at substrate level. A gauge stuck to the ceiling lies.

The warm side should offer 88 to 92 F, and the cool side about 76 to 80 F, both controlled so a hot day cannot push the warm hide past the mid 90s. The piece that matters most is the warm hide: a cave small enough that the snake touches the walls, sitting on the warm end. Add a second, equally snug hide on the cool end. Ball pythons that only have one hide must choose between temperature and security, and they usually choose security and then refuse food. A thermostat on the heat source is mandatory. Heat tape, a radiant panel, or a ceramic heater can all work. An unregulated heat rock is a burn waiting under a coiled snake.

UVB is optional and, when you offer it, should be low output with plenty of shade. Ball pythons are crepuscular. They benefit from a day-night cycle and can use a gentle linear UVB the way other snakes can, and they do not need a desert basking tube. Calcium dusting is the wrong tool. A ball python that eats whole prey already swallows bone, organs, and the minerals a lizard has to get from powder. If a snake is refusing food for a long stretch, the answer is husbandry and a reptile veterinarian. A calcium shaker over a rat misses the problem.

Substrate can be coconut husk, a soil mix, or paper while you are quarantining. Provide branches only if they are sturdy. A falling snake in a tall tank injures ribs. Cover three sides of a glass tank so the animal stops pacing the reflection. A heavy water bowl, big enough for a soak during shed, sits on the cool side so it does not steam the warm hide into a swamp.

Diet and Nutrition

Feed frozen-thawed prey, fully thawed and warmed to something near body temperature. The prey item should be about as wide as the snake at mid-body. Hatchlings often start on fuzzy mice or rat pups and move to rats as they grow. Adults commonly eat an appropriately sized rat every 10 to 14 days. Juveniles eat more often, closer to every 7 days. A snake that looks round in cross-section is getting too much or too often. A triangular, loose-skinned snake that is otherwise alert may need a smaller, more frequent meal or a warmer hide so it can digest.

Thaw prey in the refrigerator and warm it in a bag in hot water. Do not microwave a rat. Hot spots explode and cold centers chill the stomach. Feeding in the enclosure is fine for most ball pythons if you use tongs and a consistent signal. Moving the snake to a separate tub stresses some individuals into refusal. Live prey can injure a snake and is a poor plan once frozen-thawed has been offered correctly. A feeding strike, the famous ball python hunger, often lasts weeks and sometimes months. Check temperatures, hides, and weight. A snake that is losing weight, wrinkling, or refusing far beyond its own pattern needs a veterinarian. A snake that holds weight and body condition through a winter pause may simply be pausing.

Handle on non-feeding days. Many bites are a snake that smelled rodent and met a hand. Tap the cage or use a hook to wake the animal before you lift. Support the body. A dangling snake feels unsafe and will clamp.

Health and Veterinary Care

Respiratory infections are the humidity-and-heat disease: wheezing, a raised head, bubbles at the mouth, and open-mouth breathing. Low humidity, a cool cage, or a wet substrate with no ventilation can all contribute. Scale rot shows as brown or red belly scales when the snake sits on soaked, dirty bedding. Mites look like moving specks in the eye caps and water bowl and require treating the snake and the entire enclosure. Inclusion body disease is a serious viral concern in pythons, associated with neurological signs such as stargazing. Quarantine new snakes for several months, well past a short stay, and do not mix a new purchase with an established collection. Ask a reptile veterinarian about testing if you keep more than one boid.

Stuck shed on the eye cap or tail tip follows a dry cage. Raise humidity and offer a humid hide of damp moss. Do not peel an eye cap. A retained cap needs a clinician. Mouth rot, regurgitation, and mites are reasons to book that clinician before you buy a pet-store remedy. Weigh the snake monthly in grams. Ball pythons hide weight loss inside a loose coil until the spine is obvious.

Annual exams are reasonable for a snake you expect to keep for two or three decades. Bring the temperature readings and a photo of the last shed. A complete shed, including both eye caps, in one piece, is one of the best husbandry report cards you can show.

Settling a New Snake

  • Set both hides and confirm warm-hide and cool-side temperatures before the snake comes home.
  • Leave the animal alone, with water, until it has taken two or three meals.
  • Keep humidity high enough that the next shed comes off clean, and ventilate so the substrate surface is damp only in the humid hide.
  • Record weights. A feeding pause with stable weight is a different problem from a feeding pause with loss.
  • Wash hands between snakes if you ever keep more than one, and quarantine newcomers.

A secure ball python spends the day wedged in the warm hide, comes out at night to cruise the back wall, and eats with a slow, purposeful strike. A snake that glass-surfs all day, or that sits in the open with a wheeze, is asking for a tighter hide, a corrected gradient, or a veterinary visit. Give it the hide first, and call the clinic if breathing is involved.

Two Hides and a Feeding Pause

Give a new ball python both hides and then leave it alone until it has eaten more than once. The warm hide should read about 88 to 92 F where the snake's belly rests, and the cool hide should sit near 76 to 80 F. Humidity around 60 to 70 percent, higher in a moss cup during shed, produces a complete shed with both eye caps. A snake that only has one hide will pick security and then refuse food because it cannot also pick the right temperature. That is the usual beginner stall, and it looks like a "picky species" when it is a cage with one cave.

Weigh the snake in grams. A pause of several weeks with a stable weight can be a normal ball python pattern. Weight loss, a wrinkled spine, wheezing, or bubbles at the mouth need a reptile veterinarian. Feed frozen-thawed prey about as wide as the snake's mid-body, on tongs, fully thawed. Whole prey already supplies calcium. A low UVB tube with shade is optional daylight, and a desert lamp will overheat a crepuscular snake. Quarantine newcomers for months if you keep other pythons, and ask about inclusion body disease testing. A secure animal spends the day wedged in the warm hide and cruises at night. Glass-surfing all day means the hides are too open or the temperatures are wrong.

Quick Answers

Are ball pythons good for beginner snake owners?

They can be, if you provide a warm hide at 88 to 92 F, a cool hide, and humidity around 60 to 70 percent, and if you can tolerate a snake that sometimes skips meals. They are a weak choice if you want a snake that eats every Tuesday in a dry glass tank.

How much does ball python care cost?

A secure enclosure, thermostat, and two proper hides are the main opening cost and often exceed a cheap aquarium kit. Frozen rats are inexpensive month to month. The veterinary relationship is the real budget item: a 2026 roundup of exotic-clinic fees puts a reptile wellness exam at about $95 to $185 before diagnostics (findanexoticvet.com). A respiratory workup sits on top of that exam fee.
