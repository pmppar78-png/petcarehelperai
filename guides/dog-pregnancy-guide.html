Dog Pregnancy and Whelping Guide

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Detailed guide to dog pregnancy from mating through whelping. Covers signs of pregnancy, nutrition, preparing a whelping box, labor stages, and newborn care.

How long a dog is pregnant

A typical dog pregnancy runs about 63 days from ovulation, and the window people actually observe is often about 58 to 68 days from the breeding they remember. The breeding date and the ovulation date are different events, which is why two families with "the same" timeline can be a week apart. A veterinarian can narrow the due window with timed progesterone before breeding, or later with ultrasound and, in the last couple of weeks, with radiographs that count skulls and spines. Palpation in the mid-30s days and ultrasound a bit earlier can confirm that a pregnancy exists. A radiograph too early misses skeletons because they have not mineralized.

Appetite often rises after the first few weeks. Some dogs have a brief quiet period and a picky appetite around the third week, then eat well. Nipples enlarge and the belly softens and widens in the second half. Clear or slightly milky discharge can be normal late in pregnancy. Green, black, bloody, or foul discharge before any puppy is born is a reason to call the clinic immediately. A dog who was pregnant and then seems suddenly "empty," with weakness or pale gums, needs emergency care because a uterus can rupture or a puppy can die and make the dog septic.

Feed a food labeled for growth or for pregnancy and lactation once the veterinarian agrees, usually in the second half, split into smaller meals so a full uterus can share the abdomen with a modest breakfast. The amount climbs in late pregnancy and again while she is nursing. This page does not list cups or grams. The bag, the body condition, and the clinic set that. Keep her on the parasite preventives the veterinarian says are appropriate in pregnancy. Skip anything you have not asked about, including herbal supplements and leftover antibiotics.

The week you set up for whelping

Prepare a box in a quiet room before day 55. The sides should be high enough to keep newborns in and low enough for the mother to step over. Line it with washable layers. Show her the box daily so labor does not start on your bed by surprise. Gather clean towels, a bulb syringe for fluid in a puppy's mouth, unwaxed dental floss and clean scissors in case you must tie and cut a cord the mother ignores, a scale that reads small weights, and the clinic's after-hours number. A heat source such as a pad made for whelping, with a space the puppies can crawl off, waits in the closet until you need it. Overheating newborns is as dangerous as chilling them.

Ask the veterinarian ahead of time what "go in now" means for this dog, especially if she is a bulldog, a French bulldog, a Boston terrier, or another breed with a history of difficult birth. Those dogs often need a planned cesarean, and the timing is a medical decision based on progesterone, temperature trends, and imaging. Do not wait for heroic home labor in a breed the clinic already flagged. Write the route to the emergency hospital and put a crate in the car a week early.

Signs that labor has started

Many dogs refuse dinner, pant, dig, nest, and shiver in the day labor begins. A rectal temperature that has been steady may dip for a short time in the day before active pushing, then rise again. The dip is a clue, and it is easy to miss if you check once. Restlessness that settles when she lies in the box is early labor. Intermittent contractions can go on for hours while the cervix opens. Active, strong, productive pushing is the stage that has a clock on it.

The first puppy is often preceded by a straw-colored or blood-tinged fluid when the sac breaks. Puppies may arrive inside a membrane. The mother usually clears it, licks the face, and bites the cord. If she does not clear the membrane within a moment, you tear it from the face and rub the puppy with a towel. Fluid in the mouth can be sucked with the bulb syringe. A puppy who is still and blue gets a brisk rub. A puppy who does not start breathing with that attention goes to the clinic with the mother if you can move safely, while someone keeps clearing the airway. Tie a cord that is bleeding. Leave a cord that is simply long. Iodine debates can wait. Breathing cannot.

Count placentas if you can, knowing mothers eat them. A retained placenta is suspected when discharge smells rotten in the days after whelping or when she stays ill. Each puppy should nurse within a couple of hours. Weigh them twice a day. A puppy who loses weight or who is constantly shoved off a teat needs help, sometimes as simple as a rotation to a better teat and sometimes as a clinic visit for supplemental feeding instructions. Ask the clinic before you buy a milk replacer and follow that product's directions, because homemade formulas injure kidneys and bowels.

When to go to a veterinarian during whelping

Call immediately if she has strong contractions for 30 minutes with no puppy, or if she has been in active hard labor and more than two hours pass between puppies while she is still straining. Call if a puppy or a sac is visible at the vulva and does not advance with a couple of pushes. Call for green or black discharge before the first puppy, for collapse, for frantic pain, for a large breed dog who seems done but still looks heavily pregnant, or for more than a drop of persistent bleeding. A thermometer, a list of birth times, and a count of puppies help the person on the phone.

After the last puppy, she should settle, nurse, and pass any remaining membranes. Continued straining, vomiting, pale gums, or a foul smell means the birth is still in trouble. A checkup within a day or two lets the clinic confirm she is empty, talk about eclampsia risk in small mothers with big litters, and plan dewclaw or tail decisions only if you had that discussion beforehand. Eclampsia is a low-calcium emergency in the nursing period: restlessness, stiffness, tremors, and seizures. It is a drive-in emergency. Do not pour human calcium pills down a seizing dog. The clinic gives calcium in a controlled way.

Puppies who cry constantly, feel cool, or pile in a heap away from the mother are cold or hungry. Warm them before you try to feed them. A cold puppy cannot digest milk. The mother needs food, water, and quiet. Children watch from a distance. A bloody discharge can continue for a few weeks and should slowly darken and lessen. It should not smell rotten, and she should not be depressed. Mastitis shows up as a hot, painful gland and a mother who suddenly avoids nursing. That gland needs a clinic visit the same day.

  • Expect roughly nine weeks, with imaging used to count puppies and narrow the date.
  • Treat green or black discharge before the first puppy as an emergency.
  • Set up the box, towels, a scale, and the after-hours number before day 55.
  • Call if hard pushing lasts 30 minutes with no puppy, or if active straining gaps run past two hours.
  • Clear a membrane from a puppy's face if the mother does not.
  • Weigh newborns twice a day and ask for help when a puppy loses ground.
  • Treat tremors in a nursing mother as a possible calcium emergency and drive in.
  • Plan a cesarean ahead of time for breeds the clinic has already flagged.

Questions about dog pregnancy and whelping

How long is a dog pregnant?

About 63 days from ovulation, with a real-world window often landing between 58 and 68 days from a remembered breeding. Ultrasound and late-pregnancy radiographs, ordered by a veterinarian, confirm puppies and help time the birth, especially when a cesarean might be needed.

What are the signs that a dog is in labor?

Nesting, panting, refusing food, and restlessness often start first. A brief temperature dip can precede active labor. Strong pushing, a fluid sac, and the birth of a puppy mark active labor. Green or black discharge before any puppy is a reason to call at once.

When should whelping send us to a veterinarian?

Call for hard contractions lasting about 30 minutes with no puppy, more than about two hours of active straining between puppies, a puppy stuck at the vulva, collapse, or foul discharge. Small mothers who tremble while nursing need emergency care for possible eclampsia.
