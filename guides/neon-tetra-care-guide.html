Neon Tetra Care Guide

Guide to keeping neon tetras including school size, tank setup, water parameters, diet, compatible tank mates, and neon tetra disease.

Housing and Enclosure

Neon tetras are schooling fish from shaded, soft water, and they look like a different animal when the group is large and the tank is planted. A school of at least six, and better ten or more, belongs in a mature aquarium of 10 gallons or larger. Twenty gallons lets a proper group spread out and still have a dark back corner. They are tropical. Keep the water roughly 70 to 81 F with a thermostat heater and a thermometer you actually read. Slightly acidic conditions, about pH 6.0 to 7.0, match them better than a high-pH rift-lake tank.

Light should be moderate or dim. Floating plants, tall stems, and a dark substrate make the blue stripe show and give the school somewhere to settle when someone walks past the glass. A gentle filter, sponge or baffled, keeps fry-sized bodies from being plastered to an intake. Neons are poor candidates for a brand-new, uncycled, brightly lit tank with two plastic plants. They do their best work in a tank that has already been running and growing biofilm.

Buy the school in one trip when you can, from a shop that can tell you how long the fish have been in the system. Adding two neons a month to "build the school" often means the newcomers never join the group and arrive carrying the disease the species is named for. Quarantine is awkward with fish this small and still worth a spare 5- or 10-gallon tank with its own sponge.

Tank mates should be calm and similarly sized. Harlequin rasboras, small corydoras, and cherry shrimp sometimes work once the neons are settled. Avoid tiger barbs, angelfish large enough to swallow a neon, and any fish that needs hard alkaline water. Neons will not clean algae and they will not thrive as a lone pair bought to "add color" to a goldfish tank. The temperature and the temperament both miss.

Diet and Nutrition

Their mouths are tiny. Crush a quality flake between your fingers or offer a micro pellet made for small tetras. Feed once or twice a day, a portion that vanishes in a minute. A school that has to hunt through a cloud of leftover food is a school living in tomorrow's ammonia. In a planted tank they also pick at biofilm, which only supplements the prepared food.

Frozen or freeze-dried baby brine shrimp, daphnia, and finely chopped bloodworms a couple of times a week keep body condition up. Rinse frozen food. Wild-caught mosquito larvae from a bucket in the yard can introduce parasites, so cultured foods are the safer protein. Color foods marketed for neons are still just small pellets. The stripe fades when the fish is stressed, ill, or kept under blasting light, and no pellet repaints a sick animal.

Watch the thinnest fish in the school at feeding time. Neons are quick, and a fish hanging back may be the first one sliding into disease. Feed in a slight current so food drifts through the group, and so one bold tetra cannot take the whole pinch at the surface.

Health and Veterinary

Neon tetra disease is the diagnosis keepers fear, and it is a real parasitic infection caused by Pleistophora hyphessobryconis. There is no cure. Fish lose color, rest apart from the school, and may develop a lumpy or pale patch in the muscle. Remove affected fish promptly so the rest of the school has a chance. Other wasting illnesses can look similar, which is why a fish veterinarian is useful, but you should not wait on a lab result while a clearly affected fish shares the tank. Quarantine every new neon. The disease has a reputation precisely because shops mix shipments.

Ich and ordinary fin rot still happen, usually after a chill or a dirty week. The broader pattern is the one in the Merck Veterinary Manual: ammonia and nitrite poisoning is the most common aquarium problem, and it follows an uncycled tank, overfeeding, a dead fish left among the plants, or a filter that stopped. Ammonia and nitrite should both read 0 ppm. Neons cannot regulate their body temperature, so a thermometer and a thermostat heater are basic gear for this tropical species. Parasites besides Pleistophora usually arrive on new fish or plants. Bacterial infections such as fin rot usually follow poor water or an injury from a nip. Copper treatments poison shrimp, snails, and plants and can set filter bacteria back, so test ammonia and nitrite for weeks after any copper medicine.

That pattern is laid out in the Merck Veterinary Manual guide to aquarium fish management.

A neon that flashes, sprouts white spots, or gasps in a warm, fully cycled tank may have ich and needs isolation plus warmer, very clean water while you follow a fish vet's plan. A neon that simply fades and separates from the school, with no spots, is the one you remove for neon tetra disease. Mixing those two responses, and dosing the whole display "just in case," is how planted shrimp tanks lose their invertebrates.

Settling a School

  • Wait until the tank is mature and ammonia and nitrite have held at zero.
  • Release the school together after a slow temperature match and a gradual water mix.
  • Dim the light for the first day so they find the plants before they find the glass.
  • Add other species later, a few fish at a time, once the neons are eating.
  • Keep hands and soap out of the water. Use a net briefly if you must move a fish.

A settled school moves as a loose ribbon through the middle of the tank, stripes flashing on and off as they turn. Tight shivering in a top corner, day after day, means the group is too small, the light is too hard, or something in the water is wrong. Count the fish every evening. Neons die quietly in the plants, and a body left there is enough to restart an ammonia problem in a small tank.

Keeping the Stripe With the School

Count neon tetras every evening for the first month. They die in the plants without a drama at the surface, and a body left overnight is enough ammonia to stress the survivors in a small tank. A missing fish that you find pale and separated from the group, with no white spots, is the one you remove because neon tetra disease has no treatment. A fish with salt-grain spots may have ich and belongs in a quarantine tank while you talk to a fish veterinarian. Those are different problems, and a bottle poured into the display "to be safe" is how shrimp and the filter bacteria get hurt.

Light and cover decide whether the school behaves. If the tetras stack in a top corner, add floating plants or shorten the photoperiod before you add more fish. A school of ten in a dim, mature 20-gallon tank will ribbon through the middle. A pair in a bare 10-gallon tank under a blast of light will shiver and fade. Feed a crushed flake or a micro pellet they finish in a minute, and use a thawed baby brine shrimp or daphnia a couple of times a week so the thinner fish get a meal that drifts past them.

Buy the group together when you can, quarantine them, and only then introduce calmer tank mates. Add those mates a few at a time after the neons are eating. Angelfish large enough to swallow a neon, and any fish that needs hard alkaline water, belong in a different aquarium. The heater stays on this tropical species, confirmed with a thermometer, and ammonia and nitrite stay at zero. That combination, more than a color-enhancing food, is what keeps the blue line bright.

Quick Answers

Are neon tetras good for beginner fish keepers?

They can be, in a mature, planted tank of at least 10 gallons with slightly acidic water. Keep at least 6, ideally 10 or more, and quarantine new fish because Neon Tetra Disease has no cure.

What is Neon Tetra Disease?

A parasitic infection caused by Pleistophora hyphessobryconis. There is no cure, so infected fish should be removed promptly to protect the rest of the school.
