New Puppy Checklist and First Month Guide

Everything you need for a new puppy including supplies checklist, first vet visit, potty training start, socialization window, and feeding schedule.

The first two days at home

Pick the puppy up in a crate that already smells like the blanket from the litter, and keep the car ride quiet. At the door, carry the puppy to the outdoor toilet spot before any tour of the house. A short successful trip outside teaches the surface you want used, and it keeps the first accident off the rug. Offer water, then a small meal of the same food the puppy has been eating. A sudden brand change on night one is a common cause of loose stool, so plan a slow mix over several days if you intend to switch foods later.

Limit the puppy to one room for the first week. A baby gate, a crate with the door open at first, a bed, and a water bowl are enough. Cords go up, trash gets a lid, and shoes leave the floor. The puppy should sleep where you can hear whining. Most puppies need a trip outside once or twice overnight for the first week or two. Ignore the party and reward the quiet return to the crate with a calm word. A crate is a bedroom. It is a poor place for punishment, because the puppy has to want to rest there tomorrow night too.

Write down the time of every meal, every outdoor trip, and every accident. That log shows the interval the puppy can actually hold, which is shorter than most families guess. A young puppy often needs to go out after waking, after eating, after play, and after a visitor leaves. Take the puppy to the same patch of yard, wait, and praise the moment the puppy finishes. Clean indoor accidents with an enzymatic cleaner so the spot does not keep inviting a repeat.

The first veterinary visit and the vaccines by name

Book the clinic visit for within a few days of arrival, even when a breeder or shelter already handed you a card. Carry the record, any deworming notes, and a fresh stool sample in a sealed bag. The veterinarian will look at weight, heart, lungs, eyes, ears, skin, bite, and the belly, and will talk about intestinal parasites that are ordinary in puppies. Ask which core vaccines this individual still needs. In the United States the usual combination covers distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, and parainfluenza, sold under names such as DHPP or DA2PP. Rabies is a legal vaccine in most counties, and the clinic times it to local rules.

Bordetella comes up when the puppy will meet other dogs at class, daycare, or a boarding kennel. Leptospirosis comes up when the puppy will walk near wildlife, puddles, or farm runoff. Neither one belongs on every dog by default, and the clinic that can see this puppy should choose. The series is repeated across the early months because maternal antibodies fade on different schedules. There is no milliliter chart on this page. The clinic writes the dates. Until the veterinarian says the parvovirus series has gone far enough, skip dog parks, pet-store floors, and shared water bowls.

Ask about fecal testing, a deworming plan matched to the stool result, and a microchip if the puppy does not already have one. Ask which flea and heartworm products are labeled for this age and this weight, and give only those. Store human medicines and garden chemicals where a puppy mouth cannot reach them. Put the clinic number and the nearest emergency hospital number on the fridge and in your phone before you need them at night.

How often puppies eat

Frequency follows age more than any slogan on a bag. A puppy in the eight-to-twelve-week window usually eats three or four small meals a day. From about three months through about six months, three meals fit most dogs. After six months, many puppies settle into breakfast and dinner. Toy breeds and puppies who were thin at pickup may stay on an extra meal longer. The food label and the veterinarian set the daily amount. Split that amount across the meals. Handing the whole day's calories at dawn leaves the evening empty and the belly overloaded.

Measure with the same scoop each time. A heaping cup and a level cup are different meals. Free-feeding a puppy makes housetraining harder, because you lose the clock that predicts the next outdoor trip. Fresh water stays down all day unless the clinic has told you otherwise for a specific medical reason. Table scraps, grapes, raisins, onion, garlic, xylitol gum, chocolate, and cooked bones stay off the menu. A chew that is too hard for a baby tooth can crack it. Choose a toy you can dent with a fingernail.

If the puppy skips one meal and is otherwise bright, offer it again at the next scheduled time. Repeated refusal, vomiting, a bloated belly, or diarrhea with blood is a same-day clinic call. Puppies dehydrate quickly. Bring a photo of the stool and a note of what was eaten, including anything stolen from a counter.

Socialization while the vaccine series is still running

The sensitive social window is roughly the first three months of life, and it continues in a quieter way after that. The goal is a puppy who has met calm people, heard ordinary household noise, and stood on a few different surfaces while feeling safe. Carry the puppy into a hardware store aisle, a friend's kitchen, or a quiet sidewalk. Let the puppy watch children from a lap. Pair each new sight with a treat and a way to leave. Move a frightened puppy farther away. Forcing a closer look makes the fear worse.

Puppy class with a vaccine policy is useful once the clinic agrees the puppy is ready. Street meetings with unknown dogs are a poor substitute for that class, because you cannot see the other dog's health or manners. Handle paws, ears, and the mouth for a few seconds every day so nail trims and dental checks are familiar. End each session while the puppy is still willing. Short and successful beats long and overwhelming.

Crate, potty, and the first month of manners

The crate should be large enough to stand, turn, and lie down, with a divider if the crate is the one the dog will use as an adult. Feed a meal beside the open door, then just inside. Close the door for a minute while you sit nearby, then open it before fussing starts. Overnight, the crate can sit next to the bed. Daytime absences start at a few minutes and grow only when the puppy is quiet. A puppy left for a full workday in a crate is being asked for a bladder skill that has not developed yet. A midday visit, a pet sitter, or a pen with a toilet area belongs in the plan.

Biting is how a puppy explores. Trade the hand for a toy every time teeth touch skin, and end the game when the bite is hard. Children need a script: stand up and become boring. Chew toys rotate so the puppy stays interested. Name practice is a handful of treats said once, then paid when the puppy looks. Sit can wait until the household routine is steady. A puppy who knows the name and the toilet spot is ahead of a puppy who knows a trick and is still soiling the hallway.

By the end of the first month you should have a clinic relationship, a written vaccine plan, a feeding rhythm, a crate the puppy will enter, a toilet spot, and a short list of people and sounds the puppy has met calmly. Weigh the puppy at home or at the clinic often enough to see a steady gain. A puppy who is losing weight, coughing, limping, or scratching until the skin breaks needs an appointment. Another week of watching wastes time.

  • Keep the food the puppy already knows for the first days, then change it gradually if you switch brands.
  • Carry vaccine papers and a stool sample to the first clinic visit.
  • Ask about DHPP or DA2PP, rabies, and whether Bordetella or leptospirosis fits this puppy's life.
  • Feed three or four meals a day in the early weeks, then taper the number of meals as the puppy matures.
  • Take the puppy out after sleep, meals, and play, and keep a simple log.
  • Use the crate as a rest place and build the time slowly.
  • Introduce people, surfaces, and noises in short happy sessions while you still control the distance.
  • Save dog parks for after the clinic says the parvovirus series has gone far enough.

Puppy questions for the first month

When should a new puppy see a veterinarian?

Within a few days of coming home, even if a shelter or breeder already gave vaccines. Bring the records and a fresh stool sample so the clinic can plan the rest of the series, a parasite check, and products labeled for that age.

Which vaccines do puppy owners usually hear about?

The core combination is distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, and parainfluenza, often labeled DHPP or DA2PP, plus rabies on the local legal schedule. Bordetella and leptospirosis are added when lifestyle makes them relevant. The clinic sets the dates. This page has no dose chart.

How many meals does a young puppy need?

Puppies around eight to twelve weeks often eat three or four small meals a day. Many move to three meals by three to six months and to two meals after six months. The bag and the veterinarian set the amount. The clock sets how you split it.
