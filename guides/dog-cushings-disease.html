Cushing's Disease in

Practical guide to Cushing's disease (hyperadrenocorticism) in dogs. Covers symptoms, diagnosis, treatment options, and managing quality of life.

What Cushing's disease is

Cushing's disease, hyperadrenocorticism, is a long excess of cortisol. The common form is pituitary-dependent. A small pituitary tumor secretes too much ACTH, both adrenal glands answer by making cortisol, and the dog lives with a hormone surplus. An adrenal tumor is the less common form. One adrenal gland grows a tumor that makes cortisol on its own, and the opposite gland shrinks. A third pattern is iatrogenic, from steroid tablets, steroid drops, or a steroid injection given for months for an itch or a joint. The dog looks Cushingoid because the hormone came from the medicine cabinet.

Cortisol drives thirst, hunger, panting, a pot belly from weak abdominal muscles and an enlarged liver, and thin skin that loses hair on the trunk while the head and the feet stay furred. It also invites skin infections, urinary infections, high blood pressure, and clots in unlucky dogs. Diabetes can appear alongside it. A high alkaline phosphatase on a senior panel is a clue and it cannot confirm the disease by itself.

Which dogs get

Most patients are middle-aged or older. Poodles, Dachshunds, terrier breeds, Beagles, Boxers, Boston Terriers, and German Shepherd Dogs show up frequently. Any breed can develop a pituitary microadenoma with age. Adrenal tumors are the minority and can occur in the same age group. Dogs who have taken daily prednisone, or frequent steroid injections, for allergic skin or immune disease can develop the iatrogenic form, and their own adrenals may be suppressed. A young dog with the same signs is unusual enough that the clinician should look hard for an adrenal tumor or for steroids in the history, including topical products an owner forgot to mention.

Signs owners notice

The classic quartet is drinking more, urinating more, eating more, and panting at rest. The belly drops. The skin thins, bruises easily, and may develop firm mineral plaques called calcinosis cutis, which is fairly specific. Hair falls out along the flanks in a roughly symmetric pattern. The dog may seem less athletic and more interested in food than in a walk. Recurrent ear or skin infections and a urinary tract infection in dilute urine are common companions. A dull, circling, or inappetent dog who used to be ravenous can, rarely, have a pituitary macroadenoma big enough to press on the brain. That change in a dog who was stable on treatment is a reason to call.

  • Empty water bowl, accidents in the house, and a dog who asks for food right after dinner.
  • Panting while asleep, and a pot belly that showed up over months.
  • Thin skin, flank hair loss, and blackheads or firm gritty plaques.
  • Repeated skin or bladder infections in an older dog.
  • Sudden weakness, a swollen painful belly, or breathing trouble, which can mean a complication such as a clot, pancreatitis, or a gallbladder problem.

How a veterinarian

Cushing's disease, hyperadrenocorticism, is confirmed with endocrine tests, then imaging. A high alkaline phosphatase alone does not confirm it. A urine cortisol-to-creatinine ratio is a screen: a normal result makes Cushing's unlikely, and a high result still needs a confirming test. The low-dose dexamethasone suppression test and the ACTH stimulation test are the tests that confirm it. Ultrasound looks at the adrenal glands after those results, to separate a pituitary drive from an adrenal tumor.

The low-dose dexamethasone suppression test takes several blood samples over the day and catches more dogs with the disease. Patterns of suppression can also support a pituitary source. The ACTH stimulation test is slightly less sensitive, especially for adrenal tumors, and it is the test clinics reuse later to monitor trilostane. A dog with a classic history and a normal ACTH stimulation result may still need the dexamethasone test. Urine culture is wise because dilute urine hides infection that a dipstick misses. Abdominal ultrasound showing two plump adrenals fits pituitary disease. One large adrenal and a small opposite gland fits an adrenal tumor. Chest imaging is added when an adrenal tumor might have spread. None of this should start while a dog is acutely sick from something else, because stress raises cortisol and can fake the pattern.

Treatment categories

Pituitary-dependent Cushing's is usually managed with a drug that lowers cortisol production. Trilostane is the drug used to lower cortisol. The veterinarian sets the dose from follow-up ACTH stimulation tests. An adrenal tumor can be removed by adrenalectomy when imaging shows it has not spread. Medical control is the alternative when the family declines surgery.

Trilostane is given on the schedule the clinic writes, often with food, and it is never adjusted from how thirsty the dog seems on a single afternoon. The ACTH stimulation test after a change is what says the cortisol is in range. Mitotane is an older drug that damages cortisol-producing layers of the adrenal and is still used in some practices, with its own monitoring. Adrenalectomy can be curative for an adrenal tumor that has not spread, and it is a specialty operation with a real anesthetic risk. Pituitary surgery and radiation exist at a few centers for selected dogs and are uncommon next to medical control. Iatrogenic Cushing's is treated by tapering the steroid that caused it, on a schedule slow enough to let the dog's own adrenals wake up. Stopping a long steroid course abruptly can precipitate an Addisonian crisis. That taper belongs to the veterinarian who prescribed the drug.

Home care

Give trilostane exactly as labeled, with the meal the clinic specified, and do not double a dose after a missed one unless you were told how to handle a miss. Keep a log of water intake if you can, plus appetite and panting, and bring it to rechecks. Watch for weakness, vomiting, diarrhea, or a dog who suddenly refuses food. Those can mean the cortisol dropped too low, which is the opposite problem and needs the drug held and a same-day exam. Protect thin skin from clippers and from long sun on a sparsely haired back. Treat concurrent infections, because a dog with Cushing's heals poorly and gets urinary infections easily. Keep the ACTH stimulation appointments. Skipping them and refilling the drug on autopilot is how dogs become over-controlled.

When to See

Collapse, vomiting that will not stop, sudden labored breathing, a painful distended abdomen, severe weakness, or a dog on trilostane who cannot stand should be seen immediately. Low cortisol from too much medication can look like an Addisonian crisis and is treated as an emergency. A pulmonary clot can cause sudden breathing distress in a dog with uncontrolled Cushing's. Pancreatitis and gallbladder mucoceles are associated illnesses that also present with a sick, painful belly. The routine pot belly and thirst can wait for a scheduled endocrine visit. A dog who is suddenly dull and circling needs a same-day neurologic look for a larger pituitary mass.

What to ask

Pituitary-dependent Cushing's disease, the common form, is controlled for the long term. A cure would require removing the pituitary source, which most dogs never undergo. Trilostane lowers cortisol and has to be checked with ACTH stimulation tests. It does not remove the pituitary tumor. An adrenal tumor is the uncommon form, and adrenalectomy can be curative in a dog whose imaging shows one adrenal mass and no spread.

The repeating Cushing's cost is the ACTH stimulation test after each trilostane change, for the rest of the dog's life. An abdominal ultrasound that distinguishes a pituitary driver from an adrenal tumor is an earlier, separate line item. Ask for both figures before the first dose. Adrenalectomy is a specialty surgical quote on top of the medical plan.

Key Questions

Is a high liver enzyme enough to diagnose Cushing's?

No. Alkaline phosphatase rises for many reasons in older dogs, including dental disease, steroid exposure, and other liver problems. A urine cortisol-to-creatinine ratio can make Cushing's unlikely when it is normal. Confirmation still needs a low-dose dexamethasone suppression test or an ACTH stimulation test, read against the history. Treating from the enzyme alone puts a dog on trilostane without a diagnosis.

How is trilostane monitored?

With ACTH stimulation tests on the schedule the clinic sets after each dose change, plus electrolytes and a look at thirst, appetite, and energy. The test timing relative to the capsule matters, and the clinic will tell you when to give the morning dose on test day. Owners should not change the amount because the water bowl looks better for three days. Over-suppression can become an Addisonian crisis.

Can surgery cure Cushing's disease?

Adrenalectomy can cure an adrenal tumor that has not spread. Pituitary-dependent disease, the common form, is usually controlled with medication. Removing or irradiating the pituitary tumor is available only in limited centers and is a different operation from adrenalectomy. Iatrogenic disease can resolve if the outside steroid is tapered safely. The form of the disease decides which of those sentences applies, and ultrasound plus endocrine tests are how the form is named.
