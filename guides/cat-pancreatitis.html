Pancreatitis in Cats

Causes, symptoms, and treatment of feline pancreatitis. Covers diagnosis challenges, dietary management, and connection to triaditis.

Cats hide pancreatitis behind a quiet appetite

Pancreatitis in cats rarely looks like the dramatic vomiting disease people know from dogs. The common feline picture is a cat who eats less, hides, loses a little weight, and seems "off." Some cats vomit. Many do not. Some hunch, resent being picked up, or choose a cool floor because the abdomen hurts. Fever may be absent. Because the signs are vague, the disease is both over-suspected in every nauseated cat and missed in cats who only skipped breakfast. The pancreas sits near the bile duct and the small intestine, and inflammation often travels with liver and intestinal disease. Clinicians call that cluster triaditis. A cat can have pancreatitis, cholangitis, and inflammatory bowel disease in the same month, which is why a single anti-nausea tablet and a shrug is a thin plan.

Acute flares can dehydrate a cat quickly. Chronic, low-grade pancreatitis can simmer for months with waxing appetite. Diabetes mellitus sometimes appears alongside pancreatic disease, and a smaller number of cats later show signs of exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, greasy stool and weight loss despite eating. Those sequels are reasons to recheck, and they are separate tests. The first job is to recognize that a cat who is merely "not himself" may have a painful pancreas.

Tests that fit feline disease

The physical exam plus a feline pancreatic lipase blood test, often reported as Spec fPL or a similar pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity assay, is the combination clinicians use when the signs fit. A normal abdominal ultrasound does not clear the cat. Ultrasound misses a substantial share of feline pancreatitis, even in skilled hands, though it remains useful for finding thickened bowel, liver changes, and extra fluid. A complete blood count and a chemistry panel look for dehydration, bilirubin, liver-enzyme rises, and the low potassium or calcium changes that change how the cat is supported. They do not replace the lipase test. Radiographs help when the question is a foreign body or constipation pretending to be a pancreatic flare. Snap-style lipase screens exist. Your veterinarian will say whether a positive screen should be confirmed with the quantitative lab test before a long treatment plan is built on it.

Anorexia is the emergency

A cat who has not eaten for a day, especially an overweight cat, can start down the road to hepatic lipidosis while everyone waits for the pancreas to "calm down." Vomiting that will not stop, collapse, jaundice, or belly pain that makes the cat hide should be seen the same day. Do not fast a cat for several days because a dog handout said to rest the pancreas. Do not give a dog's pain medicine or a human anti-inflammatory. Cats are poisoned by several of those drugs, and pain still needs treatment that a veterinarian selects.

Support, food, and the decisions that stay veterinary

Treatment is supportive and specific to the cat in the clinic. Fluids correct dehydration. Anti-nausea medicine and pain relief are chosen and dosed by the veterinarian. Nutrition is part of the treatment, early. If the cat will eat a palatable food, offer it. If the cat will not, the veterinarian may place a feeding tube so an overweight cat keeps receiving calories and stays clear of hepatic lipidosis. The diet itself is chosen around appetite and around any concurrent intestinal or liver disease. A strict low-fat plan copied from canine pancreatitis can be the wrong priority in a cat who simply needs calories and a food that does not provoke concurrent IBD. There is no single prescription that fits every triaditis patient. Antibiotics are used when the veterinarian suspects a bacterial cholangitis or another infection, and they are a deliberate choice. Steroids sometimes enter the plan when intestinal inflammation is documented and infection has been considered. Those are internist decisions. Home care after discharge is the medication schedule you were given, a quiet room, and a scale. Call back if the cat skips another full day of food or if vomiting returns.

Recurrence happens. Keep the discharge instructions and the lipase result. A second flare should not restart the entire mystery if the record already names the pancreas. At the same time, a new episode of vomiting still deserves a check for foreign bodies, kidney disease, and hyperthyroidism in an older cat, because those problems share the same dull appetite. Ask whether liver values and a thyroid level are due at this visit.

What you can watch at home

  • Grams or ounces eaten at each meal, written down, so "a little off" becomes a number.
  • Vomiting, hairballs included, with the time and whether food was in it.
  • Water intake and litter-box clumps, which also speak to diabetes and kidney disease.
  • A hunched posture, a reluctance to jump, or a cry when lifted.
  • Yellow gums or yellow skin in the ears, which means the liver or red cells need a same-day look.

Bring the notes. A cat who ate nothing on Tuesday and Wednesday is a different patient from a cat who ate half portions and kept them down. If a feeding tube was placed, the clinic will teach you tube care, the food they want used, and the volume. Follow that sheet. Do not substitute a different food or a larger bolus because the cat seemed hungry. Tube complications are a call to the clinic, urgently if the cat coughs, the site smells, or the tube moves.

Costs of a flare

An outpatient visit with a lipase test and bloodwork is the smaller bill. Hospitalization with fluids, nausea control, and a feeding decision is the larger one, and a feeding tube adds a procedure. Ultrasound, if it is done to look at liver and bowel at the same time, sits in between. Ask the hospital for a daily estimate so a three-day stay does not arrive as a surprise. Chronic cats may repeat lipase tests and internist visits over a year. Those repeats are how the plan stays tied to this pancreas.

Questions about feline pancreatitis

Why did the ultrasound look normal?

Ultrasound misses many cases of feline pancreatitis. A cat can still have the disease when the image is unremarkable, which is why a feline pancreatic lipase test and the exam carry so much weight. The scan remains useful for thickened intestine, liver changes, and other causes of vomiting. Ask how the lipase result and the image will be read together, especially if triaditis is on the list.

Should I withhold food to rest the pancreas?

Withholding food for days is a dangerous plan in a cat, particularly an overweight one, because hepatic lipidosis can follow anorexia. Offer food the veterinarian has accepted, and call if a full day passes with nothing eaten. Pain relief, nausea control, and sometimes a feeding tube are the tools clinics use so the cat can take calories in. A low-fat rule borrowed from dogs is something to ask about, and it may be set aside when this cat's main need is to eat.

Do vomiting and diarrhea have to be present?

Many cats with pancreatitis barely vomit. A reduced appetite, hiding, and a hunched posture can be the whole story. Diarrhea points toward concurrent intestinal disease more than it proves a pancreatic flare. If your cat is quiet and off food, that is enough reason for an exam and, when the veterinarian agrees, a pancreatic lipase test. Jaundice, collapse, or another day of zero intake should not wait for a classic vomit.
