Best Cat Litter

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Top cat litter boxes reviewed including self-cleaning, covered, top-entry, and jumbo options. Covers multi-cat setups and odor control.

Open, covered, top-entry, and sifting boxes

A litter box is a container a cat will step into, turn around in, and dig. The shape changes who can do that comfortably. An open pan is a low tray with no roof. Cats can see out, seniors can step in, and you can see the urine the moment you walk past. The tradeoff is scatter on the floor and more odor in the room. A covered box adds a hood and a door. It holds some of the smell and the kicked litter, and some cats hate the small doorway or the ammonia that builds inside. In a multi-cat home a hood also gives one cat a place to block another cat in.

A top-entry box makes the cat jump over a wall or through a hole in the lid. Tracking drops, and dogs and toddlers have a harder time raiding it. Arthritic cats, overweight cats, kittens, and cats recovering from surgery often refuse the jump, then pee on the bath mat. A sifting box uses nested pans or a grate so clean litter falls through when you rotate or shake the unit. It helps only if someone actually sifts on a schedule. Cats who dislike a grate underfoot will perch on the rim or abandon it. A jumbo open pan, or a storage tote with smooth edges, is often the honest answer for a large cat who cannot turn around in a standard tray.

How many boxes, and how big

Provide one more box than the number of cats. Two cats need three boxes. One cat still does better with two, placed in different quiet rooms, so a closed door or a noisy appliance never leaves the household with zero options. In a house with stairs, put a box on each floor the cat uses. Keep every box away from the food and water. Cats treat eating and eliminating as separate jobs, and a box beside the bowl is a box many of them will walk past.

Size is a length problem. Measure the cat from the nose to the base of the tail and choose a box about 1.5 times that measurement, with enough width to turn around without standing on the rim. A Maine Coon, a Ragdoll, or any long adult often outgrows the box sold as "large" at a grocery store. High sides help a digger who flings litter. They hurt a cat who cannot lift a stiff leg. If you have to choose, buy the longer box and cut down one side for entry. A box that is merely deep, and too short for the cat's body, still gets used on the rug next to it.

Unscented clumping litter

Unscented clumping litter is the default most adult cats accept. The clay or the plant material forms a scoopable clump when it gets wet, which is what makes daily cleaning possible. Scented litter is a common reason a cat who used the box for years suddenly picks the bath mat. Perfume that smells "fresh" to a person smells like a threat in a small bathroom to a cat. Depth should let the cat dig a small hole. Around two inches suits many cats. Long-haired cats sometimes prefer a shallower bed so pellets do not tangle in feathering. Crystal litter, wood pellets, and paper bedding are reasonable experiments for a cat who already likes them. Switching a reluctant cat onto a heavily scented or a sharply textured product during a house move is how accidents start.

Change products slowly if you must change them at all. Mix a little of the new litter into the old over a week, and keep one box on the familiar product until the cat is using the new one on purpose. A cat who tracks clay through the house needs a mat and a shorter path to a hard floor. The fix is often the location and the mat. It is rarely a stronger perfume.

Seniors and high sides

Older cats get osteoarthritis in the spine and the hips, and a high wall or a covered door becomes a barrier they will not argue with. They squat beside the box, or they dribble on the way in. Joint pain belongs on the list when an older cat struggles to step into a high-sided box. Give that cat a low-entry pan with a higher back wall to catch the spray, or cut a doorway in a storage tote and leave the cut edge smooth. A step or a ramp can rescue a top-entry unit you already own, and only if the cat is willing to use it. Place the senior box in the room the cat actually sleeps in, because a long walk to the basement is the part that fails first.

Kittens need the opposite of a fortress: a low rim they can scramble over, and a box small enough that they can still find the litter. As they grow, enlarge the box before they start perching on the edge. After surgery, ask the clinic whether a low open pan should replace a hood for the recovery week. A cat in a cone often cannot fit through a covered door at all.

Multi-cat odor

Odor in a multi-cat home is a math problem. More cats, fewer boxes, and a scoop every third day will smell no matter which brand is in the pan. Scoop every box daily, and twice daily if you have several cats or a small apartment. Dump clumping litter and wash the box with mild soap and hot water on a regular cycle, sooner if a smell remains after scooping. Skip ammonia and any cleaner that smells like urine. Rinse until the soap is gone, because residue is a reason cats stand on the rim. An enzymatic cleaner on the floor and the baseboard handles old accidents outside the box. Covered hoods trap ammonia. The room can smell fine while the cat refuses to put his head in the cave.

Spread the boxes through the home so one cat cannot guard all of them. A hallway, a quiet bathroom, and a spare bedroom beat three boxes lined up in the same laundry corner. Ventilation matters more than a plug-in fragrance. An exhaust fan or an open window near the box dilutes odor without adding a scent the cat will reject. If a new cat has moved in and the resident starts missing the box, add a box and a separate feeding station before you buy a stronger litter. Conflict looks like a dirty-box problem from the doorway.

Self-cleaning caveats

A self-cleaning box rakes, rotates, or sifts on a timer or a weight sensor after the cat leaves. It can be a real help for a person who cannot bend to scoop, and it fails in predictable ways. Leave it unplugged and used as an ordinary pan until the cat enters it calmly. Noise and a moving rake scare some cats permanently. Weight sensors may ignore a kitten or a very small adult, so waste sits until you notice. They can also start a cycle when one cat leaves if a second cat has already stepped in. Look for a delay you can lengthen and a stop that halts the rake when weight returns.

Most mechanisms require clumping clay. Wood pellets, crystals, and some plant litters jam the rake or the globe. The waste drawer still has to be emptied, and three cats can fill it in a couple of days, so the "never scoop" claim dies in a real household. A power cut or a full-bin error returns you to a dirty box overnight. Keep a spare open pan in the house. One robot does not satisfy the count rule. Two cats still need three acceptable toileting spots, and a single globe is one spot. Cats with arthritis often cannot use a round unit with a high step. The machine also does not inspect the urine. Blood, straining, and frequent tiny pees are medical events. A clean drawer does not treat them.

A setup that survives the first month

Put the box down before the cat comes home, in a quiet spot with two exits so the cat is never cornered. Fill it with unscented clumping litter at a diggable depth. Scoop daily into a bag that leaves the house, and wash the pan on a schedule you can keep. If you are adding a box style the cat has never seen, leave the old pan in place until the new one is being used for both urine and stool. Move a box a few feet at a time if you must relocate it. A sudden move to the garage is a classic reason for a rug accident. Children and dogs should be kept from staring at the cat while she eliminates. Privacy is part of the equipment.

Track what you find. A clump the usual size, a stool that is formed, and a cat who covers and leaves are the normal record. A sudden change in clump size, a pile of urine next to a clean box, or a cat crying in the pan is a medical note. Buying a different gadget that afternoon skips the exam.

When blood or straining is an emergency

Choosing a box does not diagnose why a cat left it. When use changes, the useful medical test is a urinalysis. The urine is checked for blood, crystals, and concentration, and the bladder is felt. A culture is added if infection is still a question. Pain in the joints is considered in an older cat who cannot step into a high-sided box.

If the urine is normal, the useful change is the setup: a box the cat can enter, one more box than the number of cats, and daily scooping. If the urine is abnormal, the veterinarian treats cystitis, infection, or stones, and chooses the medicine. A blocked cat needs emergency care the same hour. Comparing boxes can wait until the urine has been cleared.

The medical problem owners actually meet in the box is feline lower urinary tract disease. Urethral obstruction in a male cat is an emergency and is relieved by catheterization and fluids. Feline idiopathic cystitis often flares again with stress. Stones can be removed or dissolved only when the mineral type is known. Buying a different box does not treat a blockage.

When to See

Blood in the box, straining with little or no urine, crying in the pan, vomiting, or hiding after repeated trips to the box is an emergency, especially in a male cat. A urethral blockage can become life-threatening within a day. A new litter box cannot unblock a urethra, and waiting to see if a covered pan "feels safer" wastes the hours that matter. Go to an emergency clinic, and mention how long it has been since a normal stream of urine. A cat who is bright, eating, and simply leaving occasional stool beside a dirty hood can be helped with cleaning and a second open pan while you book a regular visit if the urine itself looks normal.

What to budget

Separate the product price from the emergency urinary price. A box and the litter are a small purchase. Unblocking a male cat is hospitalization, and a perineal urethrostomy is a surgery quote if obstruction keeps returning. Ask an emergency clinic what an uncomplicated unblock costs before you need it at night. Blood in the box or straining with little urine is that emergency.

For the equipment itself, compare an open pan, a hood, and a self-cleaning unit by what you will still have to do each day. The cheap pan, unscented clumping litter, and a scoop are the starting setup that solves most households. A robot costs more up front and still needs liners or a drawer, clumping litter it can tolerate, and a spare pan. Ask whether the return window is long enough to learn if your cat will enter it. Do not let a payment plan for a machine delay an emergency visit when there is blood or straining.

Key Questions

How many litter boxes do two cats need?

Three. The working rule is one more box than the number of cats, placed in separate quiet spots away from food. Two boxes side by side in one bathroom count as one territory if a cat can stand in the doorway and guard both. A single self-cleaning unit does not bring a two-cat home up to that count. Add open pans until you are at the number, then see whether the robot is a bonus or a thing the cats ignore.

What size box fits a large cat?

Measure nose to the base of the tail and aim for a box about 1.5 times that length, wide enough to turn in. Many store "large" boxes are still short for a Maine Coon or a heavy domestic longhair. A storage tote with a low door cut into one side is a practical jumbo pan if the plastic is sturdy and the cut edge is smooth. High walls are useful behind a sprayer and a problem at the entrance for a stiff senior.

Why did my cat stop using a clean box?

Pain, a frightening event, a new scent, or a box that is hard to enter can all look the same on the rug. Have a veterinarian check the urine before you replace the furniture. Blood, crystals, and a bladder that is hard and painful are medical findings. A normal urinalysis sends you back to the setup: an open low-entry box, unscented clumping litter, one extra box, and a scoop every day. Joint pain in a senior is fixed by a lower rim, and sometimes by pain control the clinic prescribes, which a new gadget does not supply.
