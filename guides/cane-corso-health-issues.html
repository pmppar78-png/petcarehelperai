Cane Corso Health Issues

Common health problems in Cane Corsos including hip dysplasia, bloat, cherry eye. Prevention, symptoms to watch for, and treatment options.

Cane Corsos are predisposed to several health conditions including hip dysplasia, bloat, cherry eye. Understanding these risks allows you to screen early, prevent where possible, and catch problems before they become emergencies.

Health Awareness: Cane Corsos show elevated breed-level risk for hip dysplasia, bloat, cherry eye. Your vet can build a screening interval around those specific conditions; early-stage findings almost always give you more treatment options than advanced-stage ones.

Knowledge of breed-level risks helps you prioritize, but individual monitoring drives the most effective care decisions.. Cane Corsos sit in the large-size category, shed at a light level, and carry documented risk for hip dysplasia and bloat — those three factors drive most of the daily-care decisions.

Cherry eye on this mastiff-type head is a red gland that popped out. A scratch from a bush looks different and needs a different visit.

Big bones make people ignore a limp until the limp becomes the lifestyle. In a Cane Corso, dysplastic hips announce themselves with a sway, trouble climbing into the car, and a slow push up from a hard floor. I want hip evaluations on the parents and films on the dog once age 1 arrives and again by age 2, when the pictures say more than a puppy pose. Eyes fail in ways visible from across the room. Cherry eye is a red gland at the medial canthus. Entropion rolls a lid inward so lashes scrape, and the dog squints and waters. Ectropion rolls the lid outward and leaves conjunctiva showing. Book the eye evaluation early. A guardian who hurts in the face gets sharper about having that face touched.

Bloat refuses to wait for office hours. The picture is restlessness, unproductive retching, and an abdomen that swells and feels drum-tight. The stomach may be twisting, and the drive is immediate. Idiopathic epilepsy is the seizure problem that remains when the workup finds no cause. During a seizure the dog needs space, a dim quiet room afterward, and a note of how long it lasted. Heart disease, including cardiomyopathy in some lines, shows up as weakness, coughing, fainting, or a rhythm someone notices only because they listened. I have recorded 88 for a lean female and 120 for a heavy male on charts in the same month, and neither figure shielded the heart or the stomach.

Years often fall between 9 and 12. Bloat, cardiomyopathy, and ruined hips are what make the low end real. A steady, socialized guardian can still be fragile inside. Registry height for males is 25 to 27.5 inches. Registry height for females is 23.5 to 26. I trust those heights more than kitchen rumors about mass. AKC recognition arrived in 2010, recent enough that eyes, hearts, and hips still vary by line. Merck's pages on bloat and cardiomyopathy are the references worth knowing before midnight needs them.

After a seizure, stairs and other pets wait until the mind returns. A lid rolled inward hurts on every blink. A prolapsed gland dries and swells if it stays out. Films from 1 to 2 years become the baseline when the dog is 9 and reluctant to rise, inside that 9 to 12 year stretch. The scale numbers I have actually handled, 88 and 120, go in the margin. Care plans follow joints, stomach, and heart. A red mass at the eye or a fade on a brief walk outranks gloss in the order I use.
