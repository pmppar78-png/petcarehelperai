Common Aquarium Fish Diseases

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Guide to common fish diseases including ich, fin rot, swim bladder disease, dropsy, and velvet. Covers identification, treatment, and prevention.

Read the water before you name a disease

Aquarium fish live inside the thing that is hurting them when a tank is out of balance. Ammonia and nitrite should read 0 ppm on a liquid test kit in a cycled aquarium. Any number above zero is burning gill tissue, and a fish with damaged gills flashes, clamps its fins, breathes fast, and dies looking as if a parasite arrived overnight. Nitrate is the less toxic end of that same cycle. It climbs between water changes and should stay low for the species you keep, with sensitive fish given more frequent changes than a hardy community tank. A paper strip is a rough screen. The decisions in a sick tank deserve a liquid kit that can show ammonia and nitrite separately.

Temperature swings, a new filter that was rinsed until the bacteria were gone, a dead fish left in the corner, and overfeeding all push ammonia up. Fix that number before a medication goes into the display. Many shop bottles also damage the bacteria that keep ammonia at 0 ppm, so a treated tank can crash a day after it looked better. Write down ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, temperature, and, for a marine tank, salinity. An aquatic veterinarian, where you can find one, will ask for those figures before talking about a drug.

Ich, the white-spot parasite

Ichthyophthirius multifiliis is the freshwater parasite behind the salt-grain white spots. Marine white-spot disease is a different organism, Cryptocaryon irritans, and it needs a marine plan. On the fish you usually see flashing against gravel or decor, clamped fins, and small raised white dots on the body and fins. Gill ich can kill while the skin still looks almost clean, because the fish is losing oxygen. The feeding stage sits under the mucus and is shielded from many treatments. It later drops off, encysts in the tank, and releases free-swimming young. That free-swimming stage is the vulnerable one, which is why a single overnight dose so often fails and the plan has to cover several days as the water temperature speeds or slows the cycle.

New fish are the usual source. Stress from a cold night, a long drive, or an ammonia spike lets a low-level infection explode. Scaleless fish, including many loaches and catfish, tolerate salt and some dyes poorly, so a product chosen for a tetra tank can be the wrong product for them. Invertebrates such as shrimp and snails are easy to kill with ich medications aimed at fish. An aquatic veterinarian or a treatment made for aquariums, used in a separate hospital tank when the label requires it, is safer than pouring an unknown bottle into a planted display. Heat is sometimes part of a freshwater ich plan because it shortens the parasite's cycle, and it is a fish-tank adjustment a knowledgeable person sets with a thermometer.

Velvet looks like dust, and it moves faster

Freshwater velvet is caused by Piscinoodinium. Marine velvet is Amyloodinium. Both can look like a gold or rusty film on the body, as if the fish had been dusted, along with clamped fins, flashing, and gills working hard. The spots of ich are larger and more discrete. Velvet is easy to miss until the fish is exhausted. The organism uses light, so an aquatic veterinarian may include a darkened hospital tank in the plan along with a drug chosen for that species. Copper is one of the drugs used against velvet and some other external parasites. The margin between a useful level and a lethal level is narrow, and copper lingers in silicone, substrate, and equipment. Do not guess a copper dose, and do not put copper where shrimp, snails, or other invertebrates live.

Bettas, gouramis, and other fish that already breathe at the surface are easy to misread, because they always visit the top. Watch for a fish that hangs there longer than usual, refuses food, or shows that dusty sheen under a flashlight. Quarantine catches velvet before it reaches a display that would be painful to tear down.

Columnaris is a bacterium that gets called fungus

Flavobacterium columnare causes columnaris. Owners often see white or gray patches on the mouth, a pale saddle across the back, or fins that rot back toward the body with a white edge. It can look fluffy, which is why fungus medications get used and the fish keeps declining. Livebearers and recently shipped fish show it often, and warm, crowded, dirty water makes some strains move fast. A fish can go from a small mouth lesion to death in a day or two. The useful first steps are a water test, a partial water change if ammonia or nitrite is above 0 ppm, and a separate tank so the sick fish can be handled without dosing the whole display. An antibiotic chosen by an aquatic veterinarian fits this organism. A fungus bottle does not. Columnaris can also follow an injury from tankmates, so separate the fish that is being chewed.

Dropsy is a sign, and the pinecone outline is late

Raised scales that stand out from the body, the so-called pinecone look, mean fluid has built up under the skin. That picture is called dropsy in the hobby, and it is a sign of internal failure. Kidney damage, a systemic bacterial infection, and some viruses can all produce it. The eyes may bulge at the same time. By the time the scales lift, many fish do not recover, and a shop remedy marketed for dropsy rarely reverses organ failure. Still test the water, because ammonia and nitrite at anything other than 0 ppm will mimic and worsen almost every illness on this page. Isolate the fish so you can watch it and so tankmates are spared whatever is contagious. An aquatic veterinarian can sometimes tap fluid or choose an antibiotic when the fish is valuable and still eating. A fish that is already on its side, with scales fully raised, is usually past the point where another bottle helps. Human safety matters if you suspect fish mycobacteriosis, which can cause chronic wasting and internal granules: wear gloves, and keep tank water out of cuts. That bacterium is a reason to retire equipment, and it is a different problem from a pinecone fish in a tank that just spiked ammonia.

Fin rot and buoyancy problems

Fin rot is usually a bacterial infection of damaged fin tissue. Nipped fins, ammonia burns, and a filter intake that snags a long fin are typical starts. The edge looks ragged, milky, or bloody, and it shortens toward the body if the water stays poor. Correcting ammonia and nitrite to 0 ppm, plus clean water changes, stops many early cases. A fin that continues to disappear needs a veterinarian's antibiotic choice. Regrowth takes weeks and will follow the remaining fin rays.

A fish that floats, sinks, or swims upside down has a swim-bladder problem only in the sense that buoyancy is wrong. Fancy goldfish are prone because of body shape. Other fish become buoyant from infection, constipation, a chilled tank, or a tumor. Test ammonia and nitrite first and warm a tropical tank back to the species' normal range with a thermometer, gradually. A pea for a constipated goldfish is a household story that sometimes helps a fish who is still active and recently overfed. It does not treat an infection, and it delays care when the fish is clamped and refusing food. Buoyancy that lasts more than a day belongs in a conversation with an aquatic veterinarian.

Quarantine is the cheap part

A separate quarantine tank, cycled before you need it, with its own net, siphon, and thermometer, is how ich, velvet, and columnaris stay out of a display. Four weeks is a common observation period for new fish, long enough for white spots, dusty skin, frayed mouths, and weight loss to show. Feed and watch during that month. Do not share a net with the display. A sponge filter from the main tank can seed the quarantine filter with the bacteria that hold ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm, as long as that sponge comes from a tank with no known parasite. Plants, snails, and used decor can carry parasites too, so they get their own holding period. When a disease does appear, the quarantine tank is also the hospital tank, which keeps invertebrate-killing drugs away from the display and lets you watch one fish eat.

  • Keep a liquid test kit in the house and log ammonia and nitrite, both of which should be 0 ppm.
  • Quarantine new fish for several weeks in a cycled tank with separate equipment.
  • Match any medication to the organism and to the species, and keep copper away from invertebrates.
  • Treat a pinecone fish as a gravely ill animal with internal fluid, and expect a guarded outcome.
  • Replace evaporated water with dechlorinated water so salinity and hardness do not creep up by accident.

When the tank cannot wait

Several fish gasping at the surface, a sudden ammonia or nitrite reading above 0 ppm, or a fish listing on its side is an emergency for that aquarium. Do a partial water change with temperature-matched dechlorinated water, get aeration going, and contact an aquatic veterinarian if one practices in your area. A single dead fish in an otherwise stable, tested tank is a removal and a water test, then observation. A string of deaths in a few days needs a diagnosis, because mycobacteriosis and some parasites are reasons to stop adding fish.

What treatment actually changes

There is no universal fish medicine. Ich and velvet are parasites with life cycles. Columnaris is a bacterium. Dropsy is a fluid sign with more than one cause. Fin rot follows damaged tissue and dirty water. Swim-bladder signs need a cause. The person who helps you may be an aquatic veterinarian or, in many towns, a genuinely experienced fish specialist working from a microscope scrape of skin and gill mucus. That scrape is how the white-spot parasite, velvet, and flukes are actually seen. Pouring three different bottles into one tank in a weekend usually wrecks the filter and still misses the organism. If you cannot find an aquatic veterinarian, say so to an exotics clinic and ask whether they can review photos, water numbers, and a scrape. Do not borrow powders, drops, or lamps from a different kind of pet. Fish need stable, appropriate water, the right temperature for the species, and a drug only when the organism calls for one.

What this costs

A liquid test kit and a small cycled quarantine tank usually cost less than replacing a display of adult fish. Clinic fees vary by city. An exotic consult plus a skin scrape is often a few hundred dollars where an aquatic veterinarian exists. A course of a named aquarium treatment for early ich in a hospital tank is often less than that visit, and it fails when the life cycle is cut short or the water is still toxic. Breaking down a tank for mycobacteriosis, replacing porous decor, and leaving the hobby tank empty for a period your veterinarian sets can cost more than any bottle. Get the estimate before you dose an antibiotic into a large display, because the drug, the water changes, and a possible filter crash are all part of the bill.

Questions from people with a sick tank

Can white-spot ich be cleared?

Freshwater ich can be cleared when treatment covers the free-swimming stage and starts before the gills are destroyed. The fish that are already hanging at the surface may still die even if the parasite is later gone from the tank. Velvet and columnaris need their own plans, and a pinecone fish is showing internal fluid buildup that a white-spot remedy does not reverse. Name the problem from the water tests and, when you can get one, a skin scrape.

Why do new fish have to sit in another tank?

Quarantine is how you watch a new fish for ich, velvet, columnaris, and weight loss before those problems reach animals you cannot easily catch. Several weeks, often about four, in a cycled tank with its own equipment is a practical window. Shared nets defeat the point. The quarantine tank also becomes the hospital tank later, which keeps harsh drugs out of a display that holds shrimp or a mature filter.

What should ammonia and nitrite read?

In a cycled aquarium both ammonia and nitrite should read 0 ppm. A number above zero is an emergency for the gills even when you also see spots or ragged fins. Change part of the water with dechlorinated water at the same temperature, stop feeding for a short period, and find what broke the cycle. Do not add fish, and do not pile medications on top of a toxic reading.
