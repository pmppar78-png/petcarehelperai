Feline Immunodeficiency Virus (FIV)

Understanding FIV in cats. Covers transmission, testing, stages, treatment, living with an FIV-positive cat, and multi-cat household management.

FIV spreads through bites, and the test looks for antibody

Feline immunodeficiency virus is a lentivirus, a cousin of HIV in the way it is built, and it infects cats. It does not infect people, and it does not infect dogs. The important transmission route is a deep bite that drives saliva into tissue. Intact male cats who fight outdoors carry the highest risk. Casual contact in a stable home, shared bowls, and mutual grooming rarely pass FIV, which is the practical difference families need when they hear FIV and feline leukemia in the same breath. FeLV moves more readily through saliva during friendly contact. FIV generally requires a wound. A veterinarian can explain both results if a new cat was screened with a combo test, because the two viruses are confirmed and managed differently.

Screening is an antibody test, often a point-of-care ELISA. A positive result in a healthy adult should be confirmed, because no test is perfect and a previous FIV vaccine, now discontinued in the United States and Canada, can leave antibodies that still read positive years later. Kittens can carry maternal antibody from an FIV-positive mother and test positive while they are still young. Retesting after that antibody should have faded, commonly once the kitten is around six months old, separates a borrowed result from a true infection. PCR is used in selected cases when the history and the antibody tests disagree. A cat who was just bitten may not have made antibody yet, so a negative test the week of a fight can be repeated on the schedule the veterinarian sets.

What infection looks like over years

After the bite, some cats have a short illness with fever and enlarged lymph nodes, then a long quiet period. That quiet can last for years. The virus slowly damages immune function, and the cat becomes prone to mouth inflammation, chronic upper respiratory disease, skin infections, poor wound healing, and weight loss. Certain cancers are more likely than in an uninfected cat, though many FIV-positive cats never develop them. The virus itself is managed by keeping the cat healthy. There is no standard antiviral course that clears FIV the way a veterinarian-directed plan may address some other feline viruses. Claims for unproven immune boosters should be brought to the veterinarian before you spend money on them.

  • A bite wound, especially on an intact outdoor male, is the classic exposure.
  • Stomatitis, chronic nasal congestion, and recurring skin infections can be the first illness you notice years later.
  • Weight loss and a dull coat in a cat who has been positive for a long time deserve a fresh exam.
  • A fever that will not settle needs a workup for the infection the damaged immune system is allowing.

When an FIV-positive cat should be seen quickly

Difficulty breathing, a mouth so sore the cat stops eating, a deep abscess, pale gums, or collapse are urgent in any cat and more so when immune function is already under strain. Do not start a steroid because a previous cat with mouth pain received one. Steroids and other immune-suppressing drugs can be part of a stomatitis plan, and they can also make an infection worse. That choice belongs to the veterinarian who can see this cat.

Living with a positive cat

The American Association of Feline Practitioners' retrovirus guidance supports a normal, careful life for many FIV-positive cats. Keep them indoors so they do not bite other cats and so they do not collect new infections. Spay or neuter reduces roaming and fighting. In a home where cats do not fight, an FIV-positive cat and an FIV-negative cat often live together for years without transmission. Introduce them the way you would any new adult, with supervision, and separate them at the first real bite. Food bowls and litter boxes can be shared in a peaceful group. A household with daily fights needs a different arrangement, because the bite is the risk.

Wellness visits catch dental disease, parasites, and weight change early. The veterinarian decides which vaccines still make sense, weighing the benefit against the cat's indoor life and any reaction history. Parasite control still matters, because fleas, worms, and ear mites are extra burdens. Raw diets add bacterial risk to a cat whose immune system may be less forgiving. If the cat becomes ill, go sooner than you might with a young healthy housemate. Tell the clinic the cat is FIV-positive when you book, so they can plan dentistry, surgery, and any samples with that history in view.

Testing every new cat before the carrier comes inside is the cleanest prevention. If your cat comes home from a fight with a wound, have the wound treated and ask when to repeat the FIV test. There is no benefit to isolating a long-term, friendly FIV-positive cat from human family members. Standard hygiene around litter boxes is enough.

Costs of testing and monitoring

The screening test itself is one of the smaller fees in feline medicine, and confirming a positive result costs another visit or another lab. The expensive years, if they come, are the dental procedures for stomatitis, the workups for recurring infections, and the bloodwork that tracks an older cat. Many positive cats need nothing beyond ordinary wellness care for a long time. Ask the clinic to price a confirmatory plan before you make a rehoming decision off a single in-house line on a snap test.

Questions about FIV

Can an FIV-positive cat live with other cats?

Yes, when the cats do not fight. FIV is passed mainly by deep bites, so a stable indoor group that grooms and shares bowls is a low-risk household. Separate cats who inflict real puncture wounds, and keep the positive cat indoors so neighborhood fights stop. This is a different pattern from feline leukemia virus, which spreads more easily through saliva during close friendly contact. Ask your veterinarian to interpret each cat's own test.

Why would a kitten test positive and later test negative?

Kittens can carry antibodies from the mother for months. A positive antibody test at eight weeks can fade by the time the kitten is about six months old. The veterinarian will time a retest and will also consider whether an old FIV vaccine, no longer sold in the United States and Canada, could explain a positive adult. A single positive line on the day of adoption is a reason to confirm, and it is a weak reason to delay care.

Is there a cure I should be buying online?

No approved drug clears feline immunodeficiency virus. Supportive care, dentistry, prompt treatment of infections, and an indoor life are the plan with evidence behind it. Bring any immune supplement or unlicensed antiviral to your veterinarian before you give it. Steroids, in particular, should be used only when that veterinarian has decided the mouth or the skin needs them and has accounted for the infection risk.
