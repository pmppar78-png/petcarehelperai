Reptile Lighting and Heating Guide

Comprehensive guide to reptile lighting and heating including UVB, basking spots, ceramic heaters, thermostats, and species-specific requirements.

Heat is a gradient, and light is a tool

Reptiles regulate body temperature by moving. A cage with one temperature everywhere forces a choice between too warm and too cool, and the animal cannot make it. Build a warm end and a cool end, measure both with a digital probe at animal level, and confirm the basking surface with an infrared thermometer. A stick-on strip thermometer on the glass reads the glass. The animal does not live on the glass. Every heat source plugs into a thermostat. Lamps and heat mats that run full time overheat hides and cause burns, especially when a rock or a mat has no controller.

UVB is ultraviolet light that lets many reptiles produce vitamin D3 and use calcium. It is separate from the brightness you see and separate from heat. A lamp can be hot and still provide little UVB, and a UVB tube can be warm to the touch while the basking zone is still too cool. Plan heat and UVB as two jobs. Glass and most plastic filter UVB, so a bulb sitting outside a glass tank lid may be doing almost nothing. Mesh screens block a portion of the output. Distance matters: a tube that is correct at 12 inches can be too strong at 6 inches or too weak at 18. Follow the fixture guidance for that bulb and measure if you can.

Diurnal lizards

Day-active lizards such as bearded dragons, uromastyx, and many tegus evolved under strong sun. They need a bright day, a defined basking spot, and UVB that reaches the animal in that spot. A long T5-style UVB tube over part of the enclosure, paired with a basking lamp at one end, is a common setup. The cool end must stay genuinely cooler so the lizard can leave the heat. Desert species often bask on a surface that feels hot to your hand and then retreat. Tropical day-active lizards may want slightly less intense heat and more humidity, so the species sheet from a reptile veterinarian beats a generic "lizard bulb" from the aisle.

Replace UVB tubes on the schedule the manufacturer prints, often well before the tube looks dark. Output falls while the tube still glows. Mercury vapor lamps combine heat and UVB and run hot. They suit some large enclosures and they are a poor fit for small cages because the animal cannot get far enough away. Night should be dark. If the room falls below the species' safe range, use a ceramic heat emitter or a deep heat projector on a thermostat, and skip colored night-lights that wash the cage in red or blue all night. A photoperiod of roughly a long day in summer and a slightly shorter day in winter suits many diurnal lizards, adjusted for the species and for whether you are breeding.

Signs of a lighting problem include a lizard who stays glued to the warm lamp, a lizard who never basks, faded activity, poor appetite, and soft or swollen bones over time. Metabolic bone disease is a medical emergency in the making, and the fix is veterinary care plus a corrected gradient. Do not stack random calcium supplements on a broken setup and hope. Bring temperatures, a photo of the lamps, and the bulb's age to the appointment.

Snakes

Most pet snakes are kept with a thermal gradient and a thermostat, and their UVB needs are usually lower than a desert lizard's. A ball python, a corn snake, or a kingsnake still needs a warm hide and a cool hide. Belly heat from a regulated mat or a radiant panel, plus a warm-side air temperature the species actually uses, beats a single blazing bulb that leaves the rest of the cage cold. Large snakes such as boas need the same idea at a bigger scale, with the warm end measured where the snake rests. A reading at the ceiling misleads you.

Low-level UVB is optional for many crepuscular and nocturnal snakes and is more useful for day-active species such as some garter snakes and indigo snakes. If you add a UVB tube, choose a lower output than you would for a bearded dragon and give shade, including a hide the snake can use all day. Albino and other light-sensitive morphs can be stressed by intense light. Watch for refusal to eat, constant hiding on the cool side, or roaming that never settles. Those behaviors can be temperature, light, or illness. A reptile veterinarian can separate them. Heat rocks that have no reliable thermostat have burned many snakes and should stay out of the cage.

Crepuscular geckos and other low-light species

Leopard geckos, crested geckos, and African fat-tailed geckos are active at dawn, dusk, or night. They still use a warm place and a cool place. A leopard gecko typically needs a warm hide measured in the upper 80s to low 90s Fahrenheit on the floor of that hide, with a cooler side in the 70s, checked against current species guidance from your veterinarian. Under-tank heat or a radiant source must be thermostatted and must not cook the belly. Crested geckos and similar New Caledonian species often do well at ordinary room temperatures in the low-to-mid 70s Fahrenheit, with a small warm area only if the room runs cold. A desert basking lamp over a crested gecko overheats an animal who cannot escape it.

UVB for these geckos, when used, is the low-output kind, shaded by leaves or cork, and placed so the gecko can avoid it. It is a supplement to a correct diet and a correct gradient. It does not replace either one. Bright, hot, high-UVB fixtures sold for bearded dragons are the wrong tool here. Provide a humid hide for shedding even when the air humidity stays moderate, especially for leopard geckos. Mist systems and heat lamps must not leave the cage dripping and cold, or steaming and airless. Ventilation and measurement keep both extremes away.

Write down the species, the warm-side number, the cool-side number, the bulb model, and the date you installed it. Recheck after you change a lamp, a substrate, or a lid, because each of those changes the readings. If you keep more than one species, refuse the idea that one fixture serves all of them. A diurnal lizard, a ball python, and a crested gecko are three heating plans.

  • Give every reptile a warm end, a cool end, and a way to hide at both.
  • Control every heat source with a thermostat and verify with a probe and a temperature gun.
  • Provide strong UVB and a real basking zone for diurnal desert lizards.
  • Keep snake gradients steady and use only low, shaded UVB if you use it at all.
  • Use gentle heat and low-output UVB for crepuscular geckos, with a humid hide.
  • Replace UVB tubes on the manufacturer schedule even when they still look bright.
  • Remember that glass blocks UVB and mesh reduces it.
  • Bring your temperatures and bulb dates to a reptile veterinarian if appetite or bones change.

Recheck after you change a lid

A new mesh top, a deeper substrate, or a moved basking branch changes the numbers. Measure the warm end and the cool end again the same day, and confirm the UVB distance for a diurnal lizard. Crepuscular geckos still need a warm hide they can leave and a humid hide for sheds. Snakes need the gradient where the body rests. Write the bulb install date on the fixture. Replacement stays on a calendar even while the tube still glows.

Questions about reptile light and heat

Do all reptiles need the same UVB lamp?

No. Diurnal lizards such as bearded dragons generally need strong UVB over a basking zone. Many snakes need a heat gradient first, with only low UVB if any. Crepuscular geckos need shaded, low-output UVB at most, plus a warm hide they can leave.

Why does the warm side and the cool side both matter?

Reptiles move to control body temperature. A single hot temperature removes that choice. Measure both ends at animal level, put every heater on a thermostat, and recheck after you change bulbs, lids, or substrate.

How often should a UVB tube be replaced?

Follow the manufacturer interval, commonly well under a year for many tubes, even if the lamp still glows. Output drops before the light looks dim. Distance, mesh, and glass all change how much UVB reaches the animal.
