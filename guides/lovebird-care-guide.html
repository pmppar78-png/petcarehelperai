Lovebird Care Guide

Complete lovebird care guide covering species differences, housing, diet, social needs, health concerns, and whether lovebirds need a partner.

Housing and Enclosure

Lovebirds are small parrots with a loud opinion and a serious bite for their size. The cage should measure at least 24 inches wide, 24 inches deep, and 24 inches high for one bird, with bar spacing around 1/2 inch. Wider is better because they fly in short bursts and need a horizontal path. Place the cage where the household lives, out of the kitchen. Nonstick cookware fumes, strong cleaners, and smoke are lethal to parrots, and lovebirds are no exception because they are small. A play stand outside the cage gives you somewhere to move the bird during cleaning.

Social housing is the hard decision. A single lovebird bonds tightly to a person and can become noisy and nippy if that person is gone all day. A pair often bonds to each other and may ignore or even bite the owner. Two lovebirds need a cage large enough that one can escape the other, and they still need supervision because lovebirds fight. Peach-faced lovebirds are the common pet. Fischer's and masked lovebirds have similar care and should not be mixed casually if you care about hybridization and aggression. Night should be about 10 to 12 quiet hours. Lovebirds can night-fright. A dim corner and a stable cover routine help.

Avoid avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, onion, and salty or sugary people food. Millet and a sunflower seed work as training rewards because lovebirds will do a lot for a seed and very little for a lecture. Fresh water daily. Some birds dunk pellets and foul the bowl by noon, so a second water dish away from the food helps. Cuttlebone calcium plus the pellet covers the usual need. Egg-binding risk rises in a hen that is encouraged to nest, so skip nest boxes and dark huts unless you are deliberately breeding with a plan and an avian veterinarian.

Foraging is part of feeding. A paper cup with holes, a kabob of vegetables, or a pellet hidden in a shred toy slows the meal and reduces the screaming that comes from finishing breakfast in thirty seconds. Pull fresh food before it sours. A lovebird will keep chewing a fermenting pepper and pay for it the next morning.

Health and Veterinary

Psittacine beak and feather disease, polyomavirus, respiratory infections, and egg binding are the serious lovebird problems. PBFD and polyomavirus are viral and are reasons to buy from a source that tests and quarantines, and to keep a new bird away from any other parrots for weeks. Respiratory disease shows as tail bobbing, sneezing, and a wet cere. Egg binding is a hen on the cage floor, straining, fluffed, and weak. That is an emergency for an avian veterinarian the same hour. Fatty tumors and liver disease follow seed diets and a cage that never allows flight.

Choose an avian veterinarian before you need the emergency line. Annual exams, a weight in grams, and a look at the droppings catch disease while the bird is still eating. Lovebirds are stoic. A bird that is "a little quiet" may be very sick. Fluffed feathers, a change in droppings, or a sudden refusal to come out for a seed are enough reason to call. Keep a travel carrier that is already assembled. Hunting for a box while a hen is straining wastes the window.

Nails and beaks usually wear with wood and chew toys. If a beak overgrows, ask why. Liver disease and PBFD both change beak growth, and a cosmetic trim without an exam misses the cause. Wash hands, and keep other pets from "playing" with a bird that cannot win that game.

Behavior You Should Expect

  • Plan for noise at dawn and dusk. Lovebirds scream in short bursts that carry through an apartment.
  • Offer several hours out of the cage in a bird-proof room, with ceiling fans off and toilets closed.
  • Teach step-up with a seed, and stop the session when the bird starts to lunge.
  • Give chewing outlets every week so baseboards and picture frames survive.
  • Decide early whether you want a human-bonded single bird or a pair that may prefer each other.

A settled lovebird preens, chews, and flies to a shoulder or a cage top for a seed. A bird that sits fluffed, sleeps all day, or strains on the floor needs an avian veterinarian immediately. With a pellet diet, a 24-inch cage, and a real day-night schedule, peach-faced lovebirds often live into their teens.

Living With the Noise and the Bite

Lovebirds announce themselves. The morning call, the call when you leave, and the call when a bag rustles are normal flock behavior, and they carry through an apartment wall. Neighbors hear a sun-conure volume less often, and they still hear a lovebird. If that sound is a problem, this is the wrong parrot, even though the bird is small. Give the cage a corner with a view of the room and a routine that includes several hours out, and the calls often shorten because the bird can see the flock.

Bites happen when a lovebird is overstimulated, jealous, or defending a shoulder. Step-up from a perch or a hand held low, and put the bird down at the first lunge. A shoulder that is allowed to become a nest becomes a bite zone, especially in the spring when hormones rise. Remove hut-like toys, keep the night long, and skip petting the back and tail. Those cues tell a hen to lay. An avian veterinarian should see a hen that strains or sits fluffed on the cage floor the same day.

Chew budget is real. A lovebird will stripe a door frame during a single unsupervised afternoon. The legal chew is a pile of paper, palm, and soft wood inside the cage and on the stand. Rotate it weekly. Wash the dishes daily because this species dunks pellets until the water is paste. A gram scale on the counter tells you whether a fussy week is a diet conversion or an illness. Bring that number to the clinic. It is the most useful note you can hand an avian veterinarian.

Quick Answers

Are lovebirds good for first-time bird owners?

They can be, if you want a small parrot and can accept the noise, the bite, and the need for a 24-inch cage plus daily out-of-cage time. A pair may bond to each other and remain nippy with people.

The cage and stand are the first hardware bills and commonly reach a few hundred dollars. Add an avian wellness exam, which 2026 exotic-practice roundups price at roughly $95 to $185 before bloodwork (findanexoticvet.com). AAV recommends finding that veterinarian before a reproductive emergency, and it does not quote a fee (aav.org). Pellets and toys continue monthly.

Care and Breeding

Lovebirds need a cage at least 24"x24"x24" with bar spacing of 1/2 inch.

Provide multiple perches, swings, and a variety of toys including bells, shreddable items, and foraging opportunities.

Lovebirds are active and playful — they need several hours of out-of-cage time daily for exercise and socialization.

Offer small amounts of seeds as treats.

Provide cuttlebone for calcium.

Avoid avocado, chocolate, caffeine, and high-fat or salty human foods.

Common lovebird health issues include Psittacine Beak and Feather Disease, polyomavirus, respiratory infections, and egg binding in females.

Watch for fluffed feathers, nasal discharge, changes in droppings, or lethargy as warning signs.

Let the bird in front of you, not an idealized version, drive the pace of any new routine.
