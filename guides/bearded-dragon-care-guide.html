Complete Care Guide

Everything about bearded dragon care including enclosure setup, UVB lighting, diet, brumation, common health issues, and handling tips.

Housing and Enclosure

An adult bearded dragon needs a long, bright enclosure. The working minimum most reptile veterinarians now describe is a 4-foot by 2-foot by 2-foot cage, about 120 gallons. A 40-gallon breeder is a grow-out for a juvenile and becomes cramped once the dragon is full size. Height matters less than length, because the animal needs a hot end and a cool end with space between them. Front-opening PVC or a solid-top melamine cage holds heat better than a screen aquarium with a lamp sitting on mesh.

Build a basking surface, measured with an infrared thermometer on the stone or branch, around 100 to 110 F for an adult that is using it. The cool side can sit near 80 to 85 F. Nighttime can fall toward the mid 60s if the dragon has had a full day of heat and UVB. A thermostat must control whatever heat source can fail on, especially a ceramic heater or a deep heat projector. The basking bulb can be on a dimmer or a lamp stand you can raise. If the surface reads 120 F, lift the lamp. A dragon that never gapes and never leaves the cool side may be under the lamp too little, or the UVB may be missing and the animal is avoiding a harsh spotlight.

UVB is mandatory. Use a linear T5 UVB tube that spans about half to two-thirds of the cage, over the basking zone, and follow the bulb's distance chart for a desert species. Mesh tops block a large share of UVB, so the distance is measured from the bulb to the dragon's back, through the screen if there is one. Replace the tube on the schedule printed on the box, often every 12 months, even though it still lights the room. Coil bulbs screwed into a dome are a weak substitute for a tube on an adult dragon. Provide a hide on the cool side and a flat basking platform. Substrate can be tile, newspaper, or a diggable soil mix once feeding is tidy. Loose sand alone plus a messy eater is how impactions happen.

Juveniles stay in a smaller but still graded cage with the same temperatures and a closer, carefully distanced UVB. They should be able to sit under the light and also escape it. House dragons singly. Two animals in one "large" tank is a dominance fight that ends with missing toes and a thin subordinate.

Diet and Nutrition

Bearded dragons shift from insect-heavy juveniles to greens-heavy adults. A young dragon needs roughly 70 percent insects and 30 percent plants, offered in small meals most days. An adult flips that balance: a salad most days and insects a few times a week. Dubia roaches, crickets, and black soldier fly larvae are the working insects. Gut-load them. Dust insects with calcium at most feedings, and use a multivitamin less often, on a schedule you can remember. If the UVB tube is correct and fresh, calcium without heavy D3 is the usual dust, which avoids piling vitamin D on top of a strong lamp. Confirm the plan with the reptile veterinarian who sees your dragon.

Salads should be dark greens the dragon can actually digest. Collard greens, mustard greens, turnip greens, and dandelion greens, plus grated squash or prickly pear, beat a bowl of iceberg. Spinach and beet greens are occasional leaves because of oxalates, and they should not crowd out the collards. Fruit is a rare topper. Adults get fat on bananas and quiet cages. Insects stay smaller than the space between the eyes. A pinky mouse is an old feeding habit that adds fat and little else a well-run salad and roach plan is missing.

Water is a dish plus a drip over the nose if your dragon ignores standing water. Many will drink in the bath. A weekly shallow soak in lukewarm water helps some dragons shed and pass waste, and it is optional if the animal drinks and sheds cleanly. Replace salad that wilts the same day. A sour bowl of greens is how you grow bacteria in a hot cage.

Health and Veterinary

Metabolic bone disease is the classic failure: soft jaw, swollen limbs, tremors, and a dragon that drags its pelvis. It follows weak or expired UVB, a lamp placed too far away, or insects that were never dusted. It needs a reptile veterinarian. A new bulb alone, after the bones have already bent, leaves the weakness in place. Impaction shows as straining, a full belly, and no stool, often after the cool side was actually cold or the dragon ate substrate with its bugs. Respiratory infections bring bubbles at the nose, an elevated head, and open-mouth breathing that is different from a brief bask-gape. Parasites are common in pet-store dragons. Bring a fresh fecal sample to the first exam. Yellow fungus disease is a serious skin infection that needs diagnosis, isolation, and a real treatment plan from a clinic. Do not scrape and oil a yellow crust at home.

Brumation is a winter slowdown some adult dragons enter. They eat less, hide, and sleep more while the day length shortens. A juvenile that does this, or an adult that loses weight, needs a veterinarian before you "let them sleep it off." Parasites and early illness look like brumation. Weigh the dragon weekly any time appetite drops. Adenovirus exists in the captive population and is a reason to quarantine new dragons and to ask about testing if you keep more than one.

Handle with two hands under the chest and pelvis, supporting the feet. A dragon that turns black and flattens is uncomfortable. Short sessions on a lap are plenty. Wash hands afterward. Annual exams catch the mouth rot and the early tail rot that owners describe as "just shed" for months.

What a Good Day Looks Like

  • Lights and UVB come on in the morning and run about 12 hours, then go off so the night is actually dark.
  • The basking surface is checked with an infrared thermometer a few times a month and after any lamp change.
  • Salad goes in fresh. Insects are dusted and removed if they are still hopping at lights-out.
  • Stool is formed and regular. A puddle of undigested insect parts means the basking site is too cool.
  • The dragon uses both ends of the cage. Living only under the lamp or only in the hide means the gradient is off.

Beard color changes with mood and heat. A dark beard during a bask can be normal. A dark, limp dragon on the cool floor in the middle of the day needs a temperature check and, if the temperatures are right, a clinic. These lizards are interactive and long-lived when the tube, the basking number, and the salad are boringly consistent.

Quick Answers

Are bearded dragons good for beginner reptile owners?

They can be, for an owner who will buy a 4-foot enclosure, a linear UVB tube, and a thermostat, and who will feed salads plus dusted insects. They are a poor fit for a spare aquarium and a coil bulb.

The adult cage, UVB hood, basking lamp, and thermostat are a larger startup than a gecko kit, often several hundred dollars before the animal. Zoo Med's lighting sheet says to date a UVB tube and replace it one year later (zoomed.com). A 22-inch ReptiSun 5.0 T5 HO tube was listed at about $22 to $26 in October 2026 retailer listings. A 2026 exotic-clinic roundup puts a reptile wellness exam at about $95 to $185 before tests (findanexoticvet.com).
