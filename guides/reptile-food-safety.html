Safe and Dangerous

Feeding safety guide for bearded dragons, leopard geckos, and other pet reptiles. Covers toxic insects, unsafe vegetables, and calcium requirements.

Feeder insects and the wild ones to leave outside

Captive insect-eating reptiles do best on feeders you raised or bought from a feeder supplier: crickets, dubia roaches, black soldier fly larvae, hornworms from a pet supplier, and similar bugs that have been kept on clean food. Gut-load those insects with vegetables and a commercial gut-load for a day before you offer them. Dusting with calcium, and less often with a multivitamin, is common for insect-eating lizards. The exact rhythm depends on the species, the age, and whether UVB is correct. A reptile veterinarian should set that rhythm. This page is a safety guide, and it will not pretend one dusting calendar fits a bearded dragon and a gecko.

Wild insects are a different risk. Lawn and garden bugs may carry pesticide, slug bait, or herbicide on their bodies. A single sprayed cricket can poison a small lizard. Fireflies and lightning bugs are especially dangerous. The compounds that make them glow have killed bearded dragons and other lizards after one or a few insects. Teach children that fireflies stay outside and never go into a reptile cage as a "treat." Ladybugs, wild spiders, and bugs from a porch light are poor feeders for the same pesticide reason, and some wild prey bite or irritates the mouth.

Avocado, dog food, and other kitchen mistakes

Avocado should stay out of reptile diets. Persin, the compound in avocado flesh, skin, and pit, is associated with illness in a range of animals, and the pit is a choking and impaction hazard on top of that. Guacamole also brings onion, garlic, and salt. There is no benefit that justifies the experiment. The same rule covers chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, and dough made with xylitol or with yeast. Party leftovers have no place in a reptile cage.

Dog food and cat food fail as a staple for reptiles. They are built for mammals, with fat, protein balance, vitamin levels, and mineral levels aimed at dogs or cats. Fed as the main diet they are linked with obesity, gout-like problems in some species, and nutritional disease. A tiny taste stolen from a bowl is a reason to secure the bowl. It should never become "the easy dinner" for a monitor, a tegu, or a lizard who begs. If a reptile needs a prepared diet, use a food made for that species and ask a reptile veterinarian to review it.

Iceberg lettuce is mostly water. Spinach, beet greens, and other high-oxalate leaves can bind calcium when they crowd out the rest of the salad. Fruit is a treat for many lizards and a larger share only for species that actually eat fruit, such as some geckos whose commercial diet is already balanced. The salad bowl stays limited to greens you can name. Rhubarb and uncooked beans are poor ideas. When you are unsure, skip the item and offer a known safe green such as collard, dandelion, or squash, matched to the species.

Species patterns that change the menu

Bearded dragons and similar omnivores need a mix that shifts with age: more insects when they are growing, more vegetables as adults, plus correct UVB so calcium is usable. A salad of one color every day is a narrow diet. Uromastyx are largely plant eaters and do poorly on a bowl of fatty insects. Crested geckos usually eat a commercial complete diet made for them, with insects as a supplement. Replacing that diet with baby food and honey is an old habit that misses nutrients.

Snakes who eat whole prey need prey that is the right size, fully thawed, and warmed, from a trusted source. A prey item wider than the snake's widest part is a regurgitation risk. Live adult rodents can chew a snake to the bone and should not be left in the cage. Frozen-thawed prey that smells sour should be discarded. Vitamin powder sprinkled on a mouse is a side note for a snake. Calcium dust on crickets is a core habit for many insect-eating lizards. If a snake refuses food, check temperatures and hiding places before you invent a new diet. Prolonged refusal needs a reptile veterinarian, especially if the snake is losing weight.

Turtles and tortoises vary so widely that a box turtle and a sulcata should never share a feeding sheet. Aquatic turtles need a balanced pellet plus vegetables appropriate to the species, and they need water clean enough to eat in. High-protein mammal diets dumped into a slider tank pollute the water and distort growth. Tortoises need fiber and calcium from plants and correct UVB. Dog food has been used historically and it is a known bad staple for them.

When a meal becomes an emergency

Call a reptile veterinarian or a poison hotline if a reptile eats a firefly, avocado, a pesticide-coated bug, rodenticides, or a large amount of dog or cat food seasoned with onion, garlic, or xylitol. Bring the package or a photo of the insect. Skip home vomiting for a reptile unless the veterinarian on the phone directs it. Reptiles hide illness. Lethargy, a black beard in a bearded dragon, regurgitation, bloating, or seizures after a new food are same-day problems.

Impaction is the other feeding emergency. Substrate such as gravel, sand in the wrong species, or bark eaten along with a meal can block the gut, especially if temperatures are too cool for digestion. House insects in a smooth container so bedding stays out of the cage. Offer water and correct the temperatures while you travel if the clinic tells you that is safe. A reptile who has not defecated, who strains, or whose belly looks suddenly full needs an exam and often an image. Kitchen experiments are easy to prevent. A written list on the cage, readable by every person in the house, prevents the helpful guest who drops in a firefly or a slice of avocado toast.

A cage card the whole household can follow

Write the species name, the allowed greens, the feeder insects you buy, and a short forbidden list on a card taped near the cage. Include fireflies, avocado, dog food, cat food, and wild bugs in that forbidden list. Guests mean well and they still drop fruit from a plate. A card stops the conversation before the bite. If you foster or pet-sit, photograph the card and send it to the sitter with the veterinarian's number. New keepers often copy a video diet that was filmed for a different species. The card is how you refuse that copy without arguing about the video.

  • Feed supplier-raised insects and gut-load them. Leave fireflies and sprayed yard bugs outside.
  • Keep avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, and xylitol away from every reptile.
  • Do not use dog food or cat food as the staple diet.
  • Match vegetables and commercial diets to the species, and ask a reptile veterinarian about calcium dusting.
  • Thaw snake prey fully, size it to the snake, and supervise anything still moving.
  • Remove leftover crickets so they do not chew the reptile.
  • Call a veterinarian after firefly, pesticide, or avocado ingestion. Skip home vomiting unless you are told to do it.

Questions about foods that harm reptiles

Why are fireflies dangerous to pet lizards?

Fireflies contain defensive chemicals that have killed bearded dragons and other lizards after very small meals. Keep them out of the feeder cup and out of the room where reptiles live, and call a reptile veterinarian if one was eaten.

Can reptiles eat avocado or dog food?

Avocado, including the pit and skin, should stay off the menu because of persin and choking risk. Dog food and cat food are built for mammals and fail as a staple for lizards, snakes, and tortoises. Use a species-appropriate diet reviewed by a reptile veterinarian.

Are wild-caught insects safe if I rinse them?

Rinsing does not remove pesticide, herbicide, or slug-bait residue reliably. Buy feeders or raise them. Yard bugs, porch-light moths, and fireflies belong outside. If a sprayed insect was eaten, call a veterinarian and describe the product if you know it.
