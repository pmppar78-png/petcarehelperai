Rat Poison and Rodenticide Toxicity in Dogs

This Rat Poison and Rodenticide Toxicity in Dogs page leaves out dosing numbers.

Types of rat poison and their effects on dogs. Covers symptoms by poison type, emergency treatment, and pet-safe rodent control alternatives.

Any rodenticide is an emergency

If a dog eats rat poison, or you even suspect it, treat the moment as an emergency. Call a veterinarian or an emergency clinic, and call a poison hotline such as the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 888-426-4435 or the Pet Poison Helpline at 855-764-7661. Bring the package with you, including any loose pellets you can safely scoop into a jar. The active ingredient changes the treatment. A blue or green block, a throw pack, and a professional bait station can all hide different chemicals. The brand name on the front is less useful than the active ingredient on the back.

Do not induce vomiting unless a veterinarian or a poison hotline tells you to. Some dogs are already too sedated, some baits are mixed with other hazards, and a brachycephalic dog can inhale vomit. Do not offer salt, oil, milk, or food as a home remedy. If the dog is seizuring, collapsing, or having trouble breathing, go straight to the clinic and have someone else call ahead. Keep other pets away from the same bait. A second dog often ate what the first dog scattered.

Note the time, the dog's weight as you know it, and how much of the bait is missing. "The whole block was there this morning" is useful. A photo of the label, taken before you leave, still helps if the package gets left on the counter. This page explains the categories. It leaves dosing math to the toxicologist on the phone.

Anticoagulant rodenticides

Anticoagulant baits interfere with the way the body reuses vitamin K, which the liver needs to build clotting factors. Warfarin is the older, shorter-acting example. Modern products often use longer-acting ingredients such as brodifacoum, bromadiolone, difethialone, or diphacinone. The dog can look completely normal for several days. Then bleeding starts: nosebleeds, bruising, blood in stool or urine, pale gums, a cough from bleeding into the chest, or a swollen joint. Waiting for that bleeding is how dogs die at home. The quiet gap is the trap.

Because the delay is long, a dog who ate an anticoagulant yesterday and is playing today still needs the clinic. Treatment, when the ingredient is confirmed, often includes vitamin K1 prescribed by the veterinarian for a stretch of time, plus blood tests that show whether clotting has recovered. The vitamin sold for human use at the pharmacy is a different product and the wrong plan. Rechecks matter, because stopping the prescription early lets bleeding return with the long-acting baits. If the dog is already bleeding, the hospital may need to give plasma or other support. That is a reason to move early, while the dog is still stable enough to travel calmly.

Bromethalin

Bromethalin is a different class. It does not act through vitamin K, so vitamin K1 does not treat it. Bromethalin affects the nervous system and can cause fluid buildup in the brain and spinal cord. Signs can include muscle tremors, hyperexcitability, hind-end weakness, paralysis, and seizures. Some dogs show signs within hours, and some show them later, depending on the amount and the individual. A dog who looks "just a little wobbly" after access to a bromethalin bait needs a clinic now, because the window for decontamination is early and the neurologic injury is hard to reverse once it is roaring.

Labels matter here more than with any other bait, because a household that keeps vitamin K "just in case" will waste the night on the wrong drug if the ingredient was bromethalin. Read the word bromethalin out loud to the hotline. Do not assume a green block is an anticoagulant. Manufacturers change formulas, and store brands change suppliers. The package in your hand beats a memory of what rat poison "usually" is.

Cholecalciferol, or vitamin D3 baits

Cholecalciferol rodenticides are vitamin D3 in a dose meant to kill a rodent. In a dog they drive calcium up in the blood. The result is kidney injury, mineralization of soft tissues, heart rhythm problems, vomiting, increased thirst, and increased urine. Signs may be delayed for a day or more. Vitamin K does not fix this class either. Care is intensive and time-sensitive: the clinic decontaminates if the timing is right, then works to control calcium and protect kidneys. Bloodwork is repeated because the calcium can rise after the dog has gone home too soon.

Other vitamin D sources can look similar, including some psoriasis creams and supplements, but a bait block in the yard is a clue. Bring the wrapper so the team does not have to guess among the three big classes. Combination products exist. If the label lists more than one active ingredient, read each one. A misidentified class leads to the wrong monitoring. Anticoagulant dogs need clotting tests. Bromethalin dogs need neurologic monitoring. Cholecalciferol dogs need calcium and kidney values. One protocol cannot cover all three.

Secondary exposure and how to make the house less attractive to rodents

A dog who eats a rodent that died from poison may receive a smaller exposure, and the risk depends on the ingredient and how much of the carcass was eaten. Call anyway, especially if you know anticoagulant or cholecalciferol was used in the area. Do not let the dog finish the carcass "because it is natural." Bag it and ask the hotline whether the carcass should come with you. Cats who hunt and dogs who raid garages are the usual stories.

Prevention is sanitation and exclusion. Store bird seed, pet food, and livestock feed in metal cans. Close gaps in the foundation a rodent uses, after you are sure no animal is trapped inside a wall. Snap traps and electric traps can injure pets and children, so they belong inside locked boxes or in rooms pets cannot enter, checked daily. Discuss exclusion with a pest company and tell them you will not accept loose bait. If bait is truly necessary in a severe infestation, professional locked stations, placed where a dog cannot drag them, and a written active ingredient left on your fridge, are the minimum. A bowl of pellets behind the water heater is how the emergency starts.

  • Call a veterinarian or a poison hotline at the moment you suspect bait was eaten.
  • Bring the package so the active ingredient is known.
  • Do not induce vomiting unless a veterinarian or hotline tells you to.
  • Anticoagulants delay bleeding for days. Veterinary vitamin K1 is the treatment path, and waiting for visible bleeding lets dogs deteriorate.
  • Bromethalin is neurologic, and vitamin K does not treat it.
  • Cholecalciferol raises calcium and injures kidneys, and vitamin K does not treat it either.
  • A dog who eats a poisoned rodent still warrants a phone call.
  • Store feed in metal, seal entry holes, and refuse loose bait in pet areas.

Questions about rat poison and dogs

What are the main types of rat poison?

Anticoagulants such as brodifacoum and bromadiolone interfere with vitamin K and cause delayed bleeding. Bromethalin affects the nervous system and can cause tremors, weakness, and seizures. Cholecalciferol is vitamin D3 and raises blood calcium, damaging kidneys. The package tells you which one you are facing.

Should I make my dog vomit after eating rat poison?

Only if a veterinarian or a poison hotline says so for this dog and this bait. Do not use salt, oil, or other home methods. If the dog is seizuring, collapsed, or struggling to breathe, go to the clinic and call ahead.

Why bring the box if the dog seems fine?

Anticoagulant bleeding can wait several days, and the other two classes need different care. Vitamin K1 helps only the anticoagulant group. Bromethalin and cholecalciferol require other plans. The active ingredient on the label is what the clinic needs before the dog looks sick.
