Complete Care Guide

Everything you need to know about cockatiel care including diet, housing, health, training, and common behavior patterns for these popular pet birds.

Housing and Enclosure

Cockatiels are slender parrots that need a cage they can glide inside. Use at least 24 inches wide, 18 inches deep, and 24 inches high, and add width whenever the room allows. Bar spacing of 1/2 to 5/8 inch keeps a head from slipping through. Horizontal bars give a climber a path. Put the cage in the living area, clear of the kitchen, because nonstick fumes and aerosol sprays kill cockatiels the same way they kill other parrots. A window with a direct afternoon blast overheats a cage. A dark back hallway makes a social bird scream.

Perches of different widths, a swing, and a flat corner perch for sleeping protect feet. Sandpaper sleeves as the only perch abrade skin. Toys can be simple: bells, paper, foot toys, and a shreddable block. Cockatiels are less destructive than conures and still get bored. Night frights are the housing problem unique to this species. A cockatiel can thrash at a sudden noise, break a blood feather, and bleed. A small night light, a cage away from a slamming door, and a consistent bedtime reduce the crashes. Cover the cage if the room stays bright, and give about 10 to 12 hours of quiet. People with asthma or feather allergies should spend time around an adult cockatiel before deciding, because the powder down is real.

Out-of-cage time belongs in a room with the ceiling fan off, the toilet lid down, and other pets elsewhere. A full-spectrum bird lamp on a timer helps a dim apartment keep a normal day. That light is about the clock and seeing the bird. Calcium still comes from the diet and a cuttlebone. Indoor cockatiels get little UVB through window glass, so ask an avian veterinarian about a bird-specific full-spectrum lamp and blood calcium (Association of Avian Veterinarians; Merck Veterinary Manual, Nutrition in Pet Birds).

Diet and Nutrition

A cockatiel pellet should make up most of the bowl, with vegetables every day and a seed mix kept small. Leafy greens, carrot, broccoli, and cooked sweet potato are practical. Millet spray is the training jackpot and a poor staple. A bird that lives on millet and a sunflower-heavy mix gets fat and heads toward fatty liver disease. Fruit can be a thin slice a few times a week. Cockatiels are suspicious of new foods. Offer a vegetable beside the pellet for many mornings before you decide the bird "hates greens."

Fresh water daily, and wash the dish because cockatiels drop seed hulls into it. A cuttlebone or mineral block supplies calcium, which matters for hens. Egg binding shows up in cockatiel hens that are bonded, stimulated by a nest-like hut, and short on calcium. Remove huts and boxes unless you are breeding on purpose with an avian veterinarian involved. Avoid avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, and salty chips. Table food as a habit also teaches a cockatiel to scream at every meal you eat.

Weigh the bird in grams every week or two during any diet change. Cockatiels are light, and a hunger strike during a pellet conversion can become dangerous quickly. If weight falls, slow the conversion and call an avian veterinarian if the bird fluffs or sits low. Pellets are the goal because they are formulated. Seed can remain a measured garnish once the pellet is accepted.

Health and Veterinary

Respiratory infections, fatty liver disease, psittacosis, and egg binding are the cockatiel cases avian clinics describe most often. Tail bobbing, a click when breathing, nasal discharge, and a bird that sleeps on the cage floor are urgent. Psittacosis, also called chlamydiosis, can pass to people and is a reason to use an avian veterinarian and to mention the illness to your own doctor if you become sick after a bird does. Seed as the whole diet, in a cage where the bird cannot fly, is how fatty liver starts in cockatiels. Egg binding is a fluffed hen, straining, and weak. Go the same day.

Night-fright injuries include broken blood feathers and split wings. A bleeding feather may need to be pulled by someone trained, with styptic or cornstarch ready, and a vet visit if the bird is shocky. Do not dig at a broken feather in a dark room with a thrashing bird and no plan. Annual exams, a gram weight, and a look at the vent and the nares are the routine. Cockatiels live into their twenties when the diet is pellets and the nights are calm, so the veterinary relationship is a long one.

Find that avian veterinarian before the first night fright. A general practice may not stock the right tests. Bring the bird in a small carrier with a perch towel, and bring a photo of the droppings if the complaint is diarrhea. Droppings should show a formed fecal portion and white urates. Lime-green, black, or seed-filled droppings need an explanation.

Whistles, Crests, and Company

  • A raised crest can mean curiosity or alarm. A slicked crest with hissing means stop.
  • Males often whistle more. Hens can whistle too, so the song is a poor sexing method. A DNA test or an experienced exam is clearer.
  • Teach step-up with millet, in short sessions, after the bird has eaten in the new cage for a few days.
  • A single cockatiel needs human company. A pair may entertain each other and still want a flock nearby.
  • Keep bedtime consistent. Sleep debt shows up as irritability and night frights.

A content cockatiel whistles when you enter, eats a pellet without tossing the whole bowl, and tucks one foot to nap. A silent bird with a puffed face and a bobbing tail needs an avian veterinarian now. Give the cage real width, keep the seed cup honest, and treat the night as part of the setup.

Nights, Whistles, and Powder

Cockatiel nights need a plan before the first crash. A door slam, a car alarm, or a hand passing the cage in the dark can send the bird into the bars. Place the cage away from the entry, leave a small night light, and keep bedtime within the same half hour. If a blood feather breaks, the bird may bleed more than you expect for its size. Stay calm, hold gentle pressure with cornstarch or a styptic powder made for birds, and call an avian veterinarian if the bleeding continues or the bird goes limp. Practice finding the carrier in the daylight so midnight is a grab-and-go.

Whistling is the social glue. Answer a contact call with a short whistle and then go back to your task, so the bird learns that you heard it and that screaming longer is unnecessary. Males often whistle more, and hens can learn tunes too, so enjoy the song and use a DNA test or an experienced exam if you need a sex for breeding decisions. Avoid breeding as a hobby unless you have an avian veterinarian and homes for chicks. A hut in the cage is a nest signal, and chronic laying drains calcium.

Powder down dusts the room. People with asthma should spend real time near an adult cockatiel before they commit, and an air filter in the bird room helps some households. Baths reduce dust and itching. Offer a shallow dish in the morning and let the bird dry away from a draft. Weigh the cockatiel in grams during any diet change from seed toward pellets. A few grams lost is a large share of the body. Pellets, greens, millet as a treat, and a cage at least 24 by 18 by 24 inches are the boring ingredients of a bird that can live past twenty.

Quick Answers

Are cockatiels good for first-time bird owners?

Often yes. They are gentler than many parrots, provided you offer a cage at least 24 by 18 by 24 inches, a pellet diet, and an avian veterinarian, and you plan for night frights and powder down.

Cage and perches usually cost more than the bird

Cage and perches usually cost more than the bird. For the first cockatiel visit, the same 2026 exotic-clinic roundup lists about $95 to $185 for the exam alone, before a fecal Gram stain (findanexoticvet.com). Use the AAV Find-a-Vet directory when you book, because AAV recommends those visits on a schedule (aav.org). Pellets and millet stay inexpensive month to month.
