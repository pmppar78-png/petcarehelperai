Bird Diet and Nutrition Guide

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Comprehensive bird nutrition guide covering pellets vs seeds, fresh foods, supplements, and species-specific dietary needs for pet birds.

Pellets as the base, seed as the limit

Most companion parrots, cockatiels, and budgerigars do better when a formulated pellet is the majority of the diet and seed is a measured treat. Seed mixes are heavy in fat and light in vitamin A, calcium, and several other nutrients birds use every day. A bird can look busy and bright on a seed diet for months while body stores run down. The switch takes patience. Offer pellets in the morning when the bird is hungry, keep a small measured seed portion for training, and expect some birds to protest. A bird who loses weight during a conversion needs the old food brought back up and a slower plan, plus a veterinary weight check. Starving a bird into accepting pellets is dangerous.

Read the pellet label for the species size. A large macaw pellet is a poor size for a budgie, and a budgie crumble may be too small to interest a cockatoo. Extruded pellets and cold-pressed pellets both have fans. The important part is that the bird actually eats them, which you confirm by watching the crop and the droppings and by weighing the bird on a gram scale at the same time of day. A bowl that looks empty can mean the bird flung pellets and ate only the seed hidden underneath. Forage toys slow that sorting.

Fresh water is changed at least daily, more often if the bird dunks food. Bowls that sit under a perch collect droppings and need a move. Wash them with hot water and a detergent you rinse fully. Sweetened drinks, alcohol, and coffee have no role in a bird's cup.

Vitamin A and the vegetables that supply it

Vitamin A deficiency is the classic result of an all-seed diet. It shows up as a weakened lining of the mouth, sinuses, and air spaces, with sneezing, swelling around the eyes, poor feather quality, and a higher risk of infection. Inside the mouth, the little papillae that should be sharp can look smooth and blunted. That is a veterinary finding, so a bird with chronic sneezing or a crusty nostril needs an avian veterinarian and a diet change. Treating only with a random vitamin drop misses the infections and the husbandry.

Useful foods for vitamin A include red and orange peppers, carrots, sweet potato cooked until soft, mango, papaya, cantaloupe, broccoli, kale, and dandelion greens. Dark leafy greens beat pale iceberg lettuce. Offer a small chop in the morning and remove uneaten fresh food within a few hours so it does not spoil in a warm room. A single favorite vegetable is a start. A rotation through the week is better. Birds who have never seen a pepper may need it chopped into the food they already accept, then gradually made more obvious.

Calcium matters, especially for laying hens, A cuttlebone, a mineral block, or a pellet that already contains balanced calcium covers many pet birds. Egg-laying hens, birds on seed, and birds with weak bones need an avian veterinarian to set calcium, vitamin D, and light exposure. Sunlight through a window does not provide the same UVB as outdoor shade, because glass filters it. Ask before you add a bird lamp or a liquid supplement. Too much vitamin D or calcium is its own disease.

Foods that poison birds

Avocado is unsafe for companion birds. Persin in the fruit, skin, and pit has been tied to heart and respiratory failure in psittacines. Guacamole, avocado toast, and a roommate's smoothie are all hazards. Chocolate, coffee, tea, and soda bring caffeine and, in chocolate, theobromine. Onion, garlic, and chives injure a bird's red blood cells. Alcohol, even a sip from a glass, can be fatal for a small bird. Fruit pits and apple seeds contain cyanide-producing compounds. Potato eyes and raw green potato are poor offerings. Xylitol-sweetened foods and gums belong in a closed cupboard.

Salty snacks, chips, and heavily seasoned human meals load a tiny kidney with salt and fat. Fried food and buttery pastry teach begging and add calories a caged bird cannot fly off. Yard mushrooms are a gamble you should keep away from a bird. Houseplants and vegetable leaves you cannot identify stay out of reach. Pits from cherries, peaches, nectarines, and apricots go in the trash before the fruit is clipped to the cage. Rinse produce to reduce pesticide residue, and skip the produce soap theater. A thorough water rinse is the practical step.

If a bird eats avocado, chocolate, onion, or an unknown plant, call an avian veterinarian or a poison hotline immediately. Bring the label or a photo of the plant. . Birds hide illness until they are very sick. A fluffed bird, a tail bob, vomiting, a seizure, or a bird on the cage floor needs emergency care. Keep the bird warm and quiet on the way, in a carrier, away from more of the food.

Species notes and a day of feeding

Budgerigars and cockatiels are prone to seed addiction and to fatty liver disease when seed, millet sprays, and crackers make up the day. Millet can stay a training reward in a pinch-sized piece. A spray left in the cage all week is a meal plan. Conures, amazons, African greys, and macaws need the same pellet base, with portion sizes matched to weight and activity. African greys are often discussed for low blood calcium, which is a reason for an avian exam and a balanced pellet, and a reason to avoid homemade diets built from memory. Lories and lorikeets eat nectar-style diets made for them. Offering only seed or only a parrot pellet misses how they feed.

A practical day looks like this: weigh the bird a few mornings a week, offer pellets at wake-up, put a small vegetable chop in a second dish, use a few seeds or a nut piece for training, and remove perishable food before it ferments. Nuts are high-fat training tools for larger parrots, a sliver at a time. Honey sticks and yogurt drops sold as bird treats are candy. Read those labels. If the droppings change color with beets or berries, that can be the food itself. Persistent undigested food, fewer droppings, or black or red droppings that food cannot explain need a clinic visit the same day.

Weight, droppings, and the conversion week

Buy a gram scale and a small perch that sits on it. Weigh before breakfast so the number is comparable. A budgie who drops several grams, or a larger parrot who drops a noticeable fraction of body weight, needs the old diet increased again and a call to an avian veterinarian. During a pellet conversion, offer the new food first for an hour, then a measured portion of the familiar seed, and track what actually disappears. Soaked pellets tempt some birds. Hand-feeding warm mash can create a begging habit, so use it as a bridge and step back once the bird eats from a bowl. Droppings that shrink to a tiny dark spot mean intake has fallen too low. Pause the conversion and call an avian veterinarian if weight is dropping.

  • Make a formulated pellet the majority of the diet for most parrots, cockatiels, and budgies.
  • Limit seed and millet to measured training rewards.
  • Rotate vitamin A-rich vegetables such as peppers, carrots, sweet potato, and dark greens.
  • Use cuttlebone or a balanced pellet for routine calcium, and let an avian veterinarian guide laying hens.
  • Keep avocado, chocolate, caffeine, onion, garlic, alcohol, and fruit pits out of the cage.
  • Offer a cuttlebone or mineral block beside a balanced pellet.
  • Weigh the bird regularly so a conversion or an illness shows up early.
  • Call an avian veterinarian after a toxic food, and do not induce vomiting.

Questions about bird diets

Are seed mixes enough for a pet bird?

Seed alone is high in fat and poor in vitamin A. Most budgies, cockatiels, and parrots do better with pellets as the base and seed as a measured treat. Convert slowly and weigh the bird so a hunger strike is caught early.

Which foods are toxic to companion birds?

Avocado, because of persin, plus chocolate, caffeine, onion, garlic, alcohol, and fruit pits. Xylitol-sweetened foods are a risk too. If one of these is eaten, call an avian veterinarian or a poison hotline and bring the package. Do not make the bird vomit.

How should calcium be handled for birds?

A pellet plus a cuttlebone or mineral block covers many pets. Laying birds and birds with suspected deficiency need an avian veterinarian. Window glass filters UVB, so indoor parrots often get little of it. Many avian veterinarians recommend a bird-specific full-spectrum lamp and a blood calcium check, especially for African greys (Association of Avian Veterinarians; Merck Veterinary Manual, Nutrition in Pet Birds).
