Geriatric Cat Care Guide

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Caring for senior cats including common age-related conditions, comfort modifications, nutritional needs, and quality of life considerations.

Jumping, stairs, and the litter box as a mobility report

Cats hide lameness by simply quitting the jump. The senior cat who used to sleep on the refrigerator and now sleeps on a chair has given you a mobility exam. Watch the way the cat gathers the hind end before a leap onto the bed. A pause, a failed first try, or a scramble with the claws means the joints, the spine, or the strength are changing. Some cats develop a lower crouch and a shorter stride. Others stop using a favorite windowsill and you only notice because the sill is dusty.

The litter box is often the first place arthritis shows. A box with high walls in the basement asks for a jump and a flight of stairs. Urine just outside the box, stool beside the box, or a cat who cries in the box can be pain, a urinary disease, or both. Put a low-sided box on every floor the cat uses, including the room where the cat sleeps. A storage tote with a door cut in the side works when the cat still wants privacy and can no longer hop a tall rim. Scoop daily. A sore cat will refuse a dirty box faster than a young cat will.

Place food, water, and a warm bed where the cat already spends the afternoon, so a painful trip down the hall is optional. A ramp of stacked boxes with a blanket can restore a windowsill. Heated pads made for pets, with a chew-safe cord, help some cats settle. Trim nails before they catch in fabric and twist a sore toe. If the cat resents the clippers, a groomer or the clinic can do it during a visit. Ask the veterinarian about pain control made for cats. Human pain tablets, including acetaminophen, can kill a cat. Do not experiment with a dose you found for a dog.

Dental disease makes cats drop kibble, chew on one side, or drool on the blanket. Bad breath that suddenly smells rotten, a swollen face, or pawing at the mouth should move the appointment up. Softening the diet can bridge the days until a dental plan exists, and it also adds water. Many older cats live on the edge of dehydration because the kidneys waste water. A fountain, an extra bowl in a quiet room, and wet food raise intake for cats who ignore a single kitchen dish. Call the clinic if the cat stops eating for a full day. Cats develop hepatic lipidosis when they go without calories, and waiting for the appetite to "come back on its own" is a risky bet.

Constipation shows up as repeated trips to the box with little result, a hard stool, or crying. Dehydration, pain on squatting, and kidney disease contribute. This is a clinic problem when it lasts more than a day or comes with vomiting. Do not use leftover human laxatives. The veterinarian can tell a simple dry-stool problem from an obstruction.

Night vocalizing and other cognitive shifts

An older cat may begin to yowl at night, wander from room to room, or forget the location of the box even when the box is obvious to you. Some cats stare at walls. Some forget they have eaten and demand food again, or they fail to recognize a person until that person speaks. Hearing loss and high blood pressure can sit underneath the same soundtrack. Hypertension in cats can detach a retina, so a sudden bumping into furniture or widely dilated pupils is an emergency. Treat it the same day.

Keep the floor plan stable. A night light near the box and the water helps a cat with poor vision. Answer a night yowl by checking pain, the box, and hunger once, then keep the response boring so the yowl does not become the way to start a party. Play that still fits the cat, such as a slow wand toy while the cat lies down, gives a daytime anchor. Ask the veterinarian whether blood pressure, thyroid level, and a urine check should come before any assumption that the cat is "just senile." Treating the thyroid or the blood pressure sometimes quiets the nights.

Quality-of-life questions for a cat's veterinarian

Cats withdraw when they feel bad, so a quiet cat can look "fine" and be miserable. Track whether the cat still seeks a lap or a sunny spot, still grooms, still jumps to at least one favorite perch, and still eats without being coaxed for long. Note hiding, unkempt fur on the back, a hunched posture, and any day with no urine in the box. Bring that diary. Ask which changes would justify pain medicine, a diet change, or subcutaneous fluids at home, and ask which changes mean the cat should be seen before the next planned recheck.

Useful questions sound like this: Is the cat able to reach food, water, and a box without a struggle we could remove by moving the furniture? Have we looked for treatable disease behind the appetite change? What does a crisis look like for this cat, such as straining without urine, open-mouth breathing, or collapse? What help exists after hours? When the hard hours crowd out the easy ones for days at a time, ask about hospice steps at home and about euthanasia as a planned appointment, including how the clinic handles the body and what your area allows. Learn the emergency route while the cat is still having ordinary Tuesdays. The worst day is a bad time to find the address.

Grooming and the body you can check at home

A cat who stops reaching the spine will grow a greasy strip of fur and mats over the hips. That strip is a mobility and flexibility clue. Daily combing with a wide-tooth comb, done while the cat is already in a lap, keeps the skin visible. Run a hand along the ribs and the spine each week. Muscle loss shows up as a sharper backbone even when the belly still looks round. That round belly can be fat, fluid, or organ enlargement, and only an exam separates them. Look in the ears for dark debris and under the tail for stool that the cat can no longer clean. A sanitary trim helps when the fur there stays damp.

Claws on older cats thicken and can grow into a circle that pierces the pad. Check the feet when you comb. If the cat fights the trim, wrap the session into a clinic visit and ask them to look at the nails while they are already handling bloodwork. Mouth, eyes, and the skin inside the ears are the other home checks that catch pain early. A squint, a third eyelid that stays up, or a head tilt needs an appointment this week, sooner if the cat is also off food.

  • Offer a low-sided litter box on each floor the cat uses.
  • Read abandoned high perches and failed jumps as pain clues.
  • Weigh the cat regularly and report both increased and decreased appetite.
  • Treat a full day without food, or straining without urine, as urgent.
  • Ask about blood pressure, thyroid, and kidneys when night yowling starts.
  • Never give a cat a pain tablet made for people or a dose copied from a dog.
  • Keep a diary of grooming, perches, appetite, and hiding for the quality-of-life visit.

Questions about caring for an older cat

How do I tell if an older cat is in pain?

Cats often stop jumping, miss the litter box, sleep in new low places, stop grooming the back, or resent being picked up. A video of a jump and a note about the box help the veterinarian more than a cat who freezes politely on the exam table.

Why would an old cat yowl at night?

Cognitive change, hyperthyroidism, high blood pressure, hearing loss, pain, and hunger can all sound similar. Sudden blindness or dilated pupils need same-day care.

What should I ask at a quality-of-life visit for a cat?

Ask whether food, water, and the box are reachable, which diseases have been ruled out, what a crisis looks like, and how after-hours care works. Bring several days of notes on eating, grooming, and hiding so the decision rests on a pattern.
