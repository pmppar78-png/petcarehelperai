Pet Proofing Your

Complete home safety guide for pet owners covering every room. Eliminates hazards from kitchen chemicals to bathroom medications to garage antifreeze.

Kitchen

The kitchen collects the foods and chemicals that fill emergency clinics. Latch the cabinet under the sink. Move trash into a can with a lid a dog cannot knock over, or into a closet. Dishwasher pods, alcohol, chocolate, grapes, xylitol gum, and onion scraps are the usual finds. Hang purses on a hook because ibuprofen lives in them. Use back burners and turn pot handles in. A puppy gate at the doorway keeps the animal in view while you cook. Wipe the floor when you finish chopping so onion pieces and broken glass do not wait for a tongue. Cords for slow cookers should sit where a tooth cannot unplug them onto a paw.

Secure the refrigerator if you have a dog who opens it. Store pet food in a bin with a lid. An open bin becomes an all-day buffet that hides appetite changes and attracts rodents. Cleaning sprays stay in their original bottles. A spray bottle of sweet tea next to a spray bottle of disinfectant is how the wrong one gets licked. When guests cook, repeat the lid rule. A party is when the system fails, so assign one person to the kitchen door.

Bathroom and laundry

Shut the toilet lid if your dog drinks from the bowl, and keep cleaners and toilet tablets out of the tank area a pet can reach. Razors, cotton swabs, and hair ties belong in a drawer. Hair ties and dental floss are linear foreign bodies that can bunch the intestine. Toothpaste often contains xylitol. Store it inside the cabinet, and rinse the sink after you brush so residue is gone. The clothes dryer and the washer are death traps when a cat climbs in for warmth. Knock on the door and look inside before every cycle. Detergent pods go in a latched cupboard. A front-load door left open is an invitation.

Medications in a weekly organizer should live where a tail cannot sweep them down. Count pills if a bottle spills and call a veterinarian or a poison hotline with the name of the drug. Hot curling irons and diffusers need a shelf and a timeout. Essential oils and a cat in a small bathroom are a bad pair because the cat will groom whatever lands on the fur. Air the room and shut the cat out while you clean with strong products. Mop water gets dumped in a sink, then the sink is rinsed.

Living areas, windows, and cords

Bind blind cords and electrical cables so they cannot make a loop. Cats and puppies get tangled, and a cord can also be chewed into a burn. Window screens should be latched and checked for a loose corner a cat can push. Falls from upper windows happen when a screen looks closed and is only resting in the track. Houseplants go through an identification step. True lilies and daylilies leave a cat household. Sago palms leave a dog or cat household. If you love the plant and the pet, the plant lives where the pet physically cannot go, which for a cat often means it cannot live in the home at all.

Cover trash tables during holidays. Tinsel, ribbon, glass ornaments, and chocolate are a December surgery list. After guests leave, walk the rooms again and look under couches. Batteries from toys and singing cards are easy to miss and dangerous to swallow. Rocking chairs and recliners crush pets who nap in the mechanism. Look before you lower the footrest. Space heaters need a barrier so a tail or a whisker does not touch the element. Fireplaces get a screen, and the ashes stay cold before a dog investigates.

Bedrooms, garage, and the door you actually use

Bedrooms hide gum, medications, and small toys. A laundry basket of socks is a foreign-body kit. Keep the basket in a closet. Elastic hair bands go in a jar with a lid. If a child builds with tiny bricks, the bricks go into a bin the dog cannot dump. Nightstands are where people set a pill "for morning." Put the pill in the bottle. Garage floors collect antifreeze, gasoline, and rodent bait. Store those in latched cabinets, wipe drips, and keep pets out of the garage while a car is being repaired. A sweet puddle is a reason to call a veterinarian before anyone licks it.

Doors are a training project. A baby gate holds a new puppy in one visible room. A second gate protects a bird or a rabbit while a dog is loose. Self-closing hinges or a habit of latching the yard gate prevent a loose dog more reliably than a lecture. Check fences for dig spots. Inside, use baby locks on cabinets that failed the sniff test. Re-walk the house when you bring home a new plant, a new cleaner, or a holiday decoration. Proofing is a lap you repeat, because the hazards arrive in grocery bags. The goal is a pet who can be left for the length of time that species can actually handle, in a room you have already made boring.

  • Latch sink cabinets, lid the trash, and hang purses away from dogs.
  • Check the washer and dryer before every cycle, and store pods out of reach.
  • Bind cords, secure screens, and remove lilies and sago palms.
  • Sweep for tinsel, ribbon, batteries, and chocolate after gatherings.
  • Keep socks, hair ties, and small toys in closed containers.
  • Store antifreeze and bait in latched garage cabinets and wipe spills.
  • Use gates so each species has a room the others cannot enter.
  • Repeat the walk-through when new plants, cleaners, or decorations arrive.

A repeat lap when the house changes

Proofing slips when a new object arrives. A lily bouquet, a sago palm, a bottle of antifreeze, a string of tinsel, or a bag of xylitol gum each deserves a walk-through the day it comes home. Check screens by pushing gently at the corners. Tie new blind cords before the cat finds them. Look inside the washer. Confirm the trash lid still latches after a teenager took it off. Gates should actually close. A gate that leans is a gap. Write a ten-minute list and do it on the first day of a holiday weekend, when guests and food and plants show up together.

Species change the list. A puppy needs the kitchen gate and the sock basket closed. A cat needs lilies gone and cords out of loops. A bird needs the kitchen free of avocado and nonstick fumes the manufacturer warns about, with the bird out of the room while you cook. A dog and a rabbit need a closed door between them during the rabbit's floor time. Proofing is the set of doors and latches that match the animals you actually have. Repeat it when the roster changes.

Questions about pet-proofing

Which kitchen hazards should I fix first?

Lidded trash, latched under-sink cabinets, purses hung up, and grape, chocolate, onion, and xylitol foods stored high. A gate at the kitchen door helps while you cook. Wipe the floor after chopping so scraps get picked up before a lick.

How do I make windows and cords safer?

Latch screens and test that they cannot be pushed out. Tie blind cords up so they do not form a loop. Route electrical cords behind furniture. True lilies have to leave a cat home, and sago palms should leave if dogs or cats live with you.

What belongs in a garage and laundry check?

Antifreeze jugs capped and latched, no sweet puddles, rodent bait in pet-proof stations or gone, and detergent pods in a cupboard. Look inside the washer and dryer before you start them. A weekly pill box should sit where a pet cannot sweep it onto the floor.
