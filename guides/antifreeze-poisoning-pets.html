Antifreeze Poisoning in

Ethylene glycol, the main ingredient in most automotive antifreeze, tastes sweet and a small lick is enough to matter. Merck Veterinary Manual puts the kidney-injury clock at about 12–24 hours in cats and 36–72 hours in dogs when treatment has not started. Call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 888-426-4435 before any home treatment, and do not give hydrogen peroxide to a cat.

Covers ethylene glycol toxicity, symptoms, emergency treatment, and prevention tips.

Ethylene glycol is sweet and fast

Most automotive antifreeze contains ethylene glycol. It tastes sweet, and a puddle in a garage or a driveway leak from a radiator is enough to attract a dog or a cat. The body metabolizes ethylene glycol into acidic compounds that damage the kidneys. There is a window, measured in hours, when a clinic can still block that metabolism with an antidote. After the kidneys are badly injured, the same antidote cannot rewind the damage. That is why a suspected lick is an immediate trip, while the pet still looks normal or only a little drunk.

Early signs resemble alcohol: wobbliness, vomiting, drooling, and a pet who seems disoriented. Then there can be a quieter period when the family thinks the danger passed. Later, thirst increases, urine output may rise and then fall, and the pet becomes depressed as kidney failure develops. Cats often move through this faster than dogs. Pain in the belly, a low body temperature, and seizures can appear. Do not wait for the quiet period to end before you call. The quiet period is part of the poisoning.

Antidotes happen in a clinic

Veterinarians use fomepizole or, in some settings, ethanol to slow the production of the toxic metabolites. Those drugs are given in a hospital with monitoring. They work best early. This page will not describe how to mix or dose them at home. Home ethanol experiments cause a second poisoning and delay arrival. If your veterinarian or a poison hotline tells you to come in, leave immediately and bring the container. Tell them the brand and whether the label says ethylene glycol or propylene glycol. Those are different chemicals.

Propylene glycol antifreeze is marketed as less harmful to pets. It can still cause illness, and products get swapped in garages so the jug you think is "the safe one" may be the classic formula. Call anyway and read the active ingredient. Windshield washer fluid and some brake fluids are separate toxins and still belong on the phone call if that is what spilled. A mystery puddle with a sweet smell and a brightly dyed color should be treated as ethylene glycol until the label says otherwise. Do not induce vomiting unless the clinic tells you to. A wobbly pet can inhale vomit. Cats in particular should not be given peroxide at home.

What to do with the spill and the pet

Move the pet to fresh air and keep them warm. Lock other animals away from the puddle. If the coat is soaked, a veterinarian may want a mild soap bath so grooming does not continue the ingestion. Ask before you start, and dry the pet afterward. Mop the spill with absorbent material, bag it, and wash the floor. Ethylene glycol on paws is licked off indoors an hour later. Photograph the jug. If the jug is missing its label, take the jug anyway. Dye color changes by brand. You cannot trust the color as identification.

At the hospital, expect bloodwork, a test aimed at ethylene glycol when it is available, urine checks, and intravenous fluids along with the antidote if the exposure is real and the timing still allows it. Kidney values may be rechecked over the next days. Some pets need longer hospitalization. Ask what a change in urine output means and who to call overnight. A pet who is already not producing urine is in a much harder place. That sentence is the argument for leaving the house while the pet is still walking.

Garage habits that prevent the puddle

Store antifreeze in a cabinet with a latch, in the original jug, with the cap tight. A drip pan under a leaky car should be emptied immediately, and the leak should be repaired. Driveway spills from a neighbor's car still count. Rinse them away and keep pets inside until the area is dry and you have checked for remaining slick spots. Do not drain a radiator onto the ground. Many communities have a disposal site for coolant. Use it. If you switch products, retire the old jug. Two jugs on the shelf mean a leak can come from either one.

Tell every driver in the house that a sweet puddle is a medical emergency for the dog and the cat. Children should be asked to report spills and rewarded for the report. A radiator boil-over on a trip needs the same response: pets stay in the car or on a leash, away from the steam and the fluid, and a clinic gets the call if anyone may have walked through it. The toxin is common, the timeline is short, and the prevention is a closed cap. When the cap is already off, the next step is the car keys and the jug in the passenger seat.

  • Treat any possible ethylene glycol lick as an immediate veterinary emergency.
  • Expect drunken signs, a quieter gap, and then kidney failure.
  • Know that fomepizole or ethanol antidotes belong in a clinic and work best early.
  • Bring the jug and read ethylene glycol versus propylene glycol to the hotline.
  • Do not induce vomiting unless instructed, and do not give peroxide to a cat.
  • Clean spills so paws cannot carry the fluid indoors.
  • Store jugs latched and repair radiator leaks.
  • Call a poison hotline if your clinic line is busy, then drive if they say drive.

The driveway after a leak

A radiator drip can land where a pet drinks from a puddle the same afternoon. Rinse the area, keep animals inside until it is dry, and call if anyone may have licked it. Bring the jug so the clinic can see ethylene glycol versus propylene glycol. Antidotes such as fomepizole or ethanol are given in hospital and depend on arriving early, during the drunken phase or even before signs start. A quieter period afterward is part of the poisoning. It fails as proof that the kidneys are safe. Ask how urine output will be monitored if the pet stays.

Cap the jug, latch the cabinet, and repair the leak. Dispose of old coolant at a proper site. A storm drain or a patch of grass spreads the puddle. Tell every driver that a sweet puddle means a phone call. Cats investigate garages at night. A closed door is the prevention that matches that habit. If the exposure already happened, the passenger seat holds the pet and the jug, and the clinic is the destination.

Questions about antifreeze

Why is antifreeze so dangerous for pets?

Ethylene glycol tastes sweet and is metabolized into acids that destroy kidney tissue. Dogs and cats can be poisoned by a puddle. Early wobbliness may fade before kidney failure shows up. Antidotes such as fomepizole or ethanol must be given in a clinic, and they need to be started early.

What should I do if my pet walked through a green puddle?

Wipe paws if you can do it safely, keep the pet from licking, and call a veterinarian or poison hotline immediately. Bring the container if you have it. Skip home vomiting unless the antifreeze hotline explicitly clears this pet. Dye color varies by brand, so the label matters more than the color.

Is propylene glycol antifreeze something I can ignore?

Call anyway. Propylene glycol products are a different chemical and can still make a pet sick. Garages often contain both formulas, and a jug can be mislabeled by memory. Read the active ingredient to the clinic and let them decide.
