Leopard Gecko Complete Care Guide

Comprehensive leopard gecko care guide covering enclosure, heating, diet, shedding, common health issues, and morphs.

Housing and Enclosure

Leopard geckos are terrestrial, crepuscular lizards, and floor space matters more than height. One adult needs at least a 20-gallon-long footprint, and a 36-by-18-inch or 40-gallon-style enclosure is a better adult home because it lets you separate the warm hide from the cool hide by a real distance. Front-opening cages make it easier to spot-clean without grabbing a gecko that has learned to fear a hand from above. A secure lid matters. They are poor climbers and still find a loose screen corner.

Heat belongs under or at the warm end, controlled by a thermostat, with the floor of the warm hide around 88 to 92 F. The cool side can sit near 75 F. Nighttime can drop into the low 70s if the warm hide is still available. Measure the floor with an infrared thermometer or a probe inside the hide. Air at the ceiling is the wrong reading. A heat rock without a thermostat burns bellies. Overhead heat can work if a thermostat and a proper hide turn it into a warm shelter the gecko can press against. The essential piece is that warm hide. A leopard gecko that cannot get its belly onto a warm surface will not digest, even if the room feels pleasant to you.

Add a cool hide and a moist hide packed with damp paper towel or sphagnum. The moist hide is where most geckos shed. If it dries out, shed sticks to toes and eye caps. Substrate can be paper towel, slate tile, or a packed soil-and-sand mix that holds a burrow. Loose fine sand as the only floor is a poor choice for a gecko that snaps at insects and swallows grit. Provide several inches of cover. These animals dislike an open tabletop with one water dish in the middle.

UVB is no longer considered pointless for leopard geckos. A low-level linear UVB over the warm end, with plenty of shade and a hide they can enter, supports vitamin D. It should be far gentler than the high-output desert lamp used over a bearded dragon basking site. If you keep a gecko with no UVB at all, calcium dusted onto insects has to carry vitamin D3, and you should still offer a shaded gradient so the animal can reach shade and the warm hide in the same cage. Replace UVB bulbs on schedule. A lamp that still glows can be UVB-dead.

Diet and Nutrition

Leopard geckos eat insects. Gut-load crickets, dubia roaches, and black soldier fly larvae, and offer the occasional mealworm. Waxworms and hornworms are rich treats for rare occasions. Used as a routine meal, they swell the tail and make the gecko picky. Feed juveniles most days. Feed healthy adults every other day or a few times a week, depending on the tail. The tail is the fat store. A thick tail is a reserve. A thin tail means you look at temperature, parasites, and how many insects are actually being eaten.

Dust insects with calcium at most feedings. Use a calcium that includes D3 when the cage has no UVB, and a calcium with little or no D3 when a proper UVB tube is in place, and avoid stacking D3 from two sources without a plan. A small bottle cap of plain calcium in the cage lets breeding females self-serve. A multivitamin dust once a week covers the rest. Do not feed mice, cat food, or fruit. This species is an insectivore, and a "variety platter" from the fridge causes more impaction and obesity than it solves.

Water stays in a shallow dish, changed daily. Many geckos also drink droplets after you mist the moist hide. Insects should be no wider than the gecko's head. A cricket left loose in the cage overnight will chew on a sleeping gecko. Pull uneaten insects or offer them in a smooth bowl the gecko can still reach.

Health and Veterinary Care

Metabolic bone disease follows a diet of undusted insects and no UVB. The jaw softens, the legs bow, and the gecko trembles. It is an emergency for a reptile veterinarian, and the long-term fix is calcium plus the right UVB or D3 plan. One dusting after months of neglect leaves the damage in place. Impaction shows as a gecko that stops passing waste and sits in the warm hide. Cool temperatures and a mouthful of sand are the usual setup. Crypto, Cryptosporidium, causes a thin gecko with a stick tail that keeps eating and still wastes away. It spreads easily between leopard geckos on shared tools. Isolate a wasting animal and ask for a specific test. Stuck shed on toes and retained eye caps come from a dry moist hide. Eye infections and vitamin A problems show as swollen, shut eyes that need a veterinarian. Skip vitamin oil recipes copied from a forum.

Respiratory infections are less common than in tropical species and still happen when the cage is cold and damp. Mouth rot looks like cheese along the gum line. Both need a clinic that sees reptiles. Weigh the gecko monthly. A sudden tail drop in weight, with normal heat, deserves a fecal exam before you buy another tub of insects. Wash hands after handling. Crypto and salmonella are reasons to keep this animal's water dish out of the kitchen sink spray zone.

Handle from the side, supporting the body, after the gecko has eaten and settled for a week in a new cage. A tail wiggle is a warning. They can drop the tail, and it regenerates as a shorter, blunter shape. Treat a dropped tail as an injury. Short sessions on a low surface beat a long session over a tile floor.

A Simple Week

  • Confirm the warm-hide floor with a thermometer every time the season changes.
  • Refresh the moist hide so the moss is damp in the middle and the rest of the cage stays dry.
  • Gut-load insects the day before you feed, then dust them.
  • Spot-clean waste daily. Leopard geckos pick one corner, which makes this easy.
  • Watch the toes after each shed and soak only if a ring of skin is already tight, then see a veterinarian if color changes.

At dusk a healthy leopard gecko comes out of the warm hide, tongue-flicks the slate, and stalks a cricket with the tail carried up. A gecko that stays buried, or that is out all day glass-surfing, is telling you the hides or the temperatures are wrong. Fix the gradient before you add more décor.

The Tail, the Warm Hide, and the Dust

A leopard gecko's tail is the feeding record. Thick and full means the insect plan and the warm hide are working. Thin or stick-like means you check the floor of the warm hide, which should sit near 88 to 92 F, and you ask a reptile veterinarian about parasites, including Cryptosporidium if the gecko eats and still wastes away. Isolate a wasting animal. Crypto spreads on shared tools. The moist hide stays damp so sheds leave the toes and the eye caps. A dry paper towel in that hide is how rings of skin cut off toes.

Dust gut-loaded insects with calcium at most meals. Include D3 in that dust when the cage has no UVB, and ease off extra D3 when a low linear UVB tube is already in place, so the two sources stay a single plan. The UVB here stays gentle, with a hide the gecko can enter, far below the output a bearded dragon basks under. Feed juveniles often and adults every few days. Pull leftover crickets so they do not chew a sleeping gecko. Paper, slate, or a packed soil mix beats loose sand as the only floor. Short handling from the side, after the gecko has settled, is plenty. A dropped tail is an injury, even though a shorter tail can regrow.

Quick Answers

Are leopard geckos good for beginner reptile owners?

Yes, for someone who will run a thermostat, keep a warm hide near 88 to 92 F, and dust gut-loaded insects with calcium. They are a poor match for a fish tank with a heat rock and a sand-only floor.

How much does leopard gecko care cost?

A front-opening cage, thermostat, hides, and a low UVB fixture are the main startup purchases and commonly land in the low hundreds of dollars. Insects, calcium, and electricity continue monthly, and a reptile veterinarian visit should be in the yearly plan.
