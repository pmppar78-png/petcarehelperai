Responsible Dog Breeding

Comprehensive guide to responsible dog breeding covering health testing, genetics, whelping preparation, puppy care, and ethical breeding standards.

Breeding is a veterinary and a placement responsibility

Producing a litter means you are responsible for the health of the dam, the survival of the puppies, and the lifetime homes those puppies go to. A backyard breeding that starts with "she is cute and he lives down the street" skips the work that separates a planned litter from an accident. Cute at two years old does not predict hips at six, eyes at eight, or whether the puppies will be safe around children. The veterinarian who knows the dam should be part of the decision before any mating, and the plan for every puppy's home should exist before the puppies exist.

This page will not teach techniques for forcing a tie, for choosing drugs to alter a cycle, or for maximizing puppy count. Those choices belong in a clinic conversation because they can harm the dam. What belongs here is the screening, the cost, and the placement duty that remain even when a litter is accidental.

Health tests that matter before anyone is bred

Orthopedic screening depends on the breed. Hips are commonly evaluated through OFA-style radiographs or through PennHIP distraction films, read by the programs built for that purpose. Elbows are radiographed in breeds that suffer elbow dysplasia. Knees are checked for patellar luxation in many small and medium dogs. A pretty gait in the park misses early disease. The films are taken once the dog is old enough for the program you are using, and a veterinarian or a specialist positions them.

Eyes are examined by a boarded ophthalmologist through a CAER or OFA eye clinic. Some diseases show up only as the dog ages, so a single clear exam at 18 months is a start and a repeat exam later still matters for the breeding decision. Genetic tests from a reputable laboratory cover breed-specific problems such as progressive retinal atrophy, von Willebrand disease, degenerative myelopathy, MDR1 drug sensitivity, and exercise-induced collapse, among many others. The test menu follows the breed. A clear result on a test the breed does not need is marketing. A missing result on the test the breed does need is a gap. Ask the veterinarian or a breed club's health statement which results should be in hand, and read the actual reports.

Brucella canis testing matters because canine brucellosis causes infertility, abortion, and illness, and it can spread between dogs at breeding. A veterinarian guides the test timing. Thyroid function, cardiac exams by a cardiologist, and hearing tests are part of the standard in specific breeds. Temperament is a health item too. A dog who bites from fear, who cannot recover from a startle, or who has repeated severe anxiety is a poor candidate even when the radiographs are beautiful. Breeding that dog predicts puppies who may need behavioral help their owners cannot find.

The dam, the costs, and the emergencies

A dam should be in good body condition, current on the preventive care the clinic recommends, and free of active infection. Pregnancy and nursing are hard on teeth, calcium balance, and hydration. Budget for prenatal exams, ultrasound or radiographs, a possible cesarean, emergency care, dewclaw or other procedures only if already planned with the clinic, vaccines and deworming for the litter, and food for a nursing mother who will eat far more than usual. A cesarean on a weekend can cost more than the puppies will ever "return." If that number would ruin you, the breeding should wait.

Whelping emergencies include a stuck puppy, a dam who strains without progress, green discharge before any puppy, collapse, and eclampsia while nursing. The after-hours hospital route should be written down before the due date. Breeders who cannot reach a clinic within a reasonable drive are breeding without a safety net. An accidental breeding has the same emergencies as a planned one. A tie that already happened is a reason to call the veterinarian promptly for options, and those options are medical decisions. This page does not list drugs for mismating.

Placement, contracts, and dogs who should not be bred

Every puppy needs a named home or a written foster plan before you celebrate the litter. Screening homes means asking about previous pets, fencing, hours alone, and whether the family can afford food and veterinary care. A contract can require the puppy to come back to you if the home fails, can describe the health testing you actually did, and can set a spay or neuter expectation for pet homes on the timeline the veterinarian recommends for that breed. Overstating health clearances is a trust problem and, in many places, a legal one. Give the family the copies of the reports.

Dogs who should stay pets include those with failing hips, serious heart disease, inherited eye disease in a form the ophthalmologist says is breeding-significant, unstable temperaments, and any dog whose owner cannot afford a cesarean or a complication. One litter "to let her experience motherhood" or "so the kids can see birth" puts a medical event in the house for sentiment. Spaying prevents pyometra, a uterine infection that is common in intact females and often an emergency. Neutering prevents roaming and unwanted ties. The veterinarian can time those surgeries for the individual dog.

If a litter already exists, the duty shifts to keeping the puppies warm, feeding the dam, weighing the newborns, and finding homes that will vaccinate and train. Selling or giving a puppy at the roadside, or posting an ad with no questions asked, exports your emergency into someone else's kitchen. Keep puppies with the dam until they are ready for solid food and a veterinary start, on the clinic's advice, and send each puppy with records. If you cannot place them, a shelter or a breed rescue is a better call than a free ad that attracts people who flip dogs.

Records a future owner should be able to read

Keep a folder with registered names if you use a registry, the identification of both parents, the dates of orthopedic films, the eye exam certificate, genetic-test PDFs, Brucella results, and vaccine and deworming notes for the dam and the litter. Hand copies over at pickup. A verbal promise that "the parents were tested" is weaker than the paper. If a result is abnormal and you bred anyway on specialist advice, the advice belongs in writing too, so the family hears the same sentence you heard.

Track weights of the dam and, after birth, of each puppy. Note who nursed and who needed help. Those notes help the clinic if a puppy fades and they help the next owner understand the start. Identification, whether a microchip or a collar tag at going-home time, should be in the folder with the registry transfer instructions if a registry is part of your plan. Families who will never show the dog still deserve the health file.

  • Involve a veterinarian before a mating, and budget for surgery and emergencies.
  • Screen hips, elbows, knees, eyes, and breed-specific genetic tests using real reports.
  • Ask about Brucella testing and any cardiac or hearing screen the breed needs.
  • Leave cycle drugs, tie techniques, and litter-maximizing tricks to a clinic, and expect many of them to be declined.
  • Write a home for every puppy before the birth, with a return plan if the home fails.
  • Skip breeding a dog with significant inherited disease or a temperament you would not want next door.
  • Treat an accidental tie as a prompt veterinary call, and follow the medical options you are given.
  • Spay or neuter pet dogs on the schedule the clinic recommends for that body.

Questions about responsible dog breeding

Which health tests come up before breeding a dog?

Hips through OFA-style or PennHIP films, elbows and patellas where the breed requires them, an ophthalmologist eye exam, and genetic panels aimed at that breed's known diseases. Read the reports with your veterinarian.

Why is an unplanned backyard litter a veterinary problem?

The dam can need a cesarean, can stall in labor, or can develop a low-calcium crisis while nursing. Puppies need warmth, weighing, vaccines, and homes that will keep them. Those duties exist whether the breeding was planned. A clinic should hear about the pregnancy early.

What belongs in a puppy placement plan?

A screened home, copies of the health reports you actually have, a return clause if the family cannot keep the dog, and a spay or neuter plan timed with a veterinarian. Avoid ads that ask no questions. If homes do not exist, call a rescue before the puppies are desperate.
