Complete Care Guide

Complete goldfish care guide debunking bowl myths. Covers proper tank size, filtration, diet, varieties, lifespan, and common health issues.

Housing and Enclosure

Goldfish are coolwater carp, and they live for a decade or two when the tank is big enough for the waste they make. A fancy goldfish, the round-bodied fish with a double tail, needs at least 20 gallons for the first animal and about 10 more gallons for each additional fancy. Common goldfish, comets, and shubunkins grow long and powerful. Plan on 55 gallons or a pond for one of those, and more water as they approach adult length. A bowl cannot dilute their ammonia.

Most homes already sit near 65 to 75 F, which is the range fancies and commons do well in. A heater is optional equipment. Add one only if a winter room falls into the low 60s or below and you have confirmed the drop with a thermometer. Tropical community fish are the animals that need a thermostat heater. Goldfish cannot set their own temperature, so a sudden move from a cold windowsill to a warm tank still stresses them, but the fix is a stable room and a refusal to crank a reef heater to 80 F. Hot water plus a deep-bodied fancy is a recipe for oxygen trouble, because warm water holds less oxygen and these fish already gulp.

Filtration has to be oversized. Rate the filter for at least twice the tank volume, and favor a canister or a large sponge plus a hang-on-back that will not suck a wen or a flowing tail. Goldfish graze and spit gravel, so leave a clear feeding patch and avoid sharp decorative stone. A bare-bottom or fine-sand tank is easier to siphon. Live plants that survive digging, such as Anubias tied to wood, help with nitrate. Floating cover is optional shade you can skip. Give them a long horizontal swim and skip a tall column of unused water.

Stock slowly. Two fancies in a 30-gallon tank with heavy filtration is a common adult pair. Adding a third the same week the filter is still new will spike ammonia. Commons do not belong in a fancy tank once they outgrow it. They outcompete the slower fish for food and batter them during spawning chases. Outdoor ponds need shade, a deep zone that stays cool in summer, and a plan for winter ice that still lets gas escape. Indoor fish stay indoors unless you are ready to quarantine them on the way back.

Diet and Nutrition

Use a goldfish pellet or a gel diet as the staple, and prefer sinking foods for fancies. Floating flakes make round-bodied fish gulp air, and that extra air is a common trigger for buoyancy trouble. Soak pellets for a minute so they sink and finish swelling in the bowl before the fish swallows them. Feed small meals two or three times a day, only what disappears in a couple of minutes. Goldfish will beg long after they are full.

Blanched vegetables fill out the diet. Shelled peas, zucchini rounds, and a leaf of romaine or spinach offered briefly are useful. Pull spinach back out. It is a treat, and a pile of wilted leaves left overnight rots. Bloodworms or brine shrimp once or twice a week add animal protein. Skip bread, crackers, and the leftover rice from dinner. Those foods cloud the water and do nothing for a fish that already gets a complete pellet.

Fancy goldfish with buoyancy issues often improve when meals get smaller, the food sinks, and the water stays cool and clean. A frozen pea is a mild laxative in keeper lore. It helps some fish and does nothing for an infection. If the fish is listing and the water tests dirty, fix the water first.

Health and Veterinary

Swim-bladder trouble, ich, fin rot, and dropsy show up again and again in goldfish. Fancies, with their compact bodies, are the ones that roll or hang nose-down. Clean, cool water and sinking food address the husbandry side. Ich looks like salt grains and usually follows a new fish or a chill. Fin rot frays edges after ammonia or an injury from a sharp ornament. Dropsy, with raised scales, is an emergency for a veterinarian who sees fish. Red streaks in the fins, or a fish gasping while the surface is already agitated, deserve the same call.

Change 25 to 30 percent of the water every week and vacuum the waste goldfish never stop producing. Test ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate. Ammonia and nitrite poisoning is the failure the Merck Veterinary Manual flags first for aquarium fish. It shows up after an uncycled tank, overfeeding, a dead fish left in the water, or a filter that quit. Both should read 0 ppm. Goldfish cannot regulate body heat, so keep a thermometer on the glass even though a heater stays in the closet unless the room itself is cold. Ich and other parasites usually ride in on new fish or plants. Quarantine those arrivals in their own tank. Bacterial problems such as fin rot usually follow poor water or a wound. Copper medicines poison shrimp, snails, and plants and can stall the filter bacteria, which means ammonia and nitrite have to be watched for weeks after copper is used.

Further reading sits in the Merck Veterinary Manual section on managing aquarium fish.

When you buy a new goldfish, float the bag to equalize temperature, then add small amounts of tank water before release. Bring fish home a few at a time so the biofilter can grow into the load. Check adult shape and temperament before you pair a slim comet with a ranchu. Keep hands out of the tank when you can, and rinse them if you must move a decoration. Net a goldfish gently. Chasing a wen-covered fish around the glass injures the eyes and the head growth.

Weekly Rhythm

  • Read the thermometer. Confirm the room is still in the mid 60s to mid 70s F.
  • Test ammonia and nitrite, and expect zeroes in a mature tank.
  • Swap 25 to 30 percent of the water and siphon the feeding zone.
  • Rinse mechanical filter media in removed tank water so the bacteria colony stays put.
  • Watch each fish swim level. A fancy that suddenly heads for the surface may be constipated, chilled, or sitting in nitrite.

Healthy goldfish are busy. They sample the gravel, they notice you, and their fins are carried out from the body. A fish clamped in a corner with a string of waste trailing behind it is telling you the last water change was late or the last meal was too big. Fix those two things before you reach for a medication bottle.

Waste, Oxygen, and a Level Swim

Goldfish husbandry is mostly waste management in cool water. A fancy needs about 20 gallons for the first fish and 10 more for each additional fancy, with a filter rated for at least twice the volume. Commons and comets need a much larger tank or a pond. The room should sit near 65 to 75 F. Add a heater only when a thermometer shows the water falling into the low 60s. Warm water holds less oxygen, and a deep-bodied fancy already works harder to breathe. A thermometer stays on the glass either way, because these fish cannot set their own temperature.

Feed sinking pellets or gel food in small meals, and offer a shelled pea or a zucchini round as a vegetable. Floating flakes encourage gulping, which feeds the buoyancy problems fancies are known for. Change 25 to 30 percent of the water weekly and test ammonia and nitrite, both of which belong at zero. Ich, fin rot, and a pinecone body are reasons to call a veterinarian who sees fish, after you have the test numbers in hand. Quarantine new goldfish. Net them gently. A chase around a wen and a telescope eye causes injuries that look like disease a week later. Busy fish that sample the sand and carry their fins out from the body are the ones the tank volume was bought for.

Quick Answers

Are goldfish easy to care for?

They are hardy when the tank is large enough. A fancy goldfish needs at least 20 gallons plus 10 more for each extra fish, a strong filter, and weekly water changes of 25 to 30 percent.

Do goldfish need a heater?

Most homes already provide the 65 to 75°F they do well in. What they do need is strong filtration, because they produce a lot of waste.
