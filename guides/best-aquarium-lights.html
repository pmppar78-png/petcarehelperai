Best Aquarium Lights

Guide to the best aquarium lighting for planted tanks, reef tanks, and freshwater aquariums. Covers LED, spectrum, timing, and plant growth requirements.

Light is a plant and coral tool, and a viewing tool

An aquarium lamp has two jobs that pull in different directions. Fish-only tanks mostly need a fixture you can look at in the evening: even coverage, a color that makes fish look like themselves, and a timer so the room goes dark at night. Planted freshwater tanks and reef tanks need photosynthetically active radiation, usually talked about as PAR, because plants and the algae inside corals use those wavelengths to build tissue. Watts and "super bright" marketing tell you about electricity and about glare. They barely tell you whether a cryptocoryne or a small-polyp stony coral will get what it needs at the substrate. Shop by the organisms you keep, the depth of the water, and a timer you will actually program.

Kelvin is a color-temperature label. Numbers around 6500K are often sold as daylight for freshwater plants, and higher or bluer numbers show up on reef fixtures. Kelvin alone does not set PAR. Two bars with the same Kelvin can throw very different light at 18 inches of depth. If a listing prints a PAR map at a stated distance, that map is more honest than a color adjective. If it prints neither PAR nor the spectrum, you are buying a lamp for viewing, and you should stock the tank with fish and plants that tolerate modest light.

Freshwater planted tanks

Low-light plants such as Anubias, Java fern, and many Cryptocoryne species can grow under a modest LED bar on a timer. Demanding stem plants and a carpet want more intensity, and they usually want carbon dioxide and a fertilizer plan to match. Intensity without nutrients and without a limit on hours is how hair algae and green water take a tank that was handsome for three weeks. Start a new planted setup at a moderate output for about six to eight hours, and lengthen or brighten only when the plants show pale, stretched growth and you have the dosing and water-change habits to support it.

Spread matters. A tiny spotlight over the center leaves the corners dim, and those corners either stay bare or grow algae film where the plants gave up. Choose a fixture close to the length of the tank, or a pair of bars, so the front glass and the back glass both get light. Raise the fixture if the surface plants bleach. Lower it, or raise the percentage on a dimmable driver, if lower leaves yellow while the photoperiod is already reasonable. Rinse the splash lens during water changes. Mineral crust on the plastic quietly eats output, and people then buy a second light for a problem a paper towel would have fixed.

Fish-only freshwater

A community tank of tetras, rasboras, or livebearers can look excellent under a simple LED with a warm-to-neutral color and a low to moderate setting. The fish need a day-night cycle more than they need blast-level output. Too much light stresses some species, fades contrasting colors by washing the scene, and grows algae on the glass you then scrape every other day. A planted corner of Anubias on driftwood is compatible with that calmer light. Plastic ornaments do not need PAR at all. They need a fixture you can clean and a cord with a drip loop so water running down the wire misses the outlet.

Nocturnal fish such as many knifefish and some catfish appreciate a dim path and real darkness at night. A moonlight or blue-only channel, kept very low, lets you observe a feeding without flooding the room in white. Leave that channel on a second timer with a short window. An all-night bright blue glow is still light, and it can interrupt the dark period the animals use. If you are trying to view fish color for a photograph, use the white channel briefly and then return to the normal schedule. Consistency beats a dramatic setting you change every evening.

Reef tanks and why a reptile tube stays out

Reef fixtures are built to push PAR through seawater and to offer a blue-heavy spectrum that fluorescent proteins in corals respond to, along with enough of the other wavelengths the coral's symbiotic algae use. Shoppers compare spread, shimmer, dimming channels, and whether the fixture can be programmed to ramp up and down so fish are spared a sudden noon switch. Shallow soft-coral tanks can succeed with less intensity than a deep tank full of small-polyp stony corals. Chasing the highest PAR on day one bleaches new corals and grows film algae on the rock. New frags usually want a lower percentage and a period of adjustment, with alkalinity, calcium, and magnesium tested so the light has chemistry to work with.

A reptile UVB basking tube is the wrong object over a fish tank. Those lamps are designed for terrestrial enclosures and a lizard's vitamin D pathway, and the fixture, the moisture rating, and the spectrum are aimed at that job. Corals and freshwater plants are shopping for PAR in a waterproof or splash-rated aquarium fixture. Leave the reptile tube on the reptile cage. If a reef light advertises a small UV channel for coral color, read it as a reef feature with a marine manual, and keep it separate in your mind from the UVB percentage printed on a desert-lizard box. Salt spray kills cheap drivers. Pick a fixture with a documented water rating and a mount that holds it above the splash line.

Timers, algae, and heat

Plug every aquarium light into a timer or use the fixture's own clock. A photoperiod that drifts from six hours to fourteen, depending on when someone remembers the switch, grows algae and confuses fish. Many freshwater keepers do well at roughly seven or eight hours once the tank is mature. Reef keepers often ramp for an hour, hold a peak, and ramp down, with the total bright period set by the corals they have. If algae surges, shorten the period and review feeding and water changes before you add a stronger bar. Algae is a nutrient and light balance, and a new lamp is the expensive way to study that lesson twice.

LEDs run cooler than old metal-halide reef lights, which matters in a small room, yet the driver still needs air. Enclosed canopy hoods that trap heat cook both the electronics and the surface of the water. Leave a gap or use a fan the fixture's instructions allow. Metal halide and older high-output fluorescent systems still grow coral and plants, and they cost more in bulbs and electricity. If you already own a healthy T5 high-output setup with fresh bulbs on a timer, a conversion to LED can wait until the bulbs and the ballast are actually due. Bulb age matters on fluorescents: output falls long before the tube looks dark.

Size the fixture and the budget

Match the length to the tank. A 12-inch clip light on a 36-inch aquarium leaves most of the scape in twilight. Clip-on lights for small desktop tanks are often priced near $25 to $70. Mid-size dimmable freshwater bars frequently land around $80 to $220, depending on length and whether the maker publishes a PAR map. Reef-capable lights commonly run from about $200 into several hundred dollars, and very large systems cost more. Treat those figures as ordinary shelf bands. A controller, a mounting arm, and a spare power supply add to the real total.

LEDs dim over years. When a planted tank that used to pearl now grows lanky at the same percentage, or a reef looks duller at the same program, the diodes may be aging, the lens may be scaled, or the glass cover may be etched. Clean first, then decide. A glass lid blocks a slice of light and also keeps fish in and evaporation down. Factor that lid into the height you mount the lamp. Write the program down, including the hours, so a power outage does not leave you guessing why the algae changed in April.

Aquarium light questions

Is a brighter aquarium light always better for plants?

Plants need PAR matched to the species, the fertilizers, and the carbon dioxide you actually supply. A brighter bar on a low-tech tank of Anubias often grows algae faster than it grows leaves. Start moderate, run a steady six to eight hours on a timer, and increase output when plants look stretched and your water-change habit can support the extra growth.

Can I use a reptile UVB bulb over a fish tank?

Leave reptile UVB tubes on reptile enclosures. They are built for terrestrial basking and a lizard's vitamin D needs, and the fixture is a poor match for splash and for coral or plant PAR. Fish tanks want an aquarium light chosen for viewing, plants, or reef spectrum. A blue channel on a reef fixture is a marine feature, and it follows the reef manual.

How many hours should aquarium lights stay on?

Many freshwater tanks do well around seven or eight hours once they are established, with a timer so the length stays put. Reef tanks often ramp up and down around a peak set for the corals in the tank. If algae takes over, shorten the day and review feeding before you buy more output. Give fish a real dark period overnight.
