Addison's Disease in Dogs

Understanding Addison's disease (hypoadrenocorticism) in dogs. Covers symptoms, crisis situations, lifelong treatment, and daily management.

What Addison's disease is

Addison's disease, primary hypoadrenocorticism, is a failure of the adrenal cortex. In most dogs the cortex is destroyed by an immune attack, so the glands stop making enough cortisol and enough aldosterone. Cortisol is the hormone that lets a dog handle stress, illness, and a normal blood sugar. Aldosterone keeps sodium and potassium in balance and supports blood volume. Without both, a dog can look vaguely sick for weeks and then collapse in an Addisonian crisis. Atypical Addison's spares aldosterone at first, so the electrolytes look normal and the only missing hormone is cortisol. That version is easier to miss and just as real.

The disease is famous for imitating other illnesses. Vomiting and diarrhea look like gastroenteritis. High kidney values look like kidney failure. A slow heart rate in a collapsed dog looks like a cardiac emergency. The clue is the combination, plus electrolytes that do not match a simple case of dietary upset. Surgery does not restart the adrenal cortex. Lifelong hormone replacement does.

Which dogs get it

Young and middle-aged dogs are the usual patients, and females are slightly over-represented in many series. Standard Poodles, Portuguese Water Dogs, Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retrievers, Bearded Collies, Great Danes, West Highland White Terriers, Leonbergers, and Soft Coated Wheaten Terriers carry known breed risk. Mixed-breed dogs still make up a large share of cases, so a lack of a fancy pedigree should not talk anyone out of the test. A dog of any age with waxing and waning vomiting, especially if previous "stomach bugs" resolved suspiciously fast after fluids, belongs on the list.

Secondary hypoadrenocorticism, from a pituitary problem or from sudden withdrawal of long-term steroids, is less common than primary disease. It tends to lack the classic sodium and potassium shifts because aldosterone is still present. The veterinarian separates these with the history and the lab pattern. Giving a steroid the morning of the test, unless the clinician ordered that exact drug, can make the result unreadable.

Signs owners notice

The chronic picture is a dog who is intermittently lethargic, shaky, off food, vomiting, or passing soft stool, then seems fine for days. Thirst and urination may increase. Weight drifts down. Owners often describe a dog who seems unlike himself before a crisis, and the waxing course is the reason the disease gets called the great pretender. In crisis the dog collapses, the gums are pale or slow to refill, the belly may be painful, and the heart rate can be inappropriately slow for a dog in shock. That slow rate is a clue to high potassium. Some dogs arrive hypoglycemic and weak. A few have been treated for kidney disease because the blood urea was high from dehydration, while the urine stayed dilute.

  • Repeated "stomach" episodes that improve with fluids and then return.
  • Shaking, weakness, or a dog who cannot stand after a minor stress such as boarding.
  • Collapse, pale gums, and a heart rate that seems too slow for how sick the dog looks.
  • Vomiting with a low temperature, or with diarrhea that leaves the dog flat.
  • A waxing illness in a predisposed breed that nobody has explained with a fecal test.

How a veterinarian diagnoses it

Addison's disease, primary hypoadrenocorticism, is confirmed with an ACTH stimulation test. Cortisol does not rise appropriately after synthetic ACTH. A steroid given before that test makes the result unreadable. A single baseline cortisol can make the disease unlikely, and it does not prove it.

  • Chemistry: Hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, azotemia, and sometimes hypoglycemia. Those numbers suggest the crisis. The ACTH stimulation test is the confirmation.
  • CBC: A sick dog with Addison's often lacks the white-cell pattern expected in stress.
  • Urine: The urine may stay dilute while the blood urea is high, which fits the sodium loss and does not replace the ACTH stimulation test.

A baseline cortisol above the lab's decision threshold makes Addison's unlikely and lets the clinician chase other causes. A low baseline cortisol only says the test must continue. Synthetic ACTH is given and cortisol is measured again. Failure to stimulate is the confirmation of cortisol deficiency. Electrolytes then help separate typical disease, which needs mineralocorticoid replacement, from atypical disease, which may need only a glucocorticoid at first and still needs monitoring in case aldosterone fails later. An abdominal ultrasound can show small adrenal glands and is supportive. It does not replace the stimulation test.

Treatment categories

An Addisonian crisis is treated in hospital with intravenous fluids and an injectable glucocorticoid. Afterward the dog needs lifelong replacement of the missing adrenal hormones, a mineralocorticoid and a glucocorticoid, with electrolyte rechecks. The veterinarian chooses both drugs and both doses. Surgery does not restart the adrenal cortex.

Crisis fluids are chosen to fit the sodium and potassium on the blood gas or chemistry panel, and glucose is added if the dog is hypoglycemic. The injectable glucocorticoid covers the cortisol gap while the dog cannot take a pill. Once the dog is stable, mineralocorticoid replacement is usually either an injectable desoxycorticosterone pivalate, often called DOCP, given on an interval the electrolytes dictate, or oral fludrocortisone. A glucocorticoid such as prednisone covers the cortisol need, at a maintenance amount the veterinarian sets, with a written stress plan for boarding, surgery, illness, or hard exercise. Electrolyte panels are timed across the injection interval while the amount and the spacing are being adjusted. A dog who is vomiting and cannot keep the glucocorticoid pill down needs the injectable plan the clinic provided for that situation, and needs a phone call the same day.

Home care

Do not skip the mineralocorticoid because the dog "looks great." The look is the medication working. Give the glucocorticoid with food if the clinic directed that, and pack extra doses for travel using only the stress instructions you were given. Teach every pet sitter the name of the disease and the location of the injectable glucocorticoid if one was dispensed. Keep the electrolyte recheck on the calendar. Weigh the dog monthly and watch for a return of shaking, vomiting, or weakness, which can mean the replacement is short or the dog is losing pills. A medical alert tag that says hypoadrenocorticism saves time if a stranger finds the dog collapsed. Before boarding or a dental procedure, tell that clinic so the stress glucocorticoid can be arranged ahead of the day.

When it is an emergency

When to See the Vet Immediately

Collapse, repeated vomiting, inability to stand, pale gums, a slow heartbeat, or profound weakness is an emergency in a dog with known Addison's disease and in a dog who has never been tested. A crisis can be the first sign. Tell the emergency team about any steroid already given so they can interpret the cortisol test. Do not wait overnight to see if a collapsed dog "sleeps it off." High potassium can disturb the heart rhythm, and shock from sodium and volume loss is treated with fluids in the hospital. A larger dinner cannot replace those fluids.

What to ask about the bill

Primary hypoadrenocorticism stays for life. The adrenal cortex does not start making cortisol and aldosterone again. Treatment is lifelong replacement: a mineralocorticoid such as DOCP or fludrocortisone plus a glucocorticoid, guided by electrolyte checks. A dog that is stable on that plan can live out a normal lifespan.

Price Addison's disease as a monthly injection and an electrolyte panel, then multiply by twelve. The crisis hospitalization, with IV fluids before anyone has the diagnosis, is the separate emergency bill. Ask what one DOCP injection and one electrolyte recheck cost at your hospital. Ignore a national average. The dose itself comes from those blood results.

Key Questions

Can a dog live a normal life with Addison's disease?

Many dogs do, once the crisis is over and the replacement is steady. The adrenal cortex does not recover. The lifespan can still be ordinary if DOCP or fludrocortisone continues, the glucocorticoid is given, and electrolytes are rechecked when the clinic asks. The dangerous stretch is a missed injection or a vomiting dog who cannot absorb the pill and does not get the injectable backup.

Why is the ACTH stimulation test required?

Electrolyte changes suggest the crisis and they do not prove the diagnosis. Kidney failure, whipworms, and reproductive problems can shift sodium and potassium too. A baseline cortisol can make Addison's unlikely when it is clearly normal. Only the failure of cortisol to rise after synthetic ACTH confirms that the cortex cannot respond. Prednisone and other steroids given first can cross-react or suppress the axis and waste the test.

What is a stress dose?

It is a temporary increase in the glucocorticoid, decided by the veterinarian, for surgery, illness, boarding, or heavy exertion. The mineralocorticoid plan usually stays on its own schedule. Owners should follow the written instructions from their own clinic. Copying another dog's tablet count is unsafe because the amount is individualized and this page does not provide one.
