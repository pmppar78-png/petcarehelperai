Cat Upper Respiratory Infections

Guide to URI in cats covering herpesvirus, calicivirus, symptoms, home care, when antibiotics are needed, and preventing spread in multi-cat homes.

Herpesvirus and calicivirus cause most of these colds

A sneezing cat with watery eyes usually has an upper respiratory infection, and two viruses cause the majority. Feline herpesvirus-1 settles in the nerves after the first illness and can flare whenever the cat is stressed, boarded, or already sick with something else. Feline calicivirus more often adds ulcers on the tongue and the roof of the mouth, and some strains make a cat lame for a few days or, in shelter outbreaks, cause a much more severe systemic disease. Chlamydia felis tends to produce marked conjunctivitis. Bordetella and Mycoplasma show up as bacterial players, sometimes alone and sometimes on top of a virus. The FVRCP vaccine, which includes herpesvirus and calicivirus, lowers the chance of severe disease. It does not guarantee that a vaccinated cat will never sneeze, and it does not stop every carrier from shedding.

Kittens from shelters, outdoor cats, and households that just added a new animal are the classic patients. The new cat may have looked fine in the carrier and started sneezing five days later. Isolation for a week or two when a cat enters the home, with a separate bowl and a washed pair of hands, limits what the resident cats receive. Herpesvirus is fragile in the environment compared with calicivirus, which can persist on dishes and needs a disinfectant your veterinarian or shelter protocol actually names. Bleach used carelessly around cats is its own hazard. Follow a written dilution and rinse, or use a veterinary surface product.

Signs, including the ones that are no longer a simple cold

  • Sneezing fits, nasal discharge that shifts from clear to yellow or green, and crusted nostrils.
  • Watery or sticky eyes, squinting, or a cat who hates having the face cleaned.
  • Ulcers on the tongue, drooling, and a refusal to eat because the mouth hurts, more typical of calicivirus.
  • Fever, lethargy, and a voice that sounds hoarse.
  • A corneal ulcer from herpesvirus: a painful, squinted eye that needs a stain before any drop goes in.

Chronic "snufflers" may have scarred turbinates inside the nose after a bad kittenhood infection, a polyp, or a tooth root that has become infected and is draining into the nasal cavity. A cat who has sneezed for months is past the acute virus handout. That cat needs an oral exam, and sometimes imaging, looking for a polyp or a dental source. Allergic rhinitis is uncommon in cats next to these infectious and structural causes. A senior cat with a one-sided bloody nasal discharge needs a workup for a mass or a foreign body. Another week of the same lysine powder will not answer that.

Breathing and appetite thresholds

Open-mouth breathing, a blue or gray tongue, a kitten who will not eat for a day, or an adult who skips more than a day of food should be seen the same day. Cats who stop eating can develop hepatic lipidosis on top of the respiratory disease. Do not put a steroid drop or a leftover human eye ointment into a squinted eye. Herpes can ulcerate the cornea, and a steroid on an ulcer can deepen it. The clinic places a fluorescein stain first. Steam from a bathroom can loosen nasal crusts while you travel. It does not replace oxygen if the cat is working to breathe.

What the veterinarian actually does

Many first episodes are diagnosed from the exam: sneezing, fever, and discharge from the eyes or nose. A PCR panel for herpesvirus, calicivirus, Chlamydia, Bordetella, and Mycoplasma is useful when the cat is severe, when the signs have become chronic, or when a shelter is trying to stop an outbreak. The panel has limits. Herpesvirus sheds intermittently, so a negative herpes PCR on a single swab does not prove the cat has never had herpes. Treatment of a routine case is supportive. Clean the eyes and nostrils with warm saline on a cotton round, offer strong-smelling food warmed slightly, and correct dehydration. An antibiotic is added when the veterinarian suspects a bacterial component, such as Chlamydia or a thick nasal infection, and the drug and the length of the course are theirs to set. Antibiotics do not clear herpesvirus or calicivirus. Antiviral medicine such as famciclovir is a veterinary decision for some herpes cases, with a dose calculated for that cat. Published reviews have failed to show a dependable benefit from lysine supplements, so buying a large tub is optional at best and worth a conversation before you count on it.

Hospital care, fluids, and a feeding plan enter the picture when a cat is too congested to smell food or too painful from oral ulcers to swallow. A corneal ulcer is treated as an eye emergency alongside the sneeze. Never use a drop that contains a steroid unless the veterinarian has stained the eye and prescribed that drop for a non-ulcer problem. Recheck a cat who is still sick after the expected few days to ten days, or sooner if eating stops.

After the acute week

Herpesvirus stays in the body. A flare can follow a move, a new pet, a dental procedure, or a stay at a boarding facility. Reducing stress, keeping vaccines current on the veterinarian's schedule, and treating flares early are the long plan. Calicivirus carriers can shed for months. In a breeding or shelter setting, the veterinarian or shelter medicine lead should design the flow of animals and the disinfection, because a household tip about vinegar will not hold up to calicivirus. Kittens should receive the FVRCP series the veterinarian times to maternal antibody, then boosters as advised. That series is feline. It has no connection to a dog's kennel-cough or distemper appointments.

At home during a mild flare, isolate the sick cat from kittens and from elderly cats, wash hands, and keep the room warm and free of strong scents that irritate the nose. Use a humidifier if the air is dry. Offer several foods. A cat who starts eating and whose fever breaks is on the right track, and a cat who sits over the bowl and walks away needs a scale and a phone call. Weight loss of even a few ounces matters in a kitten.

Costs

A mild adult cat is often an exam, a stain of the eye, and supportive care. PCR panels, radiographs for a chronic nasal case, and hospitalization for a kitten who will not eat are the steps that raise the bill. Ask what the quote includes before you decline the eye stain. A missed corneal ulcer costs more than the stain. Prices vary with region and with after-hours care.

Questions about feline upper respiratory infections

Will this cold come back every time my cat is stressed?

Feline herpesvirus remains in the nerves and can flare with stress, illness, or boarding, so some cats do sneeze again at those moments. Calicivirus behaves differently and is more likely to cause mouth ulcers during the illness you can see. A cat who never really stops sneezing needs a check for polyps, tooth-root disease, and scarred nasal passages. The veterinarian can also time an FVRCP booster, which reduces severe disease and still leaves some vaccinated cats able to sneeze.

Are antibiotics the cure?

Antibiotics treat bacterial contributors such as Chlamydia or a secondary nasal infection. They do not clear herpesvirus or calicivirus. Many cats improve with cleaning, humidity, and food they can smell, and some need an antiviral the veterinarian calculates for herpes. Do not use a leftover dog antibiotic, and do not put a steroid in the eye before a fluorescein stain.

When is a sneeze an emergency?

Open-mouth breathing, a kitten too weak to nurse or eat, an adult who skips food for more than a day, or an eye that is clamped shut should be seen the same day. Hepatic lipidosis can follow anorexia in an overweight cat, and a corneal ulcer can worsen quickly. Bathroom steam is a comfort measure on the way to the clinic. It is a poor plan for a cat who is already gasping.
