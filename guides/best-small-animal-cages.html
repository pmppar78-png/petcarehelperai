Best Small Animal Cages and Habitats

Top cages for rabbits, guinea pigs, hamsters, and ferrets. Covers minimum sizes, bedding options, enrichment, and multi-level setups.

Floor space is the specification that matters

Small-animal cages are sold by cute levels and by "stories." The animals live on floor area, on a solid surface under their feet, and on time outside if the species needs it. A tall plastic palace with three tiny platforms still fails a rabbit whose body barely fits on one landing. Before you compare colors, write down the species, the adult size, and whether this animal is happier with a companion of its own kind. Then buy the footprint that welfare groups and experienced keepers keep repeating, even when the pet-store box says starter.

Many rabbit welfare groups treat about 12 square feet of pen as a minimum living area for one rabbit, plus several hours of safe exercise outside that pen every day. A single guinea pig is commonly given at least about 7.5 square feet, and two guinea pigs at least about 10.5 square feet, because they are social and should live with a compatible partner. Syrian hamsters are often housed in an unbroken floor of at least about 600 square inches, which is in the neighborhood of a 40-gallon breeder tank, with bedding deep enough to burrow. Dwarf hamsters still need a large continuous floor and a wheel sized for their backs. Ferrets are long, flexible escape artists who need a multi-level cage only as a bedroom, plus hours in a ferret-proofed room. These figures are widely used floors. A bigger, well-designed space is a kindness. A smaller "apartment" from a big-box shelf is the purchase people replace after the first month of guilt and chewed bars.

Rabbits

An exercise pen made of wire grids, or a C&C grid pen with a solid coroplast base, usually gives a rabbit more honest floor than a traditional hutch. The base has to be solid. Wire bottoms bruise heels and cause sore hocks that are slow to heal. The pen should be tall enough that a jump does not clear it, or it should have a secure top if the rabbit is an athlete. Provide a litter box big enough to lie in, a hay pile that is the center of the diet, a hide, and a heavy water bowl or a bottle that actually flows. Rabbits chew cords the way other animals chew kibble. Protect every cable in the room they exercise in, and block the backs of sofas where they disappear and also chew the dust cover.

Indoor housing beats a lonely outdoor hutch for most pet rabbits. Temperature swings, flies, and predators make backyard hutches a harder welfare problem than the catalog suggests. If a rabbit lives outdoors, the structure needs shade, insulation from heat as much as from cold, a raised dry floor, and hardware that stops raccoons. Many rabbits do well with a bonded partner, and many fights start when two strangers are dropped into a new pen on the same afternoon. Bond on neutral ground with a plan, or house them separately until a rabbit-savvy mentor or veterinarian helps. Spay and neuter, which are veterinary decisions, change both the housing smell and the odds of a peaceful pair.

Guinea pigs

Guinea pigs are social, heavy for their size, and messy in a way that rewards a wide, open pen you can wipe daily. C&C grids with a coroplast base, or a large solid-bottom cage, work better than a small wire hamster cage with a tube. They need room for two, because a lone guinea pig is a welfare problem unless a veterinarian has a reason for separation. Hide boxes should have two exits so a bossy pig cannot trap a shy one. Fleece liners can work if you shake them out, wash them often, and still feed hay in a pile or a rack. Paper bedding works if the dust is low and the layer stays dry. Cedar and strongly aromatic softwoods are a poor idea in a closed pen. Their oils irritate airways.

Guinea pigs do not use hamster wheels, and a wheel sold for them is usually a back injury waiting. Ramps, if you add a loft, need a gentle slope and side rails. Many keepers skip lofts entirely and give the extra money to more floor. The pen must fit a hay pile the size of the pig, because hay is the main food and also the main litter. A vitamin C plan belongs in the feeding routine, through proper pellets, vegetables, or a veterinary recommendation. The cage's job is to make that hay-and-hide life possible without forcing the animals to stand on wire. Put the pen somewhere you walk by often. Guinea pigs are quiet when they are sick, and a cage in a forgotten laundry room hides the first day they stop eating, which is an emergency for this species.

Hamsters

Hamsters are solitary in ordinary pet care. House adults alone. A Syrian and a dwarf should never share, and two adults of the same species usually fight. The enclosure wants continuous floor, deep bedding, and a solid wheel. Tubes and colorful modules can be an add-on and become a problem when they are the entire home: they are hard to clean, small for an adult Syrian, and frustrating for a burrowing animal. A large bin with ventilation, a glass tank with a secure mesh top, or a cage with bar spacing the hamster cannot squeeze through all can work if the floor area is honestly large. Dwarf hamsters slip through gaps that would hold a Syrian. Measure the bars. If the skull fits, the hamster will be in the sofa.

Wheel diameter matters more than the painted hamster on the package. Syrians generally need a wheel around 11 or 12 inches so the back stays flat while they run. Smaller dwarfs often do well around 8 inches or more, and you should still watch the back. Wire and mesh running surfaces splay feet and cause bumblefoot-type sores. Choose a solid surface. Bedding should be deep enough to tunnel, often several inches or more of paper-based bedding. Avoid cedar. Provide a sand bath for many species, a hide, and chew options that are actually safe wood or cardboard. Water bottles should be tested daily because a stuck ball means a hamster goes thirsty in silence. Place the setup out of direct sun and away from a cat's swatting range. Nocturnal running is normal. A bedroom wheel without a quiet design is a human problem you should solve with placement, and with a solid wheel that does not tick.

Ferrets and mixed households

A ferret cage should be a safe den with solid shelves or ramps that are easy on bare feet, a litter corner, hammocks, and doors a ferret cannot pop. Levels are fun and they are still a small fraction of the day. Ferrets need hours in a room where the recliner is blocked, the dishwasher is latched, and rubber objects are gone. Ferrets swallow rubber and foam, and those foreign bodies become surgery. A cage full of rubber "toys" is a medical risk. Use hard toys and dig boxes the veterinarian would recognize as safe. Ventilation matters, and so does temperature. Ferrets overheat. A cage in a sunny window or next to a radiator is dangerous in summer.

Different species do not share a cage just because they are all small. A rabbit and a guinea pig have different diets, different social needs, and a size difference that can injure the pig. A ferret is a predator. Housing a ferret where it can reach a rabbit, a rat, or a bird is how tragedies happen, even through bars a paw can enter. Give each species its own secure setup and its own exercise time. Rats, if you keep them, are social and need a friend and a cage with small bar spacing, solid platforms, and fall protection. The same floor-first thinking applies, with a layout a rat can climb without a long drop onto wire.

Cleaning, bedding, and what the hardware costs

Spot-clean daily. A full bedding change on a schedule you can keep beats a heroic monthly scrub after the ammonia smell has already irritated lungs. Wash water bottles with a brush. Biofilm in the tube is invisible and real. Keep a spare bottle so one can soak. Litter boxes for rabbits and ferrets should be dumped often enough that the animal still wants to use them. Once a box is offensive, many rabbits choose a corner of the pen and you will blame the rabbit for a cleaning lapse.

C&C grids and coroplast pens often land in a typical range around $60 to $180 depending on size. A basic wire rabbit pen can be similar or higher once you add a solid floor. Hamster tanks and large bins, including a proper lid, often run about $40 to $150. Ferret cages with solid shelves commonly cost about $80 to $250. Those are typical ranges. Used pens are fine when the coating is intact, the door latches, and you can disinfect them. Rust, sharp grid joints, and unknown wood soaked in urine are worth walking past. Spend the leftover money on hay storage, a second water source, and a hide with two doors. The animals will use those every day. They will not read the story count on the box you left in the garage.

Habitat questions

Is a pet-store starter cage big enough for a rabbit?

Most starter hutches are smaller than the floor many welfare groups ask for, about 12 square feet plus daily exercise, and a lot of them have wire that is hard on heels. An exercise pen or a C&C grid with a solid base usually gives a more honest home. Add a hide, a large litter box, and unlimited hay, and protect electrical cords in the exercise room.

Can guinea pigs and rabbits live in one pen?

House them separately. Diets, body size, and social needs differ, and a rabbit can injure a guinea pig even during play. Guinea pigs should have a compatible guinea pig partner and a wide solid-floor pen, commonly at least about 10.5 square feet for two. Rabbits need their own space, their own hay setup, and a bonding plan that does not involve the pig.

What hamster cage should I avoid?

Avoid homes made mostly of narrow tubes, wire wheels, cedar bedding, and bar gaps a dwarf can walk through. Aim for a large continuous floor, often discussed around 600 square inches for a Syrian, with deep paper bedding and a solid wheel big enough that the back stays straight. Keep adult hamsters alone, and check the water bottle every day.
