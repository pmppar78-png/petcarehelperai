Cat Ear Infections Guide

Ear infections in cats covering bacterial, yeast, and ear mite infections. Covers symptoms, treatment, cleaning techniques, and prevention.

Mites, yeast, and bacteria are different ear problems

A cat who shakes her head and scratches at an ear may have ear mites, a yeast infection, a bacterial infection, or a mix that only a smear will sort out. Otodectes cynotis, the ear mite, is common in kittens, outdoor cats, and multi-cat homes. The debris is often dark, dry, and crumbly, like coffee grounds, and both ears usually itch. That look can fool you. Yeast and bacteria also make dark or brown discharge, sometimes with a sour or sweet odor, and they may affect one ear when a polyp, a foreign body, or an allergy is driving that side. The Companion Animal Parasite Council describes Otodectes as a parasite a veterinarian-chosen product can clear. Cytology, a stained slide of the debris, is what shows mites versus yeast cells versus bacteria shaped like rods or cocci. Treatment that skips the slide treats the wrong organism.

Ear mites spread by contact. If one cat in the house is confirmed, the veterinarian will usually treat the other cats, and sometimes the dogs, because dogs can carry the same mite. People are rarely affected. Yeast otitis and bacterial otitis stay in an ear that is already inflamed. They do not sweep through a household the way mites do. Food allergy, environmental allergy, a nasopharyngeal polyp in a young cat, a tumor in an older cat, and disease behind the eardrum all keep the canal wet and sore. A single bottle of drops, used until the bottle is empty and never rechecked, is how those ears become a monthly cycle.

What you see at home

  • Head shaking, ear flicking, or a cat who cries when the base of the ear is touched.
  • Dark crumbly debris, or a moist brown, yellow, or black discharge with a noticeable smell.
  • Scratches and hair loss in front of the ear, sometimes with a swollen face if a scratch abscess follows.
  • A puffy, fluid-filled ear flap, an aural hematoma, after days of violent shaking.
  • A head tilt, circling, or a cat who cannot walk straight, which can mean the middle or inner ear is involved.

Kittens from barns and shelters deserve a mite check even when the owner has already cleaned the ear. Cleaning before the visit can wipe away the evidence and push debris deeper. Wipe only what you can see on the outer flap, and leave the canal for the clinic. Older cats with a new one-sided discharge need the polyp-and-mass question asked out loud. White cats and cats who go outdoors can also get sun damage and crusting on the ear tips, which is a skin problem of the pinna and a different visit from canal infection.

Go now

A head tilt, stumbling, an eye that flickers, or severe pain means the infection may have reached the middle or inner ear. A hematoma that is hot and tight, or any cat who stops eating because the ear hurts, should be seen promptly. Do not put a dog's leftover ear drop into a cat. Some ingredients used in canine products are dangerous to cats, and any drop is unsafe if the eardrum has ruptured. The veterinarian checks that membrane with an otoscope first.

How the clinic separates the causes

The exam starts with an otoscope and a cytology slide. Mites, eggs, yeast, and bacteria show up differently, and the drug follows the slide. If the canal is too painful or too swollen to see the eardrum, the cat may need sedation and a cleaning under that sedation so the veterinarian can look again. A young cat with a recurrent ear, a noisy gulp, or a mass in the canal is checked for a nasopharyngeal polyp, sometimes with imaging. Chronic cases that refill every few weeks may need radiographs, CT, or a deep culture, because a resistant bacterium or a pocket of disease in the middle ear will not yield to another week of the same drop. Allergy testing is a later conversation. A food trial, run exactly as the veterinarian writes it, comes ahead of a pile of supplements when food is still a live possibility.

Treatment the veterinarian chooses

Mites are treated with a parasiticide labeled and dosed by the veterinarian, often applied so that every in-contact cat is covered for the life cycle of the mite. Recheck the ears. Dark debris should stop being produced once the mites are dead, though old wax can linger and a cleaning may still be needed. Bacterial and yeast otitis get a medication matched to the cytology, and the veterinarian sets the drug and the amount. You will usually clean or the clinic will clean on a schedule, then apply the medicine so it can reach skin. Stopping the day the smell fades, with medicine left in the bottle, is how yeast returns. A polyp may be removed by traction or by a surgery of the middle ear, and that is a surgical estimate with its own risks. An aural hematoma may be drained or surgically repaired so the ear flap does not scar into a cauliflower shape. Pain control is part of these visits. Do not give a dog pain tablet, a human pain tablet, or a leftover steroid. Cats are harmed by several drugs that dogs tolerate.

Home cleaning, once the veterinarian has shown you the technique, uses a cat-safe cleanser and a cotton ball on the flap. Cotton swabs in the canal pack wax against the eardrum and cause more damage. If the cat fights hard, stop. A struggling cat and a bottle of cleanser near the face is how cleaner ends up in an eye. Ask the clinic about a towel wrap or a tech visit for cleanings.

Keeping the next infection smaller

Treat mites in the whole household the first time, then watch for scratching over the following month. For yeast and bacteria, the prevention is the underlying cause: a completed food trial if one was prescribed, flea control if the cat is allergic to fleas and traumatizing the ears, and a recheck cytology so you know the slide is clear. Cats with polyps or chronic middle-ear disease need the surgical or medical plan finished. Routine poking in a healthy ear does not prevent disease and can create it. Annual exams, sooner if the cat is senior or already has allergic skin disease, catch odor and debris before a hematoma forms. Indoor life lowers the chance of a new mite exposure from neighborhood cats.

What ears cost

A mite case with an exam, a slide, and a veterinarian-selected parasiticide is often the lower bill, especially if everyone in the house is treated before complications start. Yeast or bacterial otitis costs more because of cytology, recleaning, and rechecks. Culture, imaging, and polyp surgery are the expensive branch, and they are justified when the canal keeps refilling. Prices move with the city. Ask whether the quote you were given assumes mites. If the slide shows yeast or rods, a mite-only package is the wrong budget.

Questions about cat ears

How do I tell ear mites from a yeast infection?

You cannot tell reliably from the color of the wax. Mites often make dark crumbly debris and intense itch in kittens and outdoor cats, and yeast can look just as dark and may smell stronger. Cytology is the separation. The veterinarian looks for mites and eggs or for yeast cells and bacteria, then picks the drug. Treating the whole household makes sense for mites. It does not fix a polyp or a food allergy driving yeast.

Why does the ear infection keep coming back?

A canal that refills usually still has a cause: a polyp, disease past the eardrum, a food reaction, or a medication that was stopped when the smell improved and the organism was still there. Recheck cytology, and ask about imaging if a young cat's ear never stays clear. Do not restart a dog's leftover drops. The eardrum has to be intact, and the drug has to be safe for cats.

Should I clean my cat's ears every week?

Healthy ears do not need a weekly swab. Clean only the way a veterinarian has shown you, and only when debris is part of a diagnosed problem. Cotton swabs in the canal push material deeper. If your cat hates the process, book a technician visit. Force and a cleanser bottle are how eyes and eardrums get injured.
