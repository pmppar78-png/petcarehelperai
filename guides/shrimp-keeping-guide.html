Freshwater Shrimp Keeping

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Guide to keeping freshwater shrimp including Cherry Shrimp, Amano Shrimp, and Crystal Red Shrimp. Covers tank setup, parameters, and breeding.

Housing and Enclosure

Freshwater dwarf shrimp split into two keeping styles, and the water decides which one you can do. Neocaridina, the cherry, blue dream, and yellow shrimp sold everywhere, settle into a cycled tank of 10 gallons or more at 65 to 80 F and a pH around 6.5 to 8.0. A steady room in that band often needs no heater. Add one only after a thermometer shows the water slipping under the mid 60s. Caridina, including crystal reds and Taiwan bees, want softer, more acidic water and a keeper who already knows how to hold GH, KH, and temperature still. Give them their own tank once you can hold soft acidic water still. Mixing them into a cherry colony fights both mineral targets.

Five gallons can hold a few shrimp and will swing every time you change a mug of water. Ten to twenty gallons forgives a missed day and gives shrimplets somewhere to hide. Use a sponge filter. A hang-on-back intake will pull in babies unless it is wrapped in foam. Mature the tank before the shrimp arrive. Biofilm on wood, leaf litter, and moss is both food and cover, and a brand-new sterile scape has neither. Indian almond leaves, mulberry leaves, and cholla release tannins and grow the film shrimp graze all day.

Active substrate buffers Caridina tanks. Neocaridina are usually happier on inert gravel or sand plus mineral stones if your tap water is already in range. Test GH and KH along with pH. A failed molt often traces back to a hardness crash after a huge water change. Change small amounts, match temperature, and drip-acclimate new shrimp for an hour or more. Pouring a bag of crystals into tap water is how colonies vanish on night one.

Fish are the other housing decision. Amano shrimp are larger and can share with calm community fish. Cherry shrimp in a tank with guppies or bettas become expensive snacks, and the shrimplets disappear first. A species tank with moss, or a tank whose fish are too small to bother adults, is the honest setup. Keep Neocaridina and Caridina apart. Their mineral needs conflict, and the cross does nothing useful for either colony.

Diet and Nutrition

A mature colony eats mostly what grows in the tank. Supplemental food is still required once you have more than a handful of shrimp or the scape is young. Shrimp pellets, a slice of blanched zucchini, spinach, or kale, and a dried mulberry or Indian almond leaf cover the week. Offer protein such as frozen bloodworms or a tiny pinch of fish food once or twice a week, and remove whatever is left after a few hours so it does not foul a small tank.

Overfeeding is the usual mess. If the shrimp ignore a pellet because the moss is loaded with biofilm, skip the pellet. If they swarm the food and strip it in minutes, they needed it. Mineral intake for the shell comes from the water and from a varied diet. Products such as Salty Shrimp remineralize RO water for keepers who mix their own. They are a water additive for GH and KH, aimed at the molt.

Many fish medicines and some plant fertilizers list copper. That metal kills shrimp at levels fish tolerate. Read every label, including the snail killer and the "all-in-one" plant mix, before it goes near the colony. A fertilizer that was safe last year may have a new formula.

Health and Veterinary

Failed molts, ammonia, copper, and hitchhiker parasites explain most shrimp losses. A shrimp that dies halfway out of its old shell, or a colony that starts dropping after a large water change, is often dealing with unstable GH or a sudden shift in dissolved minerals. Keep hardness steady and change water in small portions. Ammonia and nitrite are harsher on shrimp than on many fish. Add the colony only after the tank is fully cycled and those two readings sit at zero. A dead shrimp left in the moss is enough to break that zero in a nano tank, so count heads when you do maintenance.

Copper poisoning shows up after a treatment or a fertilizer dose, sometimes with no other clue. Parasites such as Scutariella, the little white worms on the rostrum, and fuzzy growths such as Vorticella usually arrive with new shrimp. Quarantine newcomers in a separate cycled container before they join a display colony you care about. Bacterial infections follow the same route, or follow a tank that has been overfed until the water smells sweet. The Merck Veterinary Manual's aquarium-fish management chapter is the veterinary reference for these water-quality and copper warnings, including the way copper also harms snails and plants and can stall the bacteria in a filter.

A healthy shrimp is out grazing, antennae working, and it vanishes into the moss when you tap the glass. A shrimp that stays bright red and frozen on the front pane, or that turns opaque, is in trouble. Berried females carrying eggs under the tail are a good sign once the colony has settled. Dropping eggs on day one usually means the acclimation was too fast. Wash your hands and skip lotion and soap residue. Those films sit on the water surface where shrimp come to pick.

Stocking Without the Crash

  • Cycle until ammonia and nitrite are zero, then wait until you see biofilm on the wood.
  • Drip-acclimate, and keep the lights low for the first day.
  • Start with ten or more Neocaridina so the colony can absorb a loss and still breed.
  • House them away from fish large enough to eat adults, and give shrimplets moss.
  • Quarantine new shrimp even when the seller calls them "pest free."

Breeding Neocaridina in fresh water they already like takes no special trick beyond stability. The eggs hatch as tiny copies of the adults. Caridina can do the same in the right soft water. Amano shrimp are different: their larvae need brackish water, and almost nobody raises them by accident in a community tank. Know which animal you bought before you expect babies.

A Colony That Molts on Schedule

Watch a shrimp through one molt before you call the tank stable. A healthy Neocaridina disappears for a short time, leaves a clear shell in the moss, and returns with a brighter color. A shrimp that dies half out of the old shell, especially after a large water change, is telling you the hardness moved. Change smaller amounts, match temperature, and test GH and KH along with ammonia and nitrite. Both of those nitrogen readings belong at zero before the colony arrives, and they belong at zero the morning after a shrimp dies in the moss.

Feed the biofilm first. If the shrimp ignore a pellet, the wood and the leaves are doing the job. If they strip a pellet in minutes, offer a little more, then remove the leftover. Copper on a fertilizer label or a fish medicine is an emergency you prevent by reading the label first. Drip-acclimate newcomers, quarantine them, and keep Caridina in their own softer water. Amano shrimp can share with calm fish. Cherry shrimp in with a betta are often a snack. Count the colony when you prune plants so you know whether the moss is hiding babies or hiding a loss.

Quick Answers

Is shrimp keeping good for beginners?

Neocaridina such as cherry shrimp are a good start. They do well in a cycled tank of 10 gallons or more with a sponge filter, at 65 to 80°F and pH 6.5 to 8.0. Caridina need softer, more acidic water and more experience.

Why are my shrimp dying after a medication or fertilizer dose?

Copper is lethal to shrimp. Check every medication and fertilizer label before dosing, and never use copper-based fish treatments in a shrimp tank.
