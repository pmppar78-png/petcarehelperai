Best Pet Travel

Covers soft-sided, hard-sided, wheeled, and backpack carriers with size guides for major airlines.

Cabin, cargo, car, and clinic are four different trips

A travel carrier is a closed den with ventilation and a door you can lock. The right one depends on the trip. A soft bag that slides under an airplane seat is for a cat or a small dog whose airline still allows that route. A rigid kennel with ventilation on more than one side is what cargo rules and many long car trips ask for when the animal is larger. A backpack is a walking tool for a cat who tolerates being up high, and a wheeled bag is a hallway tool that some airlines then ask you to de-wheel at the gate. Buying one bag to do all four jobs is how people arrive at the airport with a carrier an inch too tall and a cat who has never seen the zipper.

Measure the animal and the rule, in that order. Length from the tip of the nose to the base of the tail, height from the floor to the top of the head or the ears, whichever the airline counts, and width across the shoulders. The animal should be able to stand, turn around, and lie down in a natural posture inside the carrier. That standard shows up in many airline and welfare guidelines, and it is also the standard that keeps a veterinary ride from becoming a panic oven. Then open the airline's current pet page. Under-seat limits differ by carrier and change. A bag that fit a friend's flight last year can miss today's sizer by half an inch. Print or save the page you relied on.

Soft cabin bags

Soft-sided carriers win the under-seat problem because they flex into a footprint a rigid box cannot. Look for a sturdy frame that still pops back into shape, mesh on more than one face so air moves when the bag is shoved under a seat, and zippers with lock holes. A determined dog can open a cheap zipper from inside with a tooth. Clips on the zipper pulls, or a lock rated for luggage, close that hobby. A fleece liner should come out for washing, because carsickness is part of travel and a sewn-in pad becomes the smell of the next three trips.

Practice with the bag under a chair at home so you know whether the pet can actually lie down once the top is compressed. Some bags advertise a generous height that disappears when the frame folds. If your dog's ears brush the ceiling in the airline shape, you are over the honest size even if the tag says otherwise. A short leash clip inside the door, attached to a harness, stops a bolt at the security table. Do not rely on a neck collar alone for that clip. A cat who backs out of a collar will do it at the worst moment, and a dog can injure a neck if the bag drops while the collar is tied to a rigid point.

Hard kennels and cargo rules

Cargo and some train or long-distance rules point at a rigid plastic or composite kennel: a metal door, ventilation openings on at least three sides, screws that fasten the top to the bottom, and a size that lets the animal stand and turn. Airlines often require food and water crocks that can be refilled from outside, absorbent bedding, and arrows and live-animal markings. Wheels and cup holders sometimes have to come off. Read the list for the airline you will actually fly, including blackout dates and temperature embargoes. Hot-weather embargos and cold-weather embargos are common, and brachycephalic dogs and cats are restricted or refused by many airlines because their airways do not tolerate heat and stress in a hold. If your pet is a Bulldog, a Pug, a Persian, or a similar flat-faced animal, assume cargo is off the table until the airline's own chart says otherwise, and think hard about whether any flight is kind.

Plastic kennels crack when they age in the sun or when a large dog charges the door. Inspect the seam screws before a trip and replace stripped hardware. Wire crates that fold for the living room are a poor cargo crate. They collapse, toes catch, and they rarely meet the written rule. In a car, strap a hard kennel so it cannot become a projectile. A loose crate in a hatchback is a battering ram during a short stop. Offer water at rest stops. Do not leave the pet in the kennel in a parked car while you eat. The plastic holds heat. Ventilation slots move a little air and still leave a parked car dangerous.

Backpacks and wheeled bags

Backpack carriers put a cat or a tiny dog on your back, which some animals prefer to a bag that swings against your knee. The pack needs a structured panel so the pet is held in a stand-and-turn shape, mesh the animal can watch through if watching helps, and a chest strap so the load stays on your hips. Weigh the pet plus the bag before a long walk. A 16-pound cat in a handsome pack is a real load on a warm sidewalk, and the cat still needs an exit plan if you fall. Zippers should lock. A top opening that gapes when you bend over is how a cat ends up on a stranger's awning.

Wheeled carriers save a shoulder in airports and scrape in gravel. Confirm whether your airline counts the extended handle and the wheels inside the under-seat box. Some travelers remove the wheel kit at the gate and stow it. If the wheels do not come off and the bag fails the sizer, you will be checking a pet you meant to keep in the cabin, which may mean the pet cannot fly that day. Test the handle with the pet inside. A high center of gravity tips when you turn, and a tipped bag is terrifying even when the zippers hold.

Teaching the carrier before you need it

The week of a flight is a late time to introduce a cave that eats cats. Leave the carrier open in a room for days, with a familiar cloth and a meal or two served just inside the door. Move the meal farther in. Close the door for a minute while the pet eats, then open it before anyone is upset. Short car rides to nowhere, with a treat at the end, beat a first experience that is already the veterinary clinic. Cats who only see the carrier when it means vaccines learn to vanish under the bed. Dogs who are shoved in headfirst learn to brace at the door. Go slower than your travel date wants you to go, and if the date is fixed, start today with the door open and the pressure low.

Pack a copy of vaccination records, a recent photo, a harness, and a collapsible water cup in the pocket, along with any medication in the original bottle. A label on the carrier should show your phone number and a second number, plus the words that the animal is inside. That label matters in cargo and it matters when a soft bag sits on an airport floor surrounded by other soft bags. Line the bottom with a towel you can throw away if the ride goes badly. Cats and birds in the same carrying space are a separate problem. Birds should travel in their own ventilated carrier, away from fumes and away from a curious paw, because a cat carrier and a bird carrier solve different risks.

Typical prices and the last fit check

Soft under-seat bags often sell in a typical range around $30 to $100. Structured backpacks frequently land around $40 to $140. Rigid airline-style kennels commonly run about $60 to $220, with heavier or larger crates above that. Wheeled versions add cost and sometimes add a gate problem. . Spend the money on zippers, vents, and an honest interior, and spend the time on fit. The night before you travel, put the pet in the carrier, zip or latch it, and watch for ears pressed flat against the ceiling, a back that cannot round into a lie-down, or frantic digging at a weak seam. Any one of those means the carrier is the wrong size or the wrong build, and the trip should wait.

Carrier questions

How do I know a carrier is big enough for an airline?

The pet should stand, turn, and lie down in a natural posture, and the bag must also meet that airline's current under-seat or cargo measurements. Measure nose to tail base and standing height, then compare those numbers with the interior once a soft bag is in its compressed shape. Policies change, and flat-faced breeds are often barred from cargo. Save the policy page you checked.

Are soft carriers safe in the car?

Belt the carrier so it cannot fly forward, and use a harness clip inside the door so a pet cannot bolt when you unzip. A soft bag is a containment and comfort tool. It is a weak crash shell compared with a rigid kennel that is strapped down, or with a vehicle harness fitted for the dog. Never leave the occupied carrier in a parked car.

When should I start getting a cat used to the carrier?

Days to weeks before the trip, with the door open, meals inside, and only brief closures at first. A carrier that appears solely for veterinary visits becomes a signal to hide. Short calm car rides help dogs and cats more than a single rehearsal the night before a flight. If the animal panics and cannot settle, postpone elective travel and ask a veterinarian or a trainer for a slower plan.
