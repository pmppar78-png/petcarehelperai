Dental Disease in Dogs

Periodontal disease in dogs covering stages, symptoms, professional cleaning, home dental care, and preventing tooth loss and infections.

What periodontal disease is

Periodontal disease is destruction of the tissues that hold a tooth in the jaw. It starts as plaque, a biofilm of bacteria on the tooth. Minerals in saliva turn plaque into calculus, the hard brown crust owners call tartar. Gingivitis is inflammation at the gumline and it can still heal. Periodontitis is the next stage: the periodontal ligament and the alveolar bone are eaten away, the tooth loosens, and that attachment does not grow back. The American Veterinary Dental College scores this in four stages, from gingivitis only to loss of more than half of the attachment. AAHA dental guidelines note that by about three years of age most dogs already have some periodontal disease, which is why "the teeth look fine from the door" is a weak screening test.

A separate problem sits beside periodontal disease and is often lumped in with it. A fractured tooth with the pulp exposed is an endodontic injury. The tooth may have little tartar and still be dead, painful, and seeding infection into the bone. A swelling under the eye in a dog who still eats is a classic carnassial tooth-root abscess. Bad breath is common to all of these and does not tell them apart.

Which dogs lose teeth early

Small and toy breeds crowd a full set of teeth into a short jaw. Yorkshire Terriers, Chihuahuas, Dachshunds, Maltese, Miniature Poodles, and many terriers develop serious periodontal disease while they are still young adults. Brachycephalic dogs rotate and crowd premolars. Dogs who retain puppy teeth pinch the adult tooth and trap plaque. Greyhounds and other sighthounds often have poor periodontal health even when the rest of the body looks athletic. Large dogs lose teeth too, especially if a slab fracture of a carnassial tooth is ignored because the dog "still eats." Chewing habits matter less than daily plaque control. A dog who never has a toothbrush in the mouth will outpace a dog who gets a dental chew and nothing else.

Signs owners notice

Halitosis, a red line where the gum meets the tooth, and calculus on the canines and carnassials are the early clues. Later you see gum recession, roots showing, a loose incisor, blood on a chew toy, and a dog who drops food or chews on one side. Pain is easy to miss because dogs keep eating. Facial swelling, a draining tract on the muzzle, sneezing, or a nasal discharge can mean a root abscess or an oronasal fistula, a hole from the mouth into the nose. Pawing at the mouth, drooling, and shying away from a head touch are pain signs even when the appetite looks normal.

  • Brown crust at the gumline and gums that bleed when touched.
  • Recession that makes the tooth look longer than it used to.
  • A broken tooth with a pink, red, or black spot where the pulp is open.
  • Swelling under one eye or a small hole on the cheek that drains pus.
  • A preference for soft food, or dropping hard kibble, in a dog who used to crunch.

How a veterinarian diagnoses it

Periodontal disease in dogs is only screened while the dog is awake. The diagnosis is made under anesthesia: every tooth is probed, charted, and radiographed. Those radiographs show root abscesses and bone loss a visual exam misses.

Awake, the clinician can see calculus, redness, a broken crown, and sometimes a loose tooth. A periodontal probe needs a still patient and measures pockets in millimeters around each tooth. Dental radiographs, ideally a full mouth, show bone loss, root fractures, and abscesses at the tips that the eye cannot see under the gum. AVDC staging uses attachment loss: stage 1 is gingivitis without loss, stage 2 is up to about a quarter of the attachment gone, stage 3 is roughly a quarter to a half, and stage 4 is more than half, often with mobility. The anesthetized chart decides which teeth can be cleaned and which should be extracted. A photo of tartar cannot make that call. Anesthesia-free scraping is a different product. AVDC and AAHA advise against it because the plaque under the gum stays put and sharp instruments around an awake mouth can injure the dog.

Treatment categories

Treatment is a dental procedure under anesthesia: scaling above and below the gum, and extraction of teeth that cannot be saved. It is repeated care as new teeth fail. Antibiotics do not replace extraction of a dead tooth. The veterinarian chooses any drug and the length of its use.

A complete oral health assessment and treatment includes charting, radiographs, supragingival and subgingival scaling, polishing, and local treatment of pockets that are still worth keeping. Teeth with severe attachment loss, a dead pulp, or a root abscess are extracted, or in selected cases referred for root canal therapy if the tooth is strategic and the owner wants to save it. Nerve blocks and a dedicated anesthetic protocol are part of the procedure, and the clinic explains the risks for a senior or a dog with heart disease before the day. Home care after healing is what slows the next round: daily brushing with a pet toothpaste the dog can swallow, and products that carry a Veterinary Oral Health Council seal if you want evidence beyond marketing. Human toothpaste can upset the stomach and does not belong in a dog's mouth.

Home care

Start brushing before the mouth hurts. Lift the lip, touch the outer surfaces of the canines and carnassials with gauze or a soft brush, and stop before the dog resents it. A few seconds a day beats a monthly wrestling match. Skip human toothpaste. If the dog will not allow a brush, a VOHC-accepted dental diet or water additive can be a partial help, and it still loses to brushing. Do not book an anesthesia-free "cleaning" as a substitute for radiographs. After a real procedure, feed the diet the clinic sends home, give only the pain control they dispensed, and watch for bleeding that continues, a swollen face, or a dog who will not eat the next day. Those are recheck problems. Waiting out the weekend is a poor plan.

When it is an emergency

When to See the Vet Immediately

A face that swells over a few hours, an eye that is pushed, bleeding that will not stop, a tooth snapped off with pulp showing after a trauma, or a dog who cannot close the mouth needs same-day care. A jaw that hangs open after a fall can be a fracture. Difficulty breathing with a dental problem is rare and urgent, because swelling can involve the neck. Ordinary bad breath and tartar should be scheduled as a dental procedure, and they should be scheduled, because infection in the bone does not stay polite while you compare prices for a month.

What to ask about the bill

Gingivitis limited to the gumline can settle after a professional cleaning and daily home care. Periodontal disease that has already eaten the ligament and the jaw bone does not grow that attachment back. AVDC and AAHA dental guidelines treat extraction of those teeth, under anesthesia with dental radiographs, as removal of pain and infection.

A dog dental invoice follows anesthesia time, full-mouth radiographs, and the number of extractions. Ask the dentist to reprice the procedure after the anesthetized oral exam, because a few loose incisors and a mouth full of rotten carnassials are different operations. Insurance seldom pays periodontal treatment that was already written in the record before enrollment.

Key Questions

How is dog periodontal disease scored?

AVDC stages run from gingivitis without attachment loss to severe bone loss with a loose tooth. The awake visit can see tartar and recession. Probing and dental radiographs under anesthesia decide the stage for each tooth and whether that tooth is extracted. A photo of brown canines cannot assign the stage, because the damage that matters is under the gum and inside the bone.

Why does my dog need anesthesia for a cleaning?

Subgingival scaling, probing, and radiographs require a still patient and an open mouth for a long time. An awake scrape polishes the crowns people can see and leaves the infected root area behind. AAHA and AVDC treat anesthesia, with monitoring, as part of a legitimate dental procedure. The pre-anesthetic exam and bloodwork are how the clinic decides whether that dog's heart, kidneys, and age make the day reasonable.

Can antibiotics save a loose tooth?

Antibiotics may calm a flare of infection and they do not rebuild a ligament that is gone. A tooth with a dead pulp or with most of its bony support missing stays a source of pain until it is extracted or, in a referral case, treated with a root canal. Using a leftover antibiotic to delay the procedure lets the bone loss continue and can make the eventual extraction harder.
