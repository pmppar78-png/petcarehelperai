Dog Ear Infections

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Practical guide to ear infections in dogs. Covers outer, middle, and inner ear infections, treatment, prevention, and breeds prone to ear problems.

What an ear infection is

Most "ear infections" in dogs are otitis externa, inflammation of the canal from the opening down to the eardrum. The canal is a warm tube, and once it is inflamed it traps moisture and grows either Malassezia yeast or bacteria. The organisms are usually secondary. The primary driver in a dog with repeated ear disease is often an allergy, either atopic dermatitis or a food reaction, sometimes with a conformation that keeps the canal damp. Otitis media is infection behind the eardrum, in the middle ear. Otitis interna involves the inner ear and shows up as a head tilt, a circling dog, or a flicker of the eyes. Those two need more than a bottle of drops.

A first yeast infection in one ear after a swim can be a short story. A dog who needs a new bottle of drops every month has a recurring disease, and the canal will mineralize and close if the allergy and the infection are both left to smolder. End-stage ears, with bone forming in the canal wall, are the ones that eventually get discussed for surgery.

Which dogs get

Floppy-eared, hairy-canaled, and allergic dogs lead the list. Cocker Spaniels, Basset Hounds, Labrador Retrievers, Golden Retrievers, and many spaniels trap heat and wax. Poodles and Schnauzers grow hair in the canal that mats with debris. French Bulldogs and other allergic brachycephalic dogs cycle through ears, paws, and skin together. Puppies from shelters sometimes have Otodectes ear mites, which look like dark coffee-ground debris and itch fiercely. Dogs who swim, especially in a pond, and dogs who get water in the ear and then sit in humidity, flare in summer. A foreign body such as a grass awn causes a sudden, severely painful ear on one side in a dog who was fine that morning.

Hypothyroidism and other systemic diseases can contribute in a middle-aged dog whose coat and energy are also off. The ear is rarely the only clue in those cases. Recurrence after a good response to drops should send the workup back to allergy, mites, a resistant bacterium, or a middle ear that was never examined.

Signs owners notice

Head shaking, ear flipping, and scratching at the base of the ear come first. The canal smells sweet if yeast dominates, or foul if bacteria do. Discharge may be brown and waxy, yellow and purulent, or black and crumbly with mites. The dog cries when the ear is touched and may hold the head down on that side. A head tilt, falling to one side, a droopy lip, or an eye that does not blink normally means the problem has gone deeper than the outer canal, toward the middle or inner ear and the nerves that pass nearby. Blood after a violent shake can be a burst vessel in the ear flap, an aural hematoma, which is a swelling of the pinna and a separate procedure from the canal infection that triggered the shaking.

  • Head shaking and a sour or sweet smell from one or both canals.
  • Brown, yellow, or black debris, and redness you can see without a scope.
  • Pain when the collar goes on, or a dog who will not let you lift the flap.
  • A head tilt, circling, or an eye that will not blink, which are neurologic signs.
  • A soft, fluid-filled swelling of the ear flap after a night of shaking.

How a veterinarian

A dog ear infection is diagnosed with an otoscope and cytology of the discharge. Cytology shows whether the smear is yeast, cocci, or rods, which is what picks the first topical drug. Culture is used when rods are present, the ear keeps relapsing, or the eardrum may be ruptured. A swab that never looks at the eardrum misses a middle-ear infection and a foreign body.

The otoscope has to see the eardrum. A canal packed with debris is cleaned enough to look, sometimes under sedation if the ear is too painful for an awake exam. Cytology is a roll of debris on a slide, stained in the clinic. Peanut-shaped yeast, round cocci, and rod-shaped bacteria tell different stories, and a mixed slide is common. Rods raise concern for Pseudomonas and for a rupture of the eardrum, so a culture and sensitivity guide the next drug if the first topical fails. Imaging, often CT, is the test for chronic otitis media when the dog has a head tilt, a draining tract, or years of failed treatment. A swab of the outer canal alone cannot describe the bulla, the bony bulb of the middle ear.

Treatment categories

Most canine ear infections are treated with a topical medication chosen from the cytology, plus cleaning the veterinarian has shown you how to do. Oral treatment is added when the middle ear is involved. The veterinarian matches the drug to the smear and sets the amount. End-stage ears that no longer respond, with mineralized canals, are the ones considered for total ear canal ablation.

Topical products combine an antibiotic, an antifungal, and often a steroid, and the combination should match the slide. If the eardrum is open, the veterinarian avoids drugs that can damage the inner ear. A cleaner may be used first so the medicated drop reaches skin. Oral antibiotics, antifungal drugs, and pain control join the plan for otitis media or for a canal so swollen that drops cannot enter. An aural hematoma is drained or repaired so the flap does not scar into a cauliflower. Total ear canal ablation with bulla osteotomy is the surgery for a canal that has turned to bone and still hurts. It removes the diseased canal. It is a referral procedure with its own nerve risks, and it is discussed only after medical care and imaging have shown the ear is end-stage. Allergy control is the treatment that stops the next infection from starting.

Home care

Clean only with the solution and the technique the clinic demonstrated. Fill the canal, massage the base gently, let the dog shake, and wipe only what comes to the opening. Do not pack a cotton swab toward the eardrum. Finish the drops for the number of days prescribed, then go back for the recheck cytology. Stopping on the day the smell fades is how yeast returns next week. Dry the ears after swimming, and ask whether a drying cleaner is appropriate for your dog's eardrum status. During an active infection, skip the dog park and keep water out of the canal. If this is the third infection this year, book the allergy workup. Ear drops forever, with no look at fleas, food, or atopy, will not hold the canal open.

When it is

When to See

A head tilt with falling, an eye that cannot blink, severe swelling that closes the canal, continuous crying, or a dog who is dizzy and vomiting should be seen the same day. Those signs can mean middle- or inner-ear disease, and some of the neurologic changes become harder to reverse if they sit for days. A foreign body suspicion, sudden severe pain in one ear after a field walk, also deserves a same-day look before the awn works deeper. A hematoma can usually wait until the next clinic day if the dog is comfortable and the canal is already being treated, and a ruptured, bleeding flap after head shaking should still be checked promptly.

What to ask

One outer-ear infection can end after cytology and a topical that matches the yeast or bacteria on the slide. Allergic otitis does not end. The canal keeps inviting the same organisms until the itch disease is controlled, and infection behind the eardrum is a middle-ear problem. Repeat cytology is how you know the episode stopped.

Budget ear care as cytology at every recheck, plus a culture if rod bacteria persist. The first bottle of drops is the small number. A total ear canal ablation, the surgery for a canal that has mineralized after years of failed treatment, is the number to request once radiographs show middle-ear disease. Controlling the allergy is the spend that avoids that operation.

Key Questions

Why does the ear infection keep coming back?

Because the yeast or the bacteria were the passengers. Atopic dermatitis, a food reaction, ear mites in a young dog, a resistant rod such as Pseudomonas, or infection behind a ruptured eardrum will reload the canal after each bottle of drops. Cytology at the recheck shows whether this episode actually cleared. A diet trial or flea control is the next step when the slide keeps growing the same organisms and the skin is itchy too.

Can I use last month's drops for a new smell?

Only if your veterinarian tells you to, after a new look in the ear. Last month's smear may have been yeast, and this month may be rods, or the eardrum may have ruptured since then. The wrong drop can damage the inner ear when the drum is open, and a leftover steroid drop can hide mites. Bring the bottle to the visit so the clinic can see what already failed.

What is a total ear canal ablation?

It is surgery that removes a canal too diseased and too mineralized to respond to drops, usually combined with opening the bony bulla. It is considered after imaging shows middle-ear disease and after medical treatment has failed. Dogs often feel better once the painful canal is gone, and the surgery has real risks to the facial nerve that the surgeon should explain before you book it. It is the expensive end of ear disease, which is why cytology and allergy control earlier are the cheaper path.
