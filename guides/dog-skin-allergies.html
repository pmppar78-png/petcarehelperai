Dog Skin Allergies and Dermatitis

Types of skin allergies in dogs including atopic dermatitis, food allergies, and contact allergies. Covers symptoms, testing, treatments, and itch relief.

What dog skin allergies are

Itchy skin in dogs usually falls into three overlapping problems: atopic dermatitis, flea allergy dermatitis, and cutaneous adverse food reaction. Atopic dermatitis is a hereditary inflammatory disease. The immune system overreacts to environmental allergens such as house dust mites, storage mites, pollens, and molds, and the skin barrier lets those allergens through more easily than it should. Flea allergy dermatitis is a hypersensitivity to proteins in flea saliva. One bite can keep a sensitized dog chewing for days after the flea is gone. A food reaction is an immune response or an intolerance to a protein the dog may have eaten for years. True contact allergy is uncommon and shows up on sparsely haired skin that rests against the offending surface, such as the belly, the feet, or the scrotum.

The itch is the disease. Redness, hair loss, dark pigment, thickening, and odor arrive later, after Staphylococcus bacteria or Malassezia yeast colonize skin that has been licked raw. Those infections are secondary, and the allergy feels much worse until they are cleared. A single dog can carry more than one allergy at once. A Labrador can be atopic, flea-allergic, and growing yeast between the toes in the same July.

Which dogs get them

Atopic dermatitis most often starts between about six months and three years of age. West Highland White Terriers, French Bulldogs, English Bulldogs, Labrador Retrievers, Golden Retrievers, German Shepherd Dogs, Boxers, Shar-Peis, and Cocker Spaniels fill dermatology schedules, and many other terriers and bully breeds do too. The tendency runs in families, so a puppy from itchy parents deserves strict flea control before the first bad summer. Food reactions can begin at any age, including in a senior who has eaten one kibble for a decade. Flea allergy can appear in any breed the week fleas enter the house, including a dog who never goes to a park if a visiting animal or a porch brings the parasite in.

The calendar helps sort the list. Pollen-driven atopy often flares in spring and fall and may become year-round as the dog reacts to more allergens. Flea allergy peaks in warm weather in seasonal climates and can smolder all year inside a heated home. Food reactions ignore the pollen calendar. A dog who scratches in January with the same intensity as in August, and who has no fleas on a comb, moves a diet trial up the list.

Signs owners notice

Watch the feet, the face, the ears, the armpits, the groin, and the base of the tail. Paw licking that stains pale fur a rusty brown, face rubbing on the rug, head shaking, and ear infections that return every few weeks are the everyday picture. Flea allergy concentrates on the rump. Some food-allergic dogs also pass soft stool, strain a little, or scoot, although plenty of them have skin signs only. Months of chewing produce lichenified, elephant-like skin and a sweet yeasty smell.

  • Scratching that wakes the dog at night and leaves broken fur on the bedding.
  • Pimple-like bumps, collarettes, and crusts when a bacterial pyoderma has joined the itch.
  • Brown, greasy ear debris that returns as soon as a bottle of drops runs out.
  • A moist, painful patch that spreads in hours where the dog has been chewing, which is a hotspot sitting on top of the allergy.
  • Chewing at the tail head and thighs during flea season even when you have not seen a flea jump.

How a veterinarian diagnoses it

Canine atopic dermatitis and food allergy are diagnoses of exclusion. The sequence is flea control that actually reaches the dog, skin scrapings for mites, and cytology for infection, then a strict diet trial when food is still possible. Intradermal or serum allergy tests do not prove atopy. They are used later to pick allergens for immunotherapy, after the diagnosis is already clinical.

Skipping a step leaves a dog on itch medicine while mites or a diet problem continue. Skin scrapings look for Sarcoptes and Demodex. Cytology, a tape prep or impression smear stained in the clinic, shows cocci, rods, or Malassezia and decides whether this flare is infected. Culture is reserved for deep or odd infections. When parasites and infection are handled and the itch remains, the food test is an elimination diet. A blood panel cannot name the food. The dog eats a hydrolyzed diet or a single novel protein and carbohydrate for about eight weeks, with no other foods. Flavored chews, toothpastes, and scraps void the trial. Serum and saliva food panels cannot name the culprit protein. Intradermal testing and serum IgE tests for pollens and mites come afterward, and only to choose the contents of allergen immunotherapy. A dust-mite result on a comfortable, non-itchy dog does not prove disease.

Treatment categories

Itch control, flea prevention, and treatment of the skin infection are done together. A food-allergic dog needs the diet that was identified, with no added treats. Allergen immunotherapy is a long plan for atopy. Drug choice and the amount stay with the veterinarian who examined the dog. A steroid cream bought without an exam can hide mites and yeast.

Itch medicines come in a few families. A short glucocorticoid course can quiet a savage flare. Oclacitinib is an oral drug used for allergic itch and is labeled for dogs old enough to receive it. Lokivetmab is an injectable antibody aimed at a canine itch signal, given on a spacing the clinic sets from how long relief lasts. Cyclosporine works more slowly and is used for longer control. Antihistamines help a minority of dogs and are a weak plan for a dog who is breaking the skin open. Allergen-specific immunotherapy, as injections or oral drops built from that dog's test, is the option aimed at changing the response over many months. None of these families replaces flea control or treatment of the bacteria and yeast on the skin.

Baths with a veterinary shampoo matched to yeast, bacteria, or a dry barrier remove allergen and calm the surface between tablets. A leave-on mousse can bridge the days between baths. Ear disease is treated from its own cytology, because the canal is the same allergy in a different place. Deep pyoderma, with fever and draining tracts, needs a longer plan than a surface rash and sometimes a culture before the drug is chosen.

Home care between visits

Put every dog and cat in the household on year-round flea prevention. Flea allergy collapses when one pet is left untreated and keeps reseeding the carpets. During a diet trial, close the trash, hand the dog only the trial food, and ask the clinic for an unflavored preventive if the usual chew is flavored. Wipe the paws and belly after grassy walks in pollen season. Fit an Elizabethan collar when a patch is moist so licking does not erase the medicine overnight. Score the itch from zero to ten once a week in a note on your phone, and bring that note plus a list of every chew, toothpaste, and leftover the dog still gets.

When it is an emergency

When to See the Vet Immediately

Go the same day if the face swells, hives spread, or breathing turns noisy or labored. That pattern can be anaphylaxis, sometimes after an insect sting or a vaccine, and it is a different event from ordinary atopic scratching. A dog who stops eating, runs a fever, or develops deep, painful, draining sores needs an urgent look for a serious skin infection. A sudden head tilt on top of an ear flare, or an eye swollen shut from rubbing, should be seen promptly so the middle ear and the cornea are checked before more damage sets in.

What to ask about the bill

Ask the clinic to price the layers separately. Cytology and a skin scraping belong to a short visit. An eight-week prescription diet is a food bill, and it buys nothing if treats continue. Environmental allergy testing and immunotherapy are a later project once atopy is the working diagnosis. Monthly itch control is the repeating cost for many atopic dogs and often continues for life. A single bottle of antibiotics cannot stand in for that whole plan. If you are thinking about insurance, ask whether ear infections already written in the record will be treated as pre-existing before you enroll.

Key Questions

Can atopic dermatitis in dogs be cured?

Atopic dermatitis is managed for life in most dogs. Flea allergy quiets when fleas are actually eliminated, and a food reaction quiets when the offending protein stays out of the bowl. Immunotherapy can lower the need for other itch drugs over many months, and secondary yeast or bacterial infection can be cleared, yet the hereditary atopic tendency remains. The useful goal is a comfortable dog with rare flares, which dermatologists track with an itch score and ear rechecks.

Why did the allergy test get postponed?

Intradermal and serum environmental tests do not prove that a dog has atopy, and they do not identify a food allergy. They are run after fleas, mites, infection, and a diet trial have been addressed, and then only to choose allergens for immunotherapy. Running them on the first itchy visit often produces a list of pollens while the real driver, a flea or a chicken-flavored chew, is still in the picture.

What should I track before the recheck?

Bring a weekly itch score, the names of every food and chew, the flea product and the date it was given, and photos of the paws and ears taken in the same light. Tell the clinic whether the dog improved on the diet and then worsened when a single protein was added back. That challenge, done with the veterinarian's timing, is what confirms a food reaction after the elimination period.
