And Care Guide

Covers species, diet, cage requirements, breeding, health issues, and singing behavior.

Housing and Enclosure

Finches are flight birds, and the cage has to be long. A pair of zebra finches or society finches needs a cage at least 30 inches wide, with less emphasis on height than a parrot cage and bar spacing of 1/2 inch or tighter so a head cannot push through. Canaries want similar length and a layout that lets them hop and flutter. One lonely finch in a tall, narrow parrot cage is a frustrated bird. Give several perches of different thickness, placed so droppings miss the food, plus a shallow bath dish or a clip-on tub. Finches bathe even when the room is cool, and a wet bird needs a draft-free corner to dry.

Hang the flight cage in a living room and leave the kitchen for cooking. Fumes from nonstick pans and strong sprays are dangerous to finches, small as they are. The room should be bright by day and dark and quiet at night for about 10 to 12 hours. A cage in a child's room with a nightlight and constant tapping on the bars will show stress as fluffed feathers and eggs that never should have been laid. Nesting material and enclosed huts encourage breeding. Offer them only if you have a plan for the chicks and an avian veterinarian. Otherwise leave the cage open and skip the coconut hut.

Finches are social and usually live as a pair or a small group. Zebra finches do well in pairs. Society finches are often peaceful. Mixing species takes space so one pair cannot trap another. Canaries are sometimes kept singly because males sing more without a rival, and they still hear the household. They are observation pets. They do not become hand-tame the way a budgie can, and chasing a finch to make it perch on a finger causes panic and injury. A bird-specific full-spectrum lamp can set the day length. Window glass filters UVB, so indoor finches may need that lamp plus a cuttlebone (Association of Avian Veterinarians; Merck Veterinary Manual, Nutrition in Pet Birds).

Diet and Nutrition

Most finches and canaries do best on a quality seed mix made for them, plus fresh greens and a regular offering of egg food. That is different from a parrot, where pellets should crowd seed out. Finch pellets exist and can be part of the bowl, and many birds still rely on a varied seed mix as the base. An all-millet diet is too narrow. Read the bag. A mix with several small seeds, plus sprouted seed if you can rinse it clean, covers more ground than a single spray. Egg food matters especially during a molt and if you are deliberately breeding. Offer it a few times a week for pet birds outside a breeding program, and pull it the same day so it does not sour.

Leafy greens, a thin slice of apple, and a broccoli floret give vitamins the seed hulls lack. Rinse produce. Cuttlebone and a mineral block stay in the cage. Grit is debated. Some finch keepers offer a small dish of insoluble grit, and others rely on the cuttlebone and a formulated mix. If you offer grit, keep it as a choice the bird can ignore, because a sick finch may gorge on grit and impact. Fresh water daily, and wash the bath so it does not become the drinking water full of seed.

Avoid avocado, chocolate, and salty scraps. Finches will sample odd foods and do not need a bite of dinner. If you add a pellet, introduce it beside the seed and watch body condition. A finch that drops weight during a diet experiment needs the familiar mix back the same day. These birds have almost no reserve.

Health and Veterinary

Air sac mites, scaly leg mites, egg binding, and respiratory infections are the finch and canary problems that show up in home aviaries. Air sac mites cause clicking, open-mouth breathing, and a tail bob, especially in gouldians and canaries, and they need an avian veterinarian. Scaly leg looks like crusty build-up on the feet and is a mite problem with a real treatment. A kitchen lotion leaves the mites working. Egg binding is a hen on the floor, fluffed and straining. It is an emergency. Respiratory infections follow drafts, a dirty cage, and new birds that skipped quarantine.

Finches hide illness until they are nearly too weak to perch. Watch who sleeps on two feet in a corner at noon, who stops bathing, and whose droppings change. A bird on the cage floor is an emergency. Treat it as a collapse. Find an avian veterinarian who will see a 15-gram patient before you buy the pair. Many clinics can do it, and some will tell you honestly if they cannot. Quarantine new finches in a separate room. Mites and pox move through a rack of cages on a shared hand.

Clean the cage on a schedule, because seed hulls and dried bath water grow the bacteria and the fungus these small lungs cannot afford. Keep the room's air free of strong perfume and smoke. Annual or twice-yearly checks are reasonable for birds you hope to keep for 5 to 10 years, which is a common span for many pet finches and canaries when the flight and the diet are right.

Watching Without Handling

  • Give the pair a long cage and more than one feeding station so a bossy bird cannot block the food.
  • Offer seed mix, greens, and egg food on a rhythm, and keep millet as a scatter treat.
  • Provide a bath and then a dry, draft-free perch.
  • Skip nest kits unless you want chicks and have homes for them.
  • Quarantine newcomers and book an avian veterinarian who accepts finches.

A healthy finch flicks between perches, bathes, and sings or chatters after the cover comes off. A puffed, clicking, or egg-bound bird needs help the same day. Keep them as the active, hands-off flock they are, with length in the cage and variety in the dish.

A Long Cage and a Hands-Off Flock

Finches show health by movement. A pair that flicks the length of a 30-inch cage, bathes, and resettles on a high perch is doing the job you built the flight for. A bird that sits fluffed at noon, clicks when it breathes, or sleeps on the floor has stopped behaving like a healthy finch. That last picture is an emergency for an avian veterinarian who will see a tiny patient. Find that clinic before you buy, and keep a small carrier ready. Air sac mites, especially in canaries and gouldians, and scaly leg mites need a real diagnosis. Home oils delay care.

Feed like a finch keeper, which is different from a parrot keeper. A varied seed mix remains the usual base, with greens and egg food through the week, and a pellet can sit beside the seed if the birds accept it. Watch weight by eye and by how the keel feels if you are experienced, and by whether the bird still flies to the food. An all-millet spray is a treat that becomes a deficiency if it replaces the mix. Pull egg food the same day. A sour egg mix in a warm room is a digestive upset these birds cannot afford.

Breeding is a decision you make on purpose. A nest and loose fiber will produce eggs, and eggs become chicks that need homes and a closer eye for binding and for parent birds that fail. Skip the hut if you wanted song and flight. Zebra finches and society finches differ in how peacefully they share, so give more than one feeding station and enough length that a chased bird can land elsewhere. Quarantine every new bird in another room. The pleasure of this pet is watching a flock work a long cage, with clean seed, a bath, and a dark night.

Quick Answers

Are finches good for first-time bird owners?

Yes, if you want to watch a pair fly and sing and you will give them a cage at least 30 inches long, a seed mix plus greens and egg food, and an avian veterinarian. They are a poor fit if you want a bird that sits on your finger.

A long flight cage costs more than the birds and is the purchase that matters. The birds still need an avian exam: 2026 fee roundups put a routine exotic avian visit at about $95 to $185 before tests (findanexoticvet.com), and AAV tells owners to schedule wellness care even for small finches (aav.org). Seed, greens, and egg food stay inexpensive.
