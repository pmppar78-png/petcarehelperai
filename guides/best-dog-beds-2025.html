Best Dog Beds 2025

Updated guide to the best dog beds including orthopedic, bolster, cooling, and indestructible options for every size and sleeping style.

Watch how the dog actually sleeps

Bed shopping starts on the floor you already have. A dog who curls into a tight circle and tucks the nose under a tail wants a bolster or a donut with a raised rim to lean on. A dog who sprawls on a hip, legs straight out, wants a flat rectangle long enough for that stretch, with no stuffed wall in the way of the hind feet. A dog who digs and circles for two minutes before dropping needs a cover that can survive that ritual, or you will replace the bed after the stuffing comes out in clumps. Puppies change their minds monthly. Seniors get more opinionated as joints stiffen, and they often want the bed exactly where the evening routine already ends, which might be beside your bed and might be a crate.

Measure the dog from nose to the base of the tail while the dog is stretched on one side, then add several inches. A bed sold as "large" because it holds a curled Lab will feel like a placemat to that Lab in July when the dog wants to pancake. Round beds photograph well and run small. If you are between sizes, go up, unless the bed has to live inside a crate. Crate pads should cover the pan without buckling up the sides. A buckled pad becomes a chew edge and a toe trap the first night.

Orthopedic foam

Orthopedic, in this aisle, usually means a thicker foam slab meant to cushion hips, elbows, and spines. Solid memory foam cradles and sleeps warmer. A denser conventional foam, or a foam with a channeled core, often gives more push-back for a heavy dog and runs cooler. Gel-swirl toppers and quilted covers change the feel and the heat. Press the display bed with a flat hand. If it collapses to the floor under moderate pressure, a 70-pound dog will bottom out by the second month. Ask what the foam density is if the listing prints it, and treat vague words like "cloud" as decoration.

These beds can make a stiff dog more willing to lie down. They do not repair a cruciate ligament or reverse arthritis. A dog who cries when rising, skips a meal, or suddenly refuses the usual jump still needs a veterinarian. Put the bed where the floor is non-slip so the dog can stand up without skating. A plush cushion on a slick hallway is harder on sore hips than a thinner pad on a yoga mat. Waterproof liners between the foam and the cover save the expensive part when a senior leaks or a puppy has an accident. Zip the liner so it does not bunch into a ridge under the shoulder.

Bolsters, cooling beds, and crate pads

Bolster rims give head and spine support to dogs who sleep curled, and they give a nervous dog a sense of a wall at the back. They also collect drool and ear dirt, and a chewer will open a bolster the way a present gets opened. If your dog disembowels toys, skip the stuffed rim and choose a flat bed with a tough cover. You can add a rolled towel on nights you want a headrest and wash the towel in the morning. Donut beds for small dogs work when the dog fits inside the ring while lying down. A donut that only fits the dog in a sphinx position will be abandoned by midnight.

Cooling beds use a gel layer, a raised mesh cot, or a washable cover with a slick surface. They help double-coated dogs, overweight dogs, and any dog who relocates to the tile every August. Gel packs warm up under a long nap and then need time to reset. Raised cots let air pass underneath and keep the dog off hot ground or damp grass on a porch, and they are a poor choice for a dog who needs a soft landing for sore elbows unless you add a thin mat the dog will actually use. In winter, a cot in a drafty room is the opposite of kind. Have a warmer bed available when the house cools, and let the dog choose.

Chewers, stuffing, and what "indestructible" can mean

No bed is truly indestructible once a determined jaw has an hour alone with a seam. Tough beds use a ballistic-style fabric, hidden zippers, and a design with less stuffed piping. They buy you time and they still lose to a dog who wants the fluff. Ingested foam and batting cause intestinal obstructions that become surgery. If your dog eats beds, skip loose stuffing entirely. Use a crate pad with a sealed cover, a heavy canvas cot, or a towel you can count at the end of the day. Supervise, or use a crate that does not contain a destructible bed, until the habit changes. A camera in the room will show you whether the destruction happens in the first ten minutes after you leave, which is a training problem as much as a fabric problem.

Puppies mouth everything during teething. Give them a legal chew and a simple pad, and store the heirloom orthopedic bed until adult dentition is finished and the dog has shown it will leave fabrics alone. Wash covers before the first use if they smell strongly of dye or sizing. Some dogs avoid a new bed because it smells like the warehouse. A blanket that already smells like you, placed on top for a few nights, changes the vote.

Covers, washing, and placement

Removable covers with a heavy zipper are the difference between a bed that lasts and a bed that leaves the house after one muddy spring. Look for a second zipper pull you can lock together so a bored dog has a harder time opening the foam compartment. Launder covers the way the sewn-in tag describes, and dry the cover fully before it goes back on damp foam. Foam that stays wet grows the smell people then try to cover with perfume sprays. Those sprays annoy some dogs and do nothing for the mold in the slab. If the foam itself gets soaked with urine, replace it. Surface wiping will not pull urine out of open-cell foam.

Place beds out of the main traffic lane and out from under a dripping water bowl. A bed next to a bowl becomes a sponge. In a multi-dog house, two beds prevent a resource fight you might misread as "he hates this brand." Crate-trained dogs often sleep better on a pad that touches the crate walls evenly, with a blanket they can nose into a pile. Greying faces may want a night-light and a bed with a low entry. A bolster with a high rim is a hurdle for a dog who can no longer hop. Cut a low entrance or choose a flat orthopedic slab with a non-slip base.

What beds cost in ordinary shops

Simple crate pads are commonly tagged around $20 to $55. Bolster and donut styles for small and medium dogs commonly run about $35 to $120. Orthopedic slabs for large dogs frequently land around $70 to $220, and heavy chew-resistant beds overlap that band and sometimes exceed it. Those are typical ranges. A waterproof liner is a small extra that protects the larger spend. Replace a bed when the foam stays compressed, the cover zipper fails, or the dog starts slipping off a once-grippy base. Keep one washable spare cover if the dog is a swimmer or a senior, so a wet bed does not mean a night on the bare floor.

Bed questions

How big should a dog bed be?

Measure the dog stretched on one side from nose to tail base and add several inches so a summer sprawl still fits. Round beds often only fit a curled posture. Inside a crate, the pad should lie flat without buckling up the walls. If you are between two labeled sizes for a free-standing bed, the larger one usually matches the way dogs actually sleep.

Do orthopedic beds replace arthritis treatment?

A supportive slab can make lying down more comfortable and can help a dog get up on a non-slip floor. It leaves the disease, the pain medication question, and the weight plan with your veterinarian. Choose a foam that does not bottom out under the dog's weight, add a waterproof liner if leaks are likely, and skip high bolsters when a senior can no longer step over them.

What bed is realistic for a dog who guts stuffed toys?

Skip loose batting and stuffed rims. Use a sealed crate pad, a heavy canvas cot, or a towel you can inventory after every absence. Ingested foam can obstruct the gut and become an emergency. Tough fabrics buy time and still lose to an unsupervised chewer, so the setup and the training matter as much as the catalog word indestructible.
