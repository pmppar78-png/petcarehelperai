Aquarium Nitrogen Cycle Guide

Understanding the nitrogen cycle for new aquarium owners. Covers cycling methods, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and establishing beneficial bacteria.

How the Cycle Works

The nitrogen cycle is the reason a brand-new aquarium kills fish and a mature one does not. Fish, shrimp, and leftover food release ammonia. In a cycled filter, one group of bacteria converts ammonia to nitrite. Another converts nitrite to nitrate. Ammonia and nitrite are toxic at small amounts. Nitrate is far less toxic and leaves the tank through water changes and, in a planted tank, through plant growth. The work happens on filter media, gravel, and any porous surface that stays oxygenated and wet.

A finished cycle means ammonia reads 0 ppm and nitrite reads 0 ppm, with some nitrate showing up on the test kit. If ammonia is zero only because you have not added a source, the tank is merely empty and the cycle has not started. The bacteria need food. They also need time, usually measured in weeks, and they die if you rinse the sponge in tap water full of chlorine or if a medication knocks them back.

Temperature changes the speed. Warm water grows these bacteria faster than a cold garage tank, which is why people sometimes run a heater during a fishless cycle and then remove it if the livestock is coolwater. The heater is a tool for the fish species you eventually keep. Goldfish and similar coolwater animals do not need one as a default once the room sits near 65 to 75 F. Bettas and other tropical fish do. The cycle itself only cares that conditions stay stable enough for the bacteria you are growing.

Diet and Nutrition

During a fishless cycle the only diner is the bacteria colony. The reliable ammonia source is pure ammonia with no surfactants, dyes, or scents, dosed so the test kit shows a modest reading, often in the 2 to 4 ppm range, and then allowed to fall as bacteria appear. Follow the kit instructions for the drop count. If the bottle contains surfactants, dye, or fragrance, it can foam and poison the filter you are trying to build. Fish food works as a backup. A pinch sinks, rots, and releases ammonia unevenly. It is messier and slower, and it is still better than adding a fish to sit in the spike.

Do not add fish, shrimp, or snails to "start the cycle." That practice, sometimes called a fish-in cycle, exposes animals to ammonia and nitrite for weeks. If you already have animals in an uncycled tank, you are in rescue mode: test daily, change water whenever ammonia or nitrite rises, and feed lightly until the bacteria catch up. Seeded media from an established, disease-free filter can shorten the wait. A bottle of bacteria may help and may do nothing. Keep testing either way. The test kit tells you when the tank is ready, whatever the bottle label promises.

Once fish are in, their food is the ongoing ammonia source. Extra flakes are extra ammonia. A dead fish left behind the plants is a sudden dose. Pull uneaten food and remove bodies the day you notice them. Plants consume some nitrate and a little ammonia, and they do not replace a filter in a new tank.

Health and Veterinary Care

An uncycled or crashed cycle looks like cloudy water, fish gasping or clamped, and a test kit that shows ammonia or nitrite above zero. The Merck Veterinary Manual treats ammonia and nitrite poisoning as the most common aquarium problem. The triggers are an uncycled tank, overfeeding, a dead fish left in the water, or a filter that stopped. Both values should read 0 ppm before you call the tank safe. Fish cannot regulate their own body temperature, so metabolism follows the water. Keep a thermometer in the tank, and use a thermostat heater for tropical species. Coolwater species still need the thermometer so you know the room is doing the job.

Ich and other parasites usually arrive on new fish or plants. Quarantine those in a separate tank so a display that is still cycling stays free of extra medicine. Bacterial infections such as fin rot usually follow poor water or an injury, and an uncycled tank supplies the poor water. Copper treatments are toxic to shrimp, snails, and plants, and they can set the filter bacteria back. If copper has been used, test ammonia and nitrite for weeks, because the cycle you thought was finished may have stalled.

If ammonia appears in a tank that used to read zero, change 50 to 75 percent of the water right away, then look for the cause. Overfeeding, a body, and a filter that stopped or was cleaned under the tap are the usual ones. Conditioner can detoxify ammonia briefly. It does not build bacteria. Keep changing water until the tests behave.

Background for these warnings is the Merck Veterinary Manual discussion of aquarium fish.

A Practical Fishless Cycle

  • Set up the filter, the thermometer, and the dechlorinator. Fill the tank and start the filter.
  • Add the ammonia source and confirm it on a liquid test kit that reads ammonia and nitrite separately.
  • Test every day. Ammonia falls, nitrite often rises, and both eventually hit zero while nitrate climbs.
  • Expect about 4 to 8 weeks. Seeded media can shorten that. Cold water can stretch it.
  • Add fish only after ammonia and nitrite have read zero and nitrate is present. Add a few animals, then wait, so the colony can grow to match the waste.

When new fish do go in, float the bag to match temperature and mix tank water in slowly. Check adult size and temperament before you buy the next species. Keep hands out of the water when you can. Use a net briefly. Chasing a fish around a newly cycled tank is a good way to injure it on the hardscape you just arranged. Rinse filter sponges in old tank water during later maintenance. Tap water belongs in the bucket you are about to dechlorinate, and it stays off the media that holds the cycle.

Reading the Kit Until Both Toxins Sit at Zero

A liquid test kit that separates ammonia from nitrite is the tool that decides when fish may enter. Strips that blur those two readings cause people to stock a tank that is still in the nitrite spike. Write the numbers on a card taped to the stand: date, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and temperature. You are looking for ammonia to rise or to be dosed, then fall, for nitrite to rise and then fall, and for nitrate to become measurable. Fish go in only after ammonia and nitrite have both read zero and nitrate has appeared. A tank that reads zero ammonia because you never added a source is simply empty.

If animals are already in an uncycled aquarium, you are doing rescue care. Test daily. Change water whenever either toxin climbs. Feed lightly. Seeded media from a healthy, disease-free filter can shorten the wait, and a bottled product might help or might not. The kit remains the judge. When the cycle later crashes in a stocked tank, change 50 to 75 percent at once and look for a dead fish, a clogged filter, a sponge rinsed under the tap, or a pile of uneaten food. Conditioner can bind ammonia for a short time. It does not grow the bacteria.

Temperature belongs to the species you will keep, and the cycle only needs stability while the bacteria establish. Tropical fish such as bettas need a thermostat heater. Goldfish and similar coolwater animals can live without one when the room already holds about 65 to 75 F. This process is filter biology: a wet, oxygenated sponge and a test kit. Rinse media in removed tank water from then on, and add new fish a few at a time so the colony can grow into the waste.

Quick Answers

How long does it take to cycle an aquarium?

Usually 4 to 8 weeks. Add an ammonia source, test daily, and add fish only after ammonia and nitrite both read zero and nitrate has started to appear.

What should I do if ammonia shows up in an established tank?

Do a large water change of 50 to 75 percent right away, then find the cause. Overfeeding, a dead fish, or a filter that stopped or was cleaned in tap water are the usual culprits.
