Best Cat Supplements

Top cat supplements for joint health, hairball control, urinary support, and overall wellness. Includes dosing guidelines and vet-recommended brands.

Start from the food, then add a reason

A cat eating a complete and balanced commercial diet already has vitamins, taurine, and minerals mixed in at levels the recipe was built around. A second multivitamin on top of that can push vitamin A or vitamin D higher than you meant, especially in a small body. Supplements earn a place when something specific is going on: a coat that stays dull after parasites and diet are addressed, hairballs that persist despite brushing, a joint plan your veterinarian sketched, or a home-cooked recipe a veterinary nutritionist wrote that needs its own balance. They are a weak fix for a cat who is losing weight, drinking more, or hiding. Those signs need an exam and lab work before a jar from the pet aisle.

Bring every product to the clinic, including fish oil and the hairball gel. Some ingredients change how a prescribed diet works, and a few "natural" drops are essential oils, which can be dangerous for cats. Dog chews are off the list. They are sized and flavored for dogs, and some contain xylitol or other sweeteners a cat must avoid. If the label lists a dose in milligrams per chew, ask the veterinarian what that means for your cat's weight. This guide will stay with categories and label-reading, because a number copied from a forum is how small animals get overdosed.

Omega-3 oils

Fish oil and some algal oils supply EPA and DHA, the omega-3 fatty acids veterinarians sometimes add for itchy skin, a dull coat, or as part of a joint and kidney conversation. The useful label names the species, lists EPA and DHA in milligrams per pump or capsule, and includes an antioxidant such as vitamin E so the oil stays stable. Smell it. Rancid oil smells sharply bitter and belongs in the trash, because oxidized fat is the opposite of a skin help. Keep the bottle cool and dark, and finish it before the date. Pump liquids let you adjust the amount your veterinarian sets. Flavored dog soft-chews often overshoot a cat and add calories a waistline cannot spare.

Introduce oil slowly. Loose stool is the usual signal that the amount is ahead of what that cat tolerates, and it is a reason to pause and ask, even when you were following a bottle. Cats on a therapeutic diet, especially a renal diet that already balances fats, should get oil only when the prescribing veterinarian adds it. Pouring a bonus pump because the coat looks dry can upset a recipe that was doing quiet work.

Joints

Glucosamine, chondroitin, and green-lipped mussel appear in cat chews and in some senior foods. The evidence in cats is thinner than the advertising, and the amounts in a grocery chew may be modest. Some cats with early stiffness still seem more willing to jump after a veterinary-directed trial of a joint product, and some cats show little change. A fair test lasts several weeks, with notes on whether the cat still reaches a favorite perch, because memory is a poor scale. Injectable joint medications exist and stay inside the clinic. They are prescribed after an exam, and they are a different decision from a chew.

Rule out pain that is actually dental, a wound, or a heavier cat being asked to jump from a tall fridge. A supplement will disappoint you if the cat needs a dental procedure or a weight plan. Ramps, a lower litter pan lip, and a measured diet change the day more reliably than a new jar. If you do buy a joint chew, pick one made for cats, with a texture your cat will eat without a fight that ends in both of you giving up.

Hairballs

A malt-flavored gel or a petroleum-based lubricant can help a long-haired cat pass a hairball that is already forming. It is a lubricant, and it works best alongside brushing, which removes the hair before the cat swallows it, and alongside a diet the cat actually digests. Some foods add extra fiber for hairball control. Those can be useful for a cat who grooms constantly and still eats well. They are a nuisance if they fill the cat up and the total calories of a complete food get displaced by gel and treats.

Vomiting more than the occasional hairball is a medical sign. So is retching with nothing coming up, hiding after meals, or a drop in stool. Do not spend a month upgrading gels while an obstruction or an inflammatory gut problem waits. Your veterinarian may want a film or a diet trial. Bring the gel tube so they can see how much the cat has been getting.

Urinary products, lysine, and probiotics

Urinary "support" treats and cranberry chews are popular and easy to over-trust. The everyday tool for a cat with crystals or cystitis history is water: wet food, a fountain, and a litter setup the cat will use so you notice straining early. Acidifying supplements and methionine-type products can push urine chemistry the wrong way if the cat already eats a urinary therapeutic diet or if the stone type wants a different pH. Use those only when a veterinarian who has seen the urinalysis names them. Cranberry products have a mixed, limited story in cats. They are optional at best and a delay at worst when a cat is straining, which is an emergency if little urine comes out.

Lysine is sold for cats with feline herpesvirus flare-ups. Research on it is mixed, and some reviews find little benefit for eye and nose signs. That makes it a veterinarian's call during a flare, paired with the care the clinic already recommended, including keeping the nostrils clear and watching appetite. Probiotics for cats list a species, a strain, and a colony count that lasts to the end of the shelf date. A vague "live cultures" line is hard to judge. Store them as the label says, because a hot mailbox can kill the organisms you paid for. They sometimes help with soft stool during a diet change. They are a side dish next to a workup for chronic diarrhea.

How to read the jar, and what it tends to cost

Look for a lot number, a date, and a company that will answer a phone call about the formula. The National Animal Supplement Council quality seal, when present, means the maker follows that group's manufacturing standards. It is a quality signal. It is an approval of a marketing claim only in the limited sense that the company agreed to a code. It does not turn a chew into a drug. Avoid products that promise to cure kidney failure, cancer, or hyperthyroidism. Those sentences are a reason to leave the page.

Typical retail bands, and only as bands, often put a small bottle of fish oil around $12 to $30, a bag of cat joint chews around $15 to $40, and probiotic packets higher because you are buying a measured strip. Hairball gels are frequently under $15. A jar the cat refuses is a full-price waste, so buy the small size first. Track stool, appetite, and one behavior you care about, such as jumping to the windowsill, for a month. If nothing in that log moved, stop the product and spend the next month's money on the lab work or the dental estimate you have been postponing.

Supplement questions for cat owners

Can I use a dog joint chew for my cat if I break it in half?

Buy a product made for cats and ask your veterinarian how it fits the cat's weight and any prescribed diet. Dog chews use different amounts and sometimes include sweeteners, including xylitol, that cats should avoid. Halving a chew also gives you a rough guess, which is a poor way to dose a small animal. Bring the jar to the appointment so the clinic can read the actual milligrams.

Do indoor cats need a multivitamin on top of complete food?

Complete commercial cat foods already include vitamins and taurine at the levels the recipe intended. An extra multivitamin can crowd vitamin A or D. Add a specific product, such as a fish oil or a probiotic, when there is a reason and a veterinary okay. Home-cooked menus are the exception, and they need a recipe from a veterinary nutritionist so taurine and calcium are planned from the start.

How long should I try a hairball gel before I worry?

A gel can help an occasional hairball while you brush out loose fur and keep the cat eating a complete diet. Repeated vomiting, empty retching, a smaller stool, or a cat who skips meals is a veterinary problem in the same week. Bring the tube and a note of how often you have been giving it so the exam starts with a clear history.
