Planted Aquarium Beginner

Getting started with live aquarium plants. Covers easy beginner plants, substrate, lighting, CO2, fertilizers, and creating an aquascape.

Housing and Enclosure

A planted aquarium is a fish tank that grows plants on purpose, and the hardware follows the plants you actually buy. Low-tech tanks, the kind a beginner should start, use easy species, a modest light, and no injected CO2. Java fern, Anubias, Java moss, and Amazon swords are the reliable first set. Tie Java fern and Anubias to wood or rock. If you bury the rhizome, the plant melts from the crown down. Swords want their roots in the substrate and a root tab nearby. Moss can be tied or left to creep.

Run the light 6 to 8 hours a day on a timer. A brighter fixture left on for twelve hours grows algae faster than it grows Anubias. You can succeed with a simple LED made for freshwater plants. You do not need a reef light. Substrate can be inert gravel plus root tabs, or an aquasoil if you want stem plants later. Starting on aquasoil and then adding a heavy fish load the same week dumps ammonia while the soil is still leaching. Cycle the tank, plant it, and let the leaves settle before the fish move in.

Filter flow should reach the plants without sandblasting them. A sponge filter is enough in a shrimp-and-plant tank. A canister or hang-on-back suits a larger community, with the outflow aimed along the surface so CO2, if you ever add it, stays in the water a little longer. Heater choice follows the fish you plan to keep. Tropical tetras and bettas need a thermostat heater. A coolwater goldfish tank can stay unheated, and goldfish will also eat the garden you just planted, so pick the livestock to match the scape.

Layout is simple if you decide it before you flood the tank. Tall plants at the back, moss and crypts in the middle, and an open front where you can siphon. Leave a gap around the heater and the filter intake so leaves do not smother them. Floaters such as salvinia shade the lower leaves and drink nutrients. They also block light entirely if you never thin them.

Diet and Nutrition

Plants eat dissolved nutrients, and fish waste covers only part of the menu. Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium are the macros. Iron and other trace elements are the micros. A fish-in tank supplies some nitrogen. A lightly stocked scape, or a new tank with a big sword, will show pale new leaves or pinholes unless you add fertilizer. Root tabs under heavy root feeders and a liquid fertilizer dosed on the bottle's schedule for low-tech tanks are enough for the beginner list. High-light stem plants and injected CO2 are a later project with a tighter dosing routine.

Deficiency clues are useful and easy to overread. Yellow older leaves can be nitrogen. Pinholes in older leaves often point at potassium. Pale new growth can be iron or simply a plant that is melting from a move. Change one thing at a time. Doubling the light and the fertilizer on the same day is how tanks turn green with algae. Fish still need their own food. Plants do not replace a pellet for a tetra, and a hungry school will pick at soft leaves.

CO2 from a pressurized system speeds growth and also crashes pH if the solenoid sticks. Low-tech tanks skip that risk. Liquid carbon supplements are algicides as much as they are carbon, and some of them injure Vallisneria, moss, and shrimp. Read the bottle, start low if you use one at all, and keep copper-containing products out of any tank with invertebrates.

Health and Veterinary

Algae, melting, and hungry leaves are the plant problems. Algae usually means the light runs too long or the nutrients outpace what the plants can use. Shorten the photoperiod, thin floaters only after you have a reason, and trim dead leaves before they decay into more food for algae. New plants often melt when they move from emersed farm growth to underwater life. Java fern and swords may look awful for two weeks and then send up new leaves. Wait for the new growth before you declare the plant dead. Snails and shrimp help with leftover food. They will not save a tank lit fourteen hours a day.

The fish in the planted tank still live under aquarium-fish rules from the Merck Veterinary Manual. Ammonia and nitrite poisoning is the common emergency. It follows an uncycled tank, overfeeding, a dead fish caught in the stems, or a filter that stopped. Both should read 0 ppm. Fish cannot set their own temperature, so use a thermometer and, for tropical species, a thermostat heater. Coolwater livestock can do without that heater when the room is already in range. Ich and other parasites usually arrive on new fish or new plants, so quarantine both when you can. Fin erosion from bacteria usually follows a dirty week or a nipped fin. Copper treatments harm shrimp, snails, and plants and can set the filter bacteria back, so ammonia and nitrite need testing for weeks afterward.

The manual's aquarium chapter is here: Merck Veterinary Manual aquarium fish management.

Rinse new plants in clean water and pick off snails or eggs if you do not want a snail bloom. A bleach dip is easy to get wrong and will burn leaves and gills if any residue remains, so skip homemade dips unless you already have a method a fish veterinarian has walked you through. Quarantine plants in a bucket with a light for a week if your display holds expensive shrimp.

Stocking Around the Plants

  • Plant and cycle before the first fish, and confirm ammonia and nitrite at zero.
  • Add fish a few at a time so the filter and the plants can take the new waste.
  • Choose species that match one temperature and will leave the leaves alone.
  • Float bags and mix water slowly. A shocked fish drives into the hardscape and tears its mouth.
  • Keep hands, soap, and lotion out of the tank. Use a net briefly when a move is required.

A low-tech tank that is working looks a little untidy and clearly alive. New leaves are larger than the imported ones, the moss is greener at the tips, and you can still see the fish. A tank that is a solid sheet of hair algae needs fewer hours of light and a look at how much you feed, long before it needs a new fixture.

The First Leaves After the Melt

New plants often look worse before they look alive. Emersed farm leaves melt underwater, and a sword or a Java fern can look ruined for two weeks while a new leaf starts at the crown. Judge the plant by that new growth. If you buried an Anubias or Java fern rhizome, lift it and tie it to wood. Root tabs go under swords and other heavy root feeders. A liquid fertilizer on a low-tech schedule covers the rest when the fish load is light. Run the light 6 to 8 hours on a timer. Algae on the glass usually means the light is longer than the plants can use, or the feeding is heavier than the stems can absorb.

The fish still need a cycled filter. Ammonia and nitrite at zero, a thermometer, and a heater only if the species is tropical, are the animal side of a planted tank. Goldfish will dig and eat the garden, so choose livestock that leaves leaves alone. Quarantine new plants if the tank holds shrimp, and keep copper fertilizers and copper medicines out. Rinse frozen fish food so it does not become a nutrient dump. Trim dead leaves before they decay. A low-tech scape that is working shows larger new leaves, green moss tips, and fish you can still see. Hair algae from top to bottom is a lighting and feeding problem long before it is a reason to buy a stronger fixture.

Quick Answers

Is a planted aquarium hard to maintain?

Not if you start low-tech. Java fern, Anubias, Java moss, and Amazon swords grow without injected CO2. Keep the light to 6 to 8 hours a day and use root tabs or a liquid fertilizer as needed.

Why is algae taking over my planted tank?

Usually too much light or too many nutrients for the amount of plant growth. Shorten the light period and trim dead leaves before they decay.
