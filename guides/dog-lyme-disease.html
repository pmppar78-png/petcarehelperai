Lyme Disease in Dogs

Lyme disease in dogs from tick bites. Covers symptoms, testing, antibiotic treatment, vaccination, and tick prevention strategies.

What Lyme disease is in dogs

Lyme disease in dogs is an infection with the spirochete Borrelia burgdorferi, passed by blacklegged ticks. In the eastern and midwestern United States the vector is Ixodes scapularis. On the Pacific coast it is Ixodes pacificus. The tick usually has to stay attached for more than a day before the bacteria move into the dog, which is why a daily tick check still matters even when a preventive is on board. Many dogs who meet the organism never look sick. The ones who do most often show fever, lethargy, and a shifting lameness as different legs take turns hurting. A small subset develops Lyme nephritis, a protein-losing kidney disease that behaves nothing like a sore wrist and can become life-threatening.

A positive antibody test means exposure, which is common in endemic towns and rare in places where these ticks do not live. Exposure and illness are different states. The 2018 ACVIM consensus statement, written by Littman and colleagues in the Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine, is the reference clinics use when they decide which seropositive dog actually needs antibiotics.

Which dogs get sick

Risk follows the tick map and the dog's habits. Dogs who hunt, hike, or live on the edge of woods in the Northeast, the upper Midwest, and parts of the northern Pacific coast meet Ixodes nymphs that are small enough to miss in a long coat. Any breed can be infected. Retrievers and other sporting dogs show up often because of how they spend their weekends. No Lyme gene explains that pattern. Puppies and adults are both susceptible. A dog who never leaves a sidewalk in a low-risk state is unlikely to have Lyme disease as the cause of a limp, and the travel history matters if the family recently visited an endemic area.

The kidney form is uncommon next to simple exposure. It is reported more often in retrievers, although any seropositive dog with protein in the urine needs that finding taken seriously. Dogs with no outdoor access, and dogs on consistent tick control who still test positive, still deserve a urinalysis. The antibody can linger long after the tick bite that caused it.

Signs owners notice

The classic complaint is a dog who was fine, then becomes dull and lame, then seems better, then favors a different leg. Fever, a poor appetite, and swollen joints can accompany that shifting lameness. Some dogs only act "off" for a few days. The kidney form looks like a different disease: more thirst, more urine, vomiting, weight loss, and sometimes swelling of the legs or belly from protein loss. A limp that never shifts and that follows a jump off the couch is more likely an orthopedic injury than Borrelia.

  • Fever and tiredness in a dog who lives where blacklegged ticks are common.
  • Lameness that moves from leg to leg over days.
  • A joint that feels warm or looks puffy, with the dog flinching when it is flexed.
  • Increased drinking and urination, vomiting, or sudden weakness, which raise concern for the kidney form.
  • A tick found embedded in the coat, especially a tiny nymph, in the weeks before the illness.

How a veterinarian diagnoses it

Lyme disease in dogs is a clinical diagnosis backed by serology in a dog with compatible signs. The veterinarian looks for shifting-leg lameness, fever, or protein-losing kidney disease, and pairs that with a C6 antibody assay or a clinic Lyme test. Bloodwork and a urine protein check look for the kidney form. The 2018 ACVIM Lyme consensus is the reference this workup follows, and the questions lower on this page separate exposure from illness.

In the exam room the common antibody screen is a C6 peptide test, including the Lyme line on a multi-analyte snap test that also looks at heartworm, Ehrlichia, and Anaplasma. C6 is a region the organism expresses during infection, so the result is read as exposure. A quantitative C6 assay can put a number on the antibody level when the clinician wants more than a yes or no. It still does not, by itself, order an antibiotic for a bright dog with a normal urine protein. A chemistry panel, a complete blood count, and a urinalysis with a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio are how the kidney form is hunted. Joint taps are uncommon and are used when a single swollen joint could be immune-mediated disease or a bacterial septic joint that needs a different drug.

Treatment categories

A dog with clinical Lyme borreliosis is treated with an antibiotic the veterinarian selects, commonly a tetracycline such as doxycycline, at a dose and length the veterinarian sets. Lyme nephritis needs more than that short course. It is a protein-losing nephropathy and may need hospitalization, fluids, and kidney care. A healthy dog with only an antibody result does not automatically receive antibiotics.

Pain control for the lame, febrile dog is a separate choice and is also dosed by the clinician, because an anti-inflammatory can affect the kidneys if the dog is dehydrated or already losing protein. The kidney form may need hospitalization, intravenous fluids, anti-nausea treatment, and drugs aimed at protein loss and blood pressure. That plan is individualized from the lab sheet. Tick prevention continues during treatment so a new bite does not complicate the picture. Vaccines against Lyme exist and are discussed for dogs who live or travel in endemic areas. A vaccine is a prevention tool. It does not treat a dog who is already lame and febrile.

Home care and prevention

Use a veterinarian-recommended tick preventive for the whole season when blacklegged ticks are active, and year-round where the clinic says the season never really stops. Check the coat daily after woods or tall grass, including the ears, the armpits, the groin, and between the toes. Remove an attached tick with fine tweezers or a tick tool, grasping close to the skin and pulling steadily, then note the date. Save the tick if your clinic wants to see it. Keep a seropositive dog's follow-up urine checks on the calendar even when the limp has resolved. Call if thirst increases or the dog starts vomiting, because those are the signs that move a stable dog back into the kidney workup.

When it is an emergency

When to See the Vet Immediately

A mild shifting limp can be booked promptly with your regular veterinarian. Collapse, repeated vomiting, severe weakness, swollen legs, labored breathing, or a dog who cannot keep water down should be seen on an emergency basis. Those signs fit Lyme nephritis and also fit other killers such as leptospirosis, immune-mediated disease, and pancreatitis. Do not wait for a planned antibody recheck if the dog is getting systemically sick. A feverish dog who cannot walk on any leg also deserves same-day care so a septic joint or a spinal problem can be caught in time.

What to ask about the bill

Uncomplicated Lyme disease in dogs, the fever and shifting-leg lameness caused by Borrelia burgdorferi, usually improves after a veterinarian-chosen antibiotic. The 2018 ACVIM consensus (Littman and colleagues, Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine) treats that illness as responsive, while Lyme nephritis, the protein-losing kidney form, often gets worse despite antibiotics. A positive antibody test by itself shows exposure. Exposure alone does not get an antibiotic.

Split the Lyme bill before any kidney test is run. A SNAP or C6 antibody test plus a short antibiotic course is a primary-care visit. Protein in the urine, a chemistry panel, and hospitalization for Lyme nephritis are the expensive branch. Ask the clinic to price those two paths separately. A policy started after the positive test will treat the infection as pre-existing.

Key Questions

Should every positive Lyme test be treated?

A bright dog with a positive C6 or snap result and a normal urine protein often needs monitoring, and the ACVIM consensus does not put that dog on antibiotics automatically. Treatment is aimed at dogs with compatible illness, and at seropositive dogs who are losing protein in the urine. Ask for the urinalysis result in writing so the decision uses the kidney findings together with the antibody line.

How long do antibodies stay positive?

Antibodies can remain detectable for months after exposure, so a later positive test does not clock a brand-new bite. The veterinarian reads the number, if a quantitative C6 was sent, against the dog's signs and the urine protein. A falling number in a dog who has recovered is interesting. It does not replace the urine check that screens for the kidney form.

Do Lyme vaccines replace tick preventives?

No vaccine removes the need to kill ticks. Preventives reduce attachment time, and a check of the coat still finds the ticks that got through. Vaccination is a separate conversation for dogs with heavy exposure in endemic regions. Puppies and newly adopted dogs should finish the schedule the clinic recommends before tick season peaks, and travelers should mention the destination at the visit before the trip.
