Marijuana and THC Toxicity in Pets

Marijuana and THC Toxicity in Pets belong in the care list for the Marijuana and THC Toxicity in Pets. Covers symptoms, emergency treatment, and keeping cannabis products away from pets.

THC is the compound that makes dogs and cats sick

Marijuana products expose pets to THC, the psychoactive cannabinoid. Dogs are the most common patients because they eat edibles, butter, and plant material left on tables. Cats are exposed less often and can be affected when they nibble plant or lick a tincture. THC disrupts normal signaling in the nervous system. The result is a pet who looks drunk: a wobbly walk, dull or dilated pupils, a startled reaction to sound, drooling, vomiting, and urine leaking. Heart rate is often slow. Some pets become profoundly sedated. A few become agitated. Tremors and, less often, seizures can occur with concentrated products.

Edibles complicate the picture. Chocolate brownies add theobromine. Sugar-free gummies may contain xylitol, which drops blood sugar and can injure the liver. Butter and oil infusions are high in fat and can inflame the pancreas. A pet who ate a plate of brownies needs the clinic to hear "THC" and "chocolate" and "possible xylitol" if the recipe was sugar-free. Bring the package. If the product was from a dispensary, the milligram marking on the label helps the clinician. You still should not try to decide at home whether that number is small enough to ignore.

What to do in the first hour

Pick up remaining product and keep other pets away. Phone your veterinarian or an emergency clinic about the THC exposure. A poison hotline can help: ASPCA Animal Poison Control at 888-426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline at 855-764-7661. Tell them the form: dried flower, edible, oil, or vape cartridge. Estimate how much is missing and when it was eaten. Leave vomiting to the clinic unless the veterinarian on the THC call clears it. A sedated pet can inhale vomit, and some oils are risky to bring back up. Do not give hydrogen peroxide because a friend said it works. Do not pour water into the mouth of a pet who cannot swallow reliably.

Transport the pet in a carrier or a crate, with the lights low and the ride quiet. A wobbly dog can fall off a seat. If urine is leaking, a towel protects the bedding and also shows the clinic a classic sign. If the pet is barely responsive, having trouble breathing, or tremoring continuously, say that when you call ahead so the team is ready. At the hospital, care is usually supportive: keeping the pet safe and warm, monitoring heart rate, treating agitation or vomiting, and watching blood sugar if xylitol is possible. There is no specific THC reversal you should attempt from a human medicine cabinet. Intravenous lipid therapy is sometimes used in hospital for severe cases. That decision belongs to the clinician.

Timing and the second wave from the edible

Signs often begin within a couple of hours and can last many hours because THC is stored in fat and released over time. A pet can look worse before looking better. Drowsiness into the next morning is common and still should be reported if you were told to watch at home. Call back for trouble breathing, seizures, pale gums, or vomiting that will not stop. The next day, belly pain and repeated vomiting can mean pancreatitis from the butter, which is a new problem even after the THC sedation fades. Ask the clinic what that looks like before you leave.

Synthetic cannabinoids and some vape liquids cause a more severe agitation and tremor picture than many people expect from "just marijuana." Name the product honestly. Emergency teams are there to treat the pet. Accurate history changes the monitoring. A cartridge chewed open can also be a plastic foreign body. Mention the plastic if pieces are missing.

Keeping products away from paws

Store flower, edibles, and oils in a latched cabinet, in the original container, never in a jar that looks like a treat tin. Guests' backpacks belong on a hook. Outdoor plants and discarded ends on a sidewalk are a walk hazard. Use a leave-it cue and a short leash where you know people smoke. Ash and leftover joints in a cup are enough for a small dog. Tell sitters and teenagers the rule in writing: any possible ingestion is a phone call the same hour, with the package, and nobody induces vomiting on their own.

If you use medical products yourself, dose them in a room the pet cannot enter and wash your hands. A drop on a counter is a lick. Compost that includes plant material should be fenced. The goal is a boring kitchen. The emergency is manageable when you are honest and fast, and it becomes dangerous when chocolate, xylitol, or a sedated airway is added to the THC. Make the call while the pet is still only wobbly.

Stairs, urine, and the morning after

Block stairs and keep the pet on a bed they cannot roll off. Leaking urine is a common THC sign and also a mess. Use a towel and mention it to the clinic. If an edible might contain chocolate or xylitol, say that even if you are unsure. The next morning, a painful belly and more vomiting can be the butter, which is a new problem after the sedation eases. Call back for breathing changes. Store the next batch in a latched cabinet before guests arrive, because backpacks on the floor are how the story repeats.

Edibles, fat, and the overnight watch

A chocolate edible combines THC with theobromine. A sugar-free gummy may add xylitol. Butter and oil infusions add a pancreatitis risk that shows up as next-day belly pain. Tell the clinic all three possibilities if the recipe is unknown, and bring wrappers. Signs of THC often include a wobbly walk, urine leaking, and a slow heart rate, and they can last into the next day. If you are sent home, keep the pet in a quiet room, block stairs, and call back for breathing trouble, seizures, or vomiting that continues. Do not start a second home remedy in the middle of the night.

Storage has to survive guests. Backpacks go on hooks. Jars that look like treat tins are a bad place for edibles. Tell sitters in writing that any suspected ingestion is a call, with the package, and that vomiting is a veterinary decision. A sedated pet can inhale vomit. Accurate history helps more than embarrassment hurts. The team needs the product name to watch blood sugar and the heart, and to decide whether the fat load needs its own plan.

Questions about marijuana and pets

What does THC toxicity look like in a dog or cat?

A wobbly or drunken walk, dilated or glassy pupils, drooling, vomiting, urine dribbling, and a slow heart rate are typical. Some pets become very sedated. Tremors can occur with concentrated products. Edibles may add chocolate, xylitol, or a fat load.

Should I make my pet vomit after eating an edible?

Only if a veterinarian tells you to. Sedated pets can inhale vomit, and peroxide given on a guess causes its own harm. Call a clinic or a poison hotline, bring the package, and say whether the edible contains chocolate or xylitol.

How long will the signs last?

Often many hours, sometimes into the next day, because THC lingers in fat. Supportive care in a clinic is the usual treatment. Call back for seizures, breathing difficulty, or vomiting that continues. Butter-based products can cause pancreatitis after the sedation fades.
