Best Bird Cages

Covers cage sizes for parakeets to macaws, bar spacing, materials, and setup accessories.

Bar spacing decides whether the cage is safe

Spacing is the first measurement, ahead of color and ahead of a play top. A bird who can pass a head through the bars and whose body stays inside can strangle. A bird who can squeeze an entire skull and shoulders through will be on the curtain rod by evening. Aviculturists commonly use about 3/8 inch between bars for finches and canaries, about 1/2 inch for budgerigars, about 5/8 to 3/4 inch for cockatiels, about 3/4 to 1 inch for conures and many small parrots, and about 1 to 1.5 inches of very strong bar for large cockatoos and macaws. Those are working ranges. Measure the bird you have, especially a slim lovebird or a parrotlet who can thread a gap sized for a bigger cousin, and ask the seller for the spacing in inches before you pay.

Strength is the second measurement. A budgie cage collapses under a macaw's beak, and the welds that look cute in a starter kit become shards. Large parrots need thick bars and hardware they cannot unbend. Powder-coated steel is the everyday material when the coating is intact and the species is a light chewer. Stainless costs more and tolerates washing and beaks better. Galvanized wire that a parrot can flake is a zinc risk. If a used cage shows gray metal under a chewed corner, retire it or have a bird-savvy person evaluate whether the metal is safe. Repaint with a paint made for animal cages only when you know the underlying metal is appropriate. A hardware-store rust spray is a fume and a toxin problem.

Width, height, and who actually flies

Finches and canaries use horizontal distance. A long, relatively low flight cage lets them launch and land, which is the exercise their bodies expect. A tall narrow tower with one perch teaches them to cling and to stay still. Give them length enough for a short flight, several perch stations, and a bar spacing from the finch range above. Budgies also do better when the cage is wider than it is a decorative column. They are flyers who get sold column cages because the column fits a corner. If the wings cannot extend fully without hitting bars or toys, the cage is a holding pen, and the bird needs a different box plus real out-of-cage flight in a safe room.

Cockatiels, conures, and larger parrots climb as well as fly. They need width to spread both wings, height to climb, and horizontal bars or a textured interior they can grip. A common failure is a tall show cage with slick vertical bars and a tiny door. The bird lives on one dowel, the feet take a repetitive load, and the person struggles to reach in without pinning a wing. Look for a wide front, a door your forearm can pass through during training, and a footprint that lets you hang two or three toys without creating a solid wall of plastic. Out-of-cage time on a play stand is part of the housing plan for parrots. The cage is the bedroom and the kitchen. It is a poor substitute for the whole day if the bird is a social species sitting alone in a back room.

Doors, grates, skirts, and bowls

A deep slide-out tray with a grate above it keeps birds off their droppings and makes the daily paper change possible without a wrestling match. Some birds pick at the grate and drop food into the tray. That is still cleaner than a solid floor covered in husks the bird eats along with feces. Seed skirts or clear guards around the lower edge cut the mess on your floor. They should come off for washing, because a fixed skirt full of damp millet grows mold against the bars. Confirm the tray actually slides once those guards are on. Some designs look complete in a photo and bind as soon as you add the skirt at home.

Food and water belong in stainless cups you can remove from outside, or in bowls on holders the bird cannot flip. Inside bowls that require you to invade the cage with a hand every morning slow you down, and skipped cleanings follow. Budgets should include a second set of cups so a dirty one can soak while the spare is in service. Spring-latch doors need a second lock for any bird who has learned to lift a simple wire. Cockatoos and many macaws will study a latch for days. A cheap clip is entertainment. A lock designed for parrots is equipment. Test it with the bird out of reach the first afternoon so you learn the two-step before the bird does.

Perches, toys, and the foot problem

Feet need variety. A single round dowel, the same diameter every day, loads the same pads and contributes to pressure sores on heavy birds. Use natural branches of safe wood in two or three diameters, a rope perch that is kept clean and replaced when it frays, and a flat wood platform for a parrot who likes to sunbathe inside. Sandpaper tubes and rough grit covers as the main perch abrade skin. They are a poor answer to nail growth. Nails are a grooming and veterinary task, handled with a perch the bird can stand on comfortably and with trims when the nails overgrow.

Toys are part of the cage purchase even though they come from a different shelf. A bare beautiful cage produces a bored bird who screams or plucks. Rotate two or three toys, keep cotton rope ends trimmed so a toe cannot catch a loop, and skip long loose strings. Hooks should close. Open S-hooks are how a bird wears a toy like a necklace. Budget a yearly toy habit into the cage decision. A mansion with nothing to shred is still an empty room, and the bird will shred the windowsill once you open the door.

Where the cage stands in the house

Kitchens kill birds through fumes people forget are fumes. Place the cage in a lived-in room away from the range, out of a draft from a window that never quite shuts, and out of all-day blasting sun with no shade. The bird should be able to retreat to a side of the cage that feels covered. A cage in the center of a shouting room, with no visual barrier, leaves some species on edge all day. Eye level or slightly above works for many parrots so they are part of the household without being underfoot. Finches often prefer a quieter, higher, longer cage where traffic does not pass at perch height.

Sleep is a housing issue. Many parrots need a long dark night, around twelve hours, or they get hormonal and loud. A separate sleep cage can be smaller because the bird only sleeps there, and it still needs safe bar spacing, a familiar perch, and clean air. Covering a cage is fine when the cover cannot be chewed into a tangle and the room still gets ventilation. A cover in a sunny window turns into a heat trap by 8 a.m. if you leave for work. Set an alarm to uncover, or skip the cover and use a dark room.

Used cages, price bands, and the setup you add later

Used cages are a good deal when the bars are straight, the spacing fits the species, the coating is intact, and every latch still works. Run a magnet if you are unsure about metal, scrub with bird-safe cleaner, and rinse until no residue smell remains. Walk away from rust bloom, bent doors a bird already defeated, and any cage that housed a bird with a contagious disease you cannot verify is gone. New starter cages for budgies often sell around $40 to $120 and are frequently too narrow. A wider flight-style budgie or finch cage more often lands around $100 to $250. Cockatiel and small parrot cages commonly run about $150 to $400. Large parrot cages, especially stainless or heavy powder-coated macaw homes, move into the hundreds and past a thousand dollars. . Stands, cups, and the first basket of toys sit on top of the tag.

Measure the doorway and the car before you fall for a cage that will not leave the store. Measure ceiling height if the cage includes a play top the bird will use with wings open. A perfect cage parked across the only escape path in a fire is a human safety problem as well as a daily annoyance. Leave a path, leave a socket for a full-spectrum avian lamp if your avian veterinarian wants one for a species who needs more light, and keep that lamp on a timer so the bird still gets a night.

Cage questions by species

How wide should a budgie cage be?

Wide enough that both wings can extend without striking bars or a wall of toys, and preferably long enough for a short flight between perches. Column cages that are mostly height leave flyers sitting still. Bar spacing around 1/2 inch keeps a budgie from slipping out. Add stainless cups you can pull from the outside and a slide-out tray you will actually change.

Are galvanized cages safe for parrots?

Zinc from galvanized metal that a parrot chews or flakes is a toxicity risk. Prefer stainless or an intact powder coat over a metal appropriate for birds, and retire cages where gray metal shows through a chewed corner. Skip hardware-store paints and rust sprays. If you are unsure about a used cage, have a bird-savvy veterinarian or an experienced aviary keeper look before a macaw moves in.

Can a smaller cage be used only for sleep?

A sleep cage can be smaller than the daytime cage because the bird is only roosting, and it still needs correct bar spacing, a familiar safe perch, and air that stays clear. Give many parrots a long dark night, about twelve hours, without parking the cage in a kitchen or under a cover that becomes a heat trap in morning sun. The daytime cage still has to be wide enough for wings and toys.
