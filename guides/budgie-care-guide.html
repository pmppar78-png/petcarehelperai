Budgie Care Guide

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Detailed guide to budgie care covering diet, cage setup, health issues, training, and why budgies make excellent first birds.

Housing and Enclosure

A budgerigar needs room to fly inside the cage, measured in inches you can check with a tape. For one bird, think of a cage at least 18 inches wide, 18 inches deep, and 18 inches high, and treat that as a cramped starting point. A flight cage closer to 30 inches wide lets a budgie move between perches without clipping a wing on the bars. Bar spacing should be about 1/2 inch so the head cannot pass through. Horizontal bars help a climber. The cage belongs in a bright room where the family actually sits, away from the kitchen. Overheated nonstick pans release fumes that kill birds, and that includes "PTFE" coatings on some cookware and heat lamps. Skip scented candles and aerosol sprays in the same room.

Perches should vary in diameter and material: a natural branch, a rope perch, and one smooth dowel, arranged so droppings fall clear of the food dishes. One sandpaper perch is enough to abrade feet and does not belong as the only standing place. Swings, bells, and shreddable paper toys get used. Mirrors entertain some budgies and make others obsessive and territorial. If a bird guards the mirror and attacks your hand, take the mirror out. Budgies are flock birds. A single bird needs hours of attention or a compatible companion. Two birds are often kinder than one bird left alone in a covered cage while everyone is at work. Introduce a second bird carefully, because a resident can bully a newcomer.

Sleep is a housing issue. Budgies need about ten to twelve hours of quiet dark. A cage cover or a dark room does the job. A television under the cage all night does not. Drafts from a window and a heater blasting the bars both cause trouble. Daytime light can include a bird-safe full-spectrum lamp on a timer if the room is dim. That lamp supports a normal day length. Many avian veterinarians suggest asking about a bird-specific full-spectrum lamp for indoor budgies, because window glass filters UVB (Association of Avian Veterinarians; Merck Veterinary Manual, Nutrition in Pet Birds). Pellets and a cuttlebone still do the nutritional work.

Diet and Nutrition

The daily diet is a budgie pellet, or a measured mix of pellet and a quality seed blend, plus fresh greens. An all-seed cup, refilled whenever it looks empty, is mostly millet hulls and oil. That pattern leads to obesity and fatty liver disease. Aim for pellets as the majority of the bowl, with seed as a smaller portion while you convert a seed-addicted bird, and vegetables every day: leafy greens, broccoli, carrot tops, and herbs. Fruit is occasional because it is sugary. Millet spray is a training treat. A bird that gets a whole spray every morning will ignore the pellet and the greens.

Calcium for a budgie comes from a cuttlebone or a mineral block, plus the formulated pellet. Hens that lay need that calcium and still should be discouraged from constant laying with a long night and no nest box. Keep avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, fruit pits, and salty plate scraps out of the cage. Fresh water daily, in a dish the bird will actually use. Some budgies prefer a bottle. Wash either one, because seed soup grows bacteria by afternoon.

Conversion from seed to pellet takes patience. Offer the pellet in the morning when the bird is hungry, and limit the seed to a measured afternoon portion. A bird that sits fluffed and loses weight during a conversion needs the seed returned and a slower plan, plus an avian veterinarian if the weight drop is sharp. Weigh a budgie on a gram scale. A few grams is a large percentage of a 30-gram bird.

Health and Veterinary

Megabacteria, also called avian gastric yeast, scaly face mites, fatty liver disease, tumors, and respiratory infections are the budgie problems clinics see. Scaly face looks like crusty build-up around the cere and beak and needs a proper mite treatment. Petroleum jelly alone leaves the mites in place. A seed-only cup plus a cage with no flight path is the usual road to fatty liver. Tumors are common in older budgies and show up as a lump or a sudden loss of balance. Respiratory disease looks like a bobbing tail, a click, or discharge at the nares. Any of those signs need an avian veterinarian. A dog-and-cat clinic that is willing to "take a look" may miss the dose and the species.

Droppings are your daily lab. A healthy budgie produces a dark fecal part, white urates, and a small amount of clear urine. Persistent watery droppings, yellow urates, or undigested seed in the feces are reasons to call. Fluffed feathers, a bird on the cage floor, and a tail that bobs with each breath are emergencies in an animal this small. Budgies hide illness until they cannot. Annual wellness exams, including a weight, catch problems while there is still time to feed and medicate.

Wing trimming, if you choose it, should leave the bird able to glide down, and it should be done by someone who knows budgie molt patterns. A bird stripped to the shaft crashes and then fears hands. Nails and beaks usually stay in shape with varied perches and a cuttlebone. Bleeding from a broken blood feather needs cornstarch or styptic and a calm room, then a vet if it will not stop.

Taming and Company

  • Let a new budgie eat and sleep for several days before you ask it to step up.
  • Offer millet from your hand at the cage door, then inside, then on a play stand.
  • Keep sessions short. A budgie that flies off is done for that round.
  • Teach step-up with a perch first if the bird fears fingers.
  • Cover or darken the cage at a consistent bedtime, away from kitchen fumes.

A comfortable budgie chatters, grinds its beak before sleep, and flies to the front perch when you open the seed lid. A silent, fluffed bird at the bottom is the opposite picture and needs an avian veterinarian the same day. These are small parrots with real lifespans, often 7 to 15 years when the diet and the night routine are steady, and they reward a household that talks to them.

Millet, a Dark Night, and a Gram Scale

Use millet as a paycheck. An open pantry of millet replaces the pellet. A spray in the hand teaches step-up. A spray in the cup all day teaches the budgie to ignore pellets and greens, which is how fatty liver and obesity show up in a bird that still looks busy. Offer the pellet in the morning, vegetables beside it, and a measured seed portion later if you are still converting. Weigh the bird in grams. A budgie is often near 30 grams, and a small drop is a large percentage. If weight falls during a conversion, slow down and call an avian veterinarian if the bird fluffs or sits low.

Nights should last about ten to twelve hours, in a quiet dark room or under a cover, away from a television. Days can include a bird-safe lamp if the room is dim. Calcium is a cuttlebone and the pellet. Keep the cage out of the kitchen so an overheated nonstick pan cannot fill the room with fumes. Bar spacing near 1/2 inch and a cage wider than 18 inches, ideally closer to 30 inches across, let the bird fly between perches. A second budgie is often the kindest company for a bird left alone all day, introduced carefully so the resident does not bully the newcomer. Annual exams catch scaly face, respiratory disease, and the lumps older budgies grow, while there is still time to help.

Quick Answers

Are budgies good for first-time bird owners?

Yes, for someone who will use a cage at least 18 inches in each direction, feed pellets plus greens, and find an avian veterinarian. They still need daily interaction or a second budgie, and they still suffer on an all-seed diet.

How much does budgie care cost?

A proper flight cage, perches, and the first exam are the opening costs. A 2026 roundup of exotic-clinic fees puts a routine avian wellness exam at about $95 to $185 before tests (findanexoticvet.com). The Association of Avian Veterinarians recommends regular checkups and does not set a national price (aav.org). Pellets and produce stay modest each month, while an illness in a bird this small can cost more than the setup.
