Chameleon Care Guide

Guide to keeping pet chameleons including Veiled and Panther species. Covers enclosure, misting, UVB, diet, and why chameleons need experienced owners.

Housing and Enclosure

Chameleons are arboreal specialists, and the common pet species, veiled and panther, need tall screen cages with live plants and moving air. A veiled adult does well in something around 24 inches wide, 24 inches deep, and 48 inches tall, and larger is easier to landscape. Panther chameleons want a similar vertical space, with males needing the full height. Glass tanks trap stale air and turn into saunas. Screen cages in a quiet room, out of drafts from an air vent, are the usual successful setup. One chameleon per cage. They view other chameleons, including their reflection, as rivals.

Lighting is a basking bulb plus a linear UVB tube, on a timer for about 12 hours. Veiled chameleons often bask with a body temperature in the mid to upper 80s F, with a basking spot you measure on a branch around 85 to 95 F depending on the individual and the species. Panthers are similar and sometimes a little cooler at the top of the range. The lower cage should be several degrees down so the animal can retreat. A thermostat or a carefully raised fixture keeps the top branch from cooking a chameleon that has nowhere else to go. Night temperatures can fall into the 60s for most captive veiled and panther chameleons. Heat at night is only for a room that crashes below that.

UVB must reach the basking branch at the strength the bulb chart describes for a basking reptile, and there must be leaves below it. A chameleon that cannot escape the tube will sit with closed eyes and stop eating. Replace linear UVB on schedule. Branches of different diameters, placed horizontally and at diagonals, matter more than any plastic vine. Live pothos, schefflera, or a ficus give drinking surfaces and sight breaks. Confirm plant safety before you install it. Pesticides on a nursery plant will kill a chameleon that licks the leaves.

Hydration is a droplet system. A bowl by itself rarely gets the job done. Most chameleons drink moving droplets. A dripper over a plant for an hour or two each morning, plus a misting session or an automatic mister, keeps them drinking. The cage should dry between mistings. Constant drip onto a soaked floor grows bacteria and respiratory disease. A clear glass at the bottom lets you see that water is being offered and also lets you spot a chameleon that has fallen, which is an emergency in this family.

Diet and Nutrition

Feed gut-loaded insects: crickets, dubia roaches, silkworms, and the occasional hornworm. Veiled chameleons also chew plant matter and may nibble the pothos. That browsing does not replace the insects. Dust calcium without D3 at most feedings when the UVB tube is strong and correctly placed. Add calcium with D3 about twice a month, and a multivitamin about twice a month, unless your reptile veterinarian adjusts that rhythm. A heavy D3 dust on every cricket under a strong UVB tube is how some chameleons get oversupplemented. Insects should be a sensible size for the animal's mouth. A handful dumped into the cage and left overnight will bite a sleeping chameleon and die in the screen corners.

Juveniles eat daily. Adults can eat every other day, with veiled chameleons watched carefully for obesity. A veiled that looks like a fist on a branch is overfed. Variety matters because a cricket-only diet, even dusted, is a narrow gut-load. Keep the feeder colony itself well fed on greens and a commercial gut-load so nutrition is already inside the insect before any powder is added.

Supplements stay on the insects. Do not mix a calcium slurry into a water glass and call it hydration. Chameleons may not drink standing water at all, and a thick powder in a dripper clogs the line and doses the animal unpredictably. If you suspect a deficiency, the fix is a veterinary exam plus a corrected UVB distance. A new jar from a general pet aisle leaves a bad lamp distance unchanged.

Health and Veterinary Care

Metabolic bone disease is common and looks like a weak grip, a bowed limb, or a chameleon that can no longer shoot the tongue straight. It traces to UVB that never reached the branch, a burned-out tube, or calcium that was forgotten. Dehydration shows as sunken eyes and a thick, sticky tongue. Respiratory infections follow cold, wet, stale cages: gaping at rest, bubbles, and a posture that stays hunched. Eye injuries and infections swell the turret shut. Egg binding in females, including veiled females that produce eggs without a male, is a life-threatening swelling and straining that needs a reptile veterinarian the same day. Do not "wait for her to pass them" over a weekend.

Stress is a medical problem in this group. A chameleon in the center of a busy room, with a hand in the cage every evening, stops eating and fades from the bright colors people bought it for. Watching from a distance, feeding with a cup, and keeping the cage visually broken up with plants is handling enough for many animals. Some individuals will walk onto a hand. Many will not, and that preference deserves respect. Parasites are frequent in imported animals. A fecal test at the first visit belongs on the setup list next to the dripper.

Find a veterinarian who has treated chameleons before you bring one home. The species is a poor beginner reptile because the cage, the water, and the UVB all have to be right at once. A veiled chameleon from a fair, placed in a glass tank with a heat rock, is the case clinics see in the first month. Budget for that level of equipment or choose a different lizard.

Keeper Routine

  • Run basking and UVB on a timer, and measure the basking branch after you move any plant that now shades it.
  • Drip or mist in the morning and let the cage dry later.
  • Feed dusted, gut-loaded insects and pull the leftovers.
  • Look at the eyes and the grip every day. Sunken eyes and missed branches are early alarms.
  • Keep other pets and ceiling fans from blasting the screen. Drafts and barking dogs at the glass are constant stress.

A comfortable chameleon sits in a plant, tail coiled, eyes moving independently, and changes color with the light. A dark, collapsed animal on the floor of the cage needs heat, fluids from a veterinarian, and a review of last night's temperatures. These are extraordinary animals to observe and demanding animals to keep.

Droplets, Branches, and a Cage That Dries

Each morning, confirm three things: the dripper or mister actually ran, the basking branch is in the 85 to 95 F band your species uses, and the linear UVB still sits at the charted distance above that branch. A plant that grew into the beam can shade the basking site or push the chameleon too close to the bulb. Leaves must also offer an escape from the light. Veiled and panther chameleons drink moving water. A bowl on the floor is a backup at best. Let the screen cage dry between mistings so the air does not stay wet and stale.

Gut-load the insects and dust calcium without D3 at most feedings when UVB is strong, with D3 and a multivitamin only a couple of times a month unless your reptile veterinarian sets a different rhythm. Sunken eyes mean dehydration. A missed grip or a tongue that shoots crooked can mean metabolic bone disease and needs a clinic, plus a check that the UVB tube is the one you think it is. Females can bind eggs without ever seeing a male. Straining and a swollen body are a same-day emergency. These lizards are for keepers who enjoy watching more than handling. Many will never want a hand, and the plants are the enrichment that matters.

Quick Answers

Are chameleons good for beginner reptile owners?

Most beginners should wait. Veiled and panther chameleons need a tall screen cage, a drip or mist system, measured basking heat, and a linear UVB tube, and they handle stress poorly.

How much does chameleon care cost?

Screen cage, automatic mister or dripper, linear UVB, and live plants often push the opening setup well past a basic lizard kit. Feeder insects and veterinary visits continue, and a female with eggs can become an emergency bill.
