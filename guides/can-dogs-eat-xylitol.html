Xylitol Poisoning in

Why xylitol (birch sugar) is deadly for dogs. Products containing xylitol, symptoms, emergency treatment, and prevention guide.

Xylitol triggers a dog's insulin

Xylitol is a sweetener used in sugar-free gum, mints, some peanut butters, baked goods, toothpaste, mouthwash, gummy vitamins, and nicotine gum. In people it does not spike insulin in a dangerous way. In dogs it can trigger a sharp release of insulin. Blood sugar falls. A dog can become weak, stagger, vomit, or seize. Larger exposures are also tied to liver injury that shows up hours to a couple of days later, with vomiting, jaundice, and bleeding tendencies. Both problems are emergencies. A piece of gum that looks trivial in a pocket can be enough for a small dog, which is why this page refuses a "one piece is fine" rule.

Labels hide the word. Look for xylitol, birch sugar, and sometimes "sugar-free" as a prompt to read the ingredient panel. Products sweetened with stevia or erythritol are a different conversation, and you should still read them to be sure xylitol was not blended in. Gum is especially risky because dogs chew packs and because the xylitol amount per piece varies by brand and by year. The company may need to be contacted by the hotline. Keep the package.

Signs, including the ones that arrive late

Low blood sugar can develop within about half an hour to a few hours. You may see vomiting, a drunken walk, collapse, tremors, or a seizure. The dog can rally and then worsen. Liver injury is the second wave. A dog who seems stable in the afternoon can be in trouble overnight. Pale gums, a yellow tint in the eyes or skin, vomiting, and unusual bruising are reasons to return even if the first visit felt reassuring. Do not offer a spoon of sugar or honey as a substitute for veterinary care. A clinician may use dextrose as part of treatment. A kitchen experiment delays the drive and can choke a dog who is too weak to swallow.

Other sweeteners and other toxins confuse the picture. Sugar-free chocolate may contain both theobromine and xylitol. Peanut butter used to hide a pill may be the xylitol source. Toothpaste left on a bathroom floor is a common night story. Tell the hotline every product, because the monitoring plan changes when the liver is at risk as well as the blood sugar.

What to do immediately

Take the gum or the food away and confine other pets. Call your veterinarian or an emergency clinic, and call a poison hotline if you cannot reach the clinic. Read the ingredient list to them, including the order of ingredients if you can, and estimate how many pieces are missing. Do not induce vomiting unless they explicitly tell you to. A dog who is already weak or seizuring can inhale vomit. Go in if they say go in. Bring the container even if it is empty.

Clinic care often includes checking blood sugar immediately, giving intravenous dextrose if it is low, decontamination when it is still appropriate, and repeat bloodwork to watch the liver. There is no household antidote. If you are sent home, ask how often to check for wobbliness and when the next lab test is. Set an alarm. Xylitol cases go wrong when everyone relaxes because the dog ate dinner and looked normal at 9 p.m.

House rules that prevent the next call

Keep purses off the floor. Gum lives in purses. Toothpaste and mouthwash belong inside a cabinet a nose cannot nudge open. Before you use peanut butter as a pill disguise or a toy stuffing, read the full ingredient list each time you buy a jar, because brands reformulate. If xylitol appears, choose a different jar and tell everyone in the house. Bake sales and "keto" desserts brought by guests need a question before they hit the counter. Children should know that sugar-free gum is a dog emergency, and that telling an adult quickly is the right move.

Write the two hotline numbers on a card inside the cabinet where medicines are stored. When a guest says "it is only gum," you already have the script: dogs release insulin in response to xylitol, people do not handle it the same way, and the package has to be read. That script is shorter than an ICU stay. Check every new "natural sweetener" before it becomes a habit in the kitchen.

  • Search labels for xylitol and birch sugar in gum, peanut butter, toothpaste, and baked goods.
  • Call a veterinarian or poison hotline for any suspected amount.
  • Do not induce vomiting unless you are told to, especially if the dog is weak or seizuring.
  • Bring the package so the product can be identified.

The second night

Low blood sugar is the early crisis. Liver injury can arrive after the dog has already eaten dinner and looked steady. If you were sent home, set an alarm for the recheck and for any middle-of-the-night wobbliness. Bring the gum pack or the jar again. A new weakness, vomiting, or a yellow tint in the eyes means you return even if the appointment is later. Tell sitters which cupboard holds the checked peanut butter and which shelf is off limits because a reformulated jar is how a second exposure happens.

Read drink mixes and flavored dental products too. Gum is only one source. A bathroom floor is a common place for a punctured tube of sweetened product overnight.

If a sitter will feed toys while you are away, show them the checked jar and the off-limits shelf before you leave. A reformulated peanut butter is a common second exposure.

Products people forget to read

Beyond gum, xylitol shows up in some powdered drink mixes, chewable antacids, children's vitamins, and peanut or almond butters aimed at a low-sugar diet. A dog who raids a bedside table may have eaten a combination of a sleep aid and a sugar-free mint. List every bottle that is open or on the floor. The hotline can rank which problem is fastest. Blood sugar is the early threat. Liver enzymes are the later one. If you are sent home after an initial check, set alarms for the recheck times they give you. A normal evening appetite is encouraging and still compatible with a liver hit the next day.

Prevention in a real week means a habit at the grocery store. Before a new jar or a new pack of gum goes into the pantry, one adult reads the ingredient panel out loud. If xylitol or birch sugar appears, that item gets a mark and a high shelf, and the dog-safe peanut butter stays in a different cupboard. Tell dog walkers and house sitters which cupboard is theirs. A well-meaning sitter who stuffs a toy from the wrong jar creates the exposure you already prevented once.

Questions about xylitol

Why is xylitol dangerous for dogs?

It can cause a dog's pancreas to release insulin, which drops blood sugar and may lead to weakness, collapse, or seizures. Some exposures also damage the liver over the following day or two. People tolerate xylitol differently, so a human serving is a meaningless guide.

Which products often contain xylitol?

Sugar-free gum and mints, some peanut butters, toothpaste, mouthwash, gummy vitamins, and sugar-free baked goods. The label may say xylitol or birch sugar. Bring the package to the clinic or read the ingredients to a poison hotline.

What should I do if my dog ate sugar-free gum?

Call a veterinarian or a poison hotline immediately, even if the dog looks normal. Hold vomiting unless the xylitol consult directs it. Low blood sugar can come on fast, and liver injury can come later, so ask when rechecks should happen.
