Catfish Care Guide

Complete care guide for Corydoras catfish covering species, tank setup, school size, feeding, breeding, and why they are ideal community fish.

Housing and Enclosure

Corydoras are armored catfish that live on the sand in groups, and the substrate is half the husbandry. Use fine sand. Rough gravel wears the barbels down to nubs, and those barbels are how the fish finds food. A tank of 20 gallons or more suits the common bronze, albino, and panda corydoras kept as a group of at least six of the same species. Pygmy corydoras are smaller and spend more time off the bottom, yet they still want a school and a soft floor. A lone cory in a community tank is a stressed cory, even if it eats.

Temperature for the usual aquarium species sits around 72 to 78 F. They are tropical, so a thermostat heater and a thermometer belong on the tank when the room cannot hold that range by itself. Corydoras sterbai prefers the warmer end of that band, while some other species are less fussy. Read the species on the bag. "Cory" at a shop can mean three different animals with three different adult sizes. Gentle filtration, a sponge or a guarded intake, protects the barbels and the eggs if you are lucky enough to get a spawn. Leaves, caves, and driftwood give the group a place to pile up at midday, which is normal social behavior and can happen while the filter is running well.

They share tanks well with tetras, rasboras, peaceful livebearers, and dwarf shrimp, provided nobody owns the bottom so aggressively that the corys never eat. Large cichlids and fast, hungry barbs are a poor match. Keep the water moving enough to stay oxygenated. Corydoras dart to the surface for a gulp of air now and then. That roll is normal. A whole group hanging at the surface and breathing hard means the water needs a test and stronger surface agitation.

Diet and Nutrition

Corydoras need their own meal. They will nose through leftover flakes and still go thin if that is all they get. Sinking pellets or wafers made for bottom feeders are the staple, dropped in the evening when the group is awake and the upper fish are less frantic. Frozen bloodworms, brine shrimp, and a blanched vegetable round out the week. They are poor algae eaters. A green-glass tank still needs a cory feeding, and the wafers have to reach the sand before the tetras steal them.

Feed after the lights dim if the community fish are bullies. Many keepers drop the pellets on the far side of a piece of wood so the catfish can work in peace. Pull uneaten wafers after an hour or two if your group is small. A school of eight will often finish a modest serving. A school of two will leave a rotting disk under the heater.

Color and belly shape tell you the diet is working. A healthy cory has a gently rounded abdomen and barbels that reach the sand. Sunken bellies in a fish that "always looks busy" usually mean the food is being eaten by someone else on the way down.

Health and Veterinary

Barbel erosion, ich, and bacterial infections are the corydoras problems tied directly to keeping. Red, shortened barbels point at gravel, at ammonia, or at both. Ich looks like fine white grains and shows up after new fish. These catfish are sensitive to salt and to many fish medicines. Do not follow a package dose written for a gourami. Ask a veterinarian who treats fish before you medicate, and move shrimp out of any tank that might receive a treatment. Half-strength guesswork is still a dose, and this guide will not give one.

The Merck Veterinary Manual ranks ammonia and nitrite poisoning as the leading aquarium emergency. It appears after an uncycled setup, extra food, a dead fish left on the sand, or a filter that stopped running. Keep both at 0 ppm. Corydoras cannot set their own temperature, so body function follows the water. A thermometer plus a thermostat heater fits these tropical catfish. Ich and similar parasites usually travel in on new fish or plants, so quarantine newcomers in a separate tank. Bacterial infections, including fin and barbel rot, usually follow dirty water or an injury. Copper treatments are poisonous to shrimp, snails, and live plants, and they can stall filter bacteria, which means you keep testing ammonia and nitrite for weeks after copper has been used.

The source for that water-quality framing is the Merck Veterinary Manual page on aquarium fish.

Spawning is a sign of comfort more than a goal. A female grows round, the males shiver against her, and eggs appear on the glass. You can raise fry in a separate tank on fine foods, or you can let the adults eat the eggs. Either choice is fine. What you should not ignore is a cory resting on its side for hours, or a group that stops the evening dash to the surface. Treat those as health signs.

Bringing a Group Home

  • Set the sand and the heater before you shop, and confirm ammonia and nitrite are at zero.
  • Buy six or more of one species and leave the six-species sampler at the shop.
  • Float the bag, mix tank water in slowly, and release them near the sand.
  • Add the school before louder fish if you are stocking a new community, so they learn the feeding spot first.
  • Rinse hands, skip the lotion, and net a cory only when a move is unavoidable.

Evening is when the tank should look alive at the bottom. The group trots across the open sand, piles into a corner, and then explodes into a short swim. A cory pressed into the glass with ragged barbels is asking for softer substrate and cleaner water, and no algae wafer will repair that.

Evening on the Sand

Corydoras wake up when the room quiets. If you only look at noon, you will think the group is shy and underfed. Sit with the tank after the lights dim and watch the trot across open sand. That patrol is the picture of a working school. Drop the sinking pellet where you saw them gather, on the far side of a piece of wood if the tetras are thieves. Pull any wafer still intact after a couple of hours so it does not sour under the heater.

Barbels tell the truth about the floor. Long, pale whiskers that touch the sand mean the substrate and the water are doing their jobs. Short, red, or missing barbels mean gravel, ammonia, or both. You cannot regrow a damaged barbel with a new food. You can stop the damage with sand and with ammonia and nitrite held at zero. During a water change, vacuum the open sand lightly. Corydoras spit and forage, and a deep gravel vacuum that rearranges the whole bed stresses them for no benefit.

When you add fish above them, add the cory group first so they learn the feeding corner. Quarantine new catfish even when they look identical to the ones you have. Different shop systems carry different ich and different bacteria, and corydoras show stress by clamping and by fading the barbels within days. A fish veterinarian should choose any medicine. These catfish handle salt and many common cures poorly, and a shrimp in the same tank handles copper worse. Count the school at the evening feed. A missing cory is often under the wood, and sometimes it is the ammonia source you have been chasing.

Quick Answers

Are corydoras good for beginner fish keepers?

Yes, they are hardy and peaceful, but they need a group of at least 6 of the same species, a tank of 20 gallons or more, and soft sand that will not wear down their barbels.

Can corydoras live on leftover food?

No. They are not cleanup crew. Feed sinking pellets or wafers as their staple, ideally in the evening when they are most active, and supplement with frozen foods.
