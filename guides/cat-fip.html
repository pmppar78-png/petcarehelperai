Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP)

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Understanding FIP in cats including wet and dry forms, diagnosis, the GS-441524 treatment breakthrough, and prognosis for affected cats.

A common intestinal virus, and the uncommon mutant

Feline infectious peritonitis begins with feline coronavirus, a contagious intestinal virus that many cats catch in multi-cat homes, shelters, and catteries. Most of those cats get a short bout of diarrhea or no illness you notice, and they never develop FIP. In an unlucky cat the virus mutates inside the body and the immune response builds a devastating disease. You cannot point to the moment of mutation from the litter box. What you can see is a young cat, often under two years, who develops a fever that will not settle, loses weight, and then takes one of two broad paths. The wet, or effusive, form fills the abdomen or the chest with fluid. The dry, or non-effusive, form builds inflammatory lesions in organs, eyes, or the nervous system without a big pocket of fluid. The same cat can show pieces of both. Dry disease is easier to miss, because the cat looks "chronically unwell" and the belly never balloons.

Stress, crowding, and a very young age raise the chance that coronavirus exposure ends in FIP. A single indoor cat with no housemates has a low chance of meeting the parent virus at all. Breeding catteries and shelter nurseries see more cases because kittens share the virus early. Genetic susceptibility is studied, and no household test can score it. The useful prevention is less crowding, clean litter practice, and a plan for introducing kittens that your veterinarian and, in a cattery, a shelter-medicine or feline practitioner can design. There is an old FIP vaccine. Routine use in household cats is uncommon in current feline-practitioner discussions, and the veterinarian can say whether it has any role for a specific high-risk setting. It is a poor substitute for husbandry.

Wet and dry disease look different

  • Wet FIP: a pot belly from abdominal fluid, or fast breathing when fluid is in the chest, often with a fever and a poor appetite.
  • The fluid is typically high in protein and relatively low in cells. A Rivalta test on that fluid supports the suspicion in the clinic.
  • Dry FIP: persistent fever, weight loss, dullness, and sometimes a thickened intestine or enlarged lymph nodes without a dramatic effusion.
  • Eyes: uveitis, a change in color of the iris, cloudiness, or sudden vision trouble.
  • Brain and spinal cord: wobbliness, seizures, behavior change, or a cat who cannot jump onto a familiar counter.

None of those signs belong only to FIP. Heart failure, pyothorax, lymphoma, toxoplasmosis, and liver disease can mimic pieces of the picture. That is why the diagnosis is built from several clues. One sign cannot stamp the disease. A cat with chest fluid needs the heart considered. A cat with a pot belly needs the fluid tapped and looked at, when it is safe to tap, so the laboratory clues exist.

How the case is built

No single blood test proves FIP in every cat in the clinic. The veterinarian stacks the exam with laboratory clues: a high globulin, a low albumin-to-globulin ratio, anemia or a lymphopenia, and an effusion profile when fluid is present. PCR on the effusion can support the diagnosis. A negative PCR on a cat who otherwise fits the picture leaves the suspicion standing, because the sample and the timing can miss virus. Antibody titers against feline coronavirus show exposure, which is common, and they do not by themselves diagnose FIP. Imaging finds the fluid, the thickened bowel, or the odd kidney, and it looks for the diseases on the mimic list. Neurologic and ocular cases may need a specialist exam. Ask the veterinarian to say, in plain language, how strong the case is before a long treatment starts. A probable diagnosis is sometimes the most honest label, and treatment decisions are made with that uncertainty named.

Breathing, collapse, and a hard belly

A cat struggling to breathe because of chest fluid, a kitten who collapses, or a seizure should be seen the same day at a hospital that can give oxygen and tap a chest if needed. Do not try to drain a belly at home. Do not give a leftover steroid while you wait for the appointment, because steroids can change fever and immune signs the veterinarian needs to interpret, and they are sometimes used later as part of a prescribed plan. Carry recent lab results if you already have them.

Treatment is a veterinary decision

Supportive care treats fever, dehydration, pain, and the mechanical problem of an effusion. Draining a chest so a cat can breathe is an emergency procedure. Draining an abdomen is done with judgment, because fluid returns and protein is lost. Nutrition matters, and a cat who stops eating needs a feeding plan so hepatic lipidosis does not join the list.

Antiviral treatment is the other half of the modern conversation, and it belongs to the prescribing veterinarian. Nucleoside analogs such as GS-441524 have produced long remissions in published groups of cats, including reports in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery. On May 10, 2024, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration said it does not intend to enforce new-animal-drug approval requirements for GS-441524 compounded for a specific cat with FIP when the pharmacy follows the conditions in Guidance for Industry 256. The agency also describes drugs compounded from bulk substances as unapproved. The legal and practical path families should discuss is a patient-specific prescription from the veterinarian, with a compounding pharmacy that veterinarian will stand behind. Unlabeled vials sold through social media sit outside that path. Do not buy them. Neurologic and ocular disease often need a plan the veterinarian adjusts, and some cats relapse, especially when the nervous system was involved. The dose, the length of the course, and the bloodwork that monitors it are clinical decisions. This page will not print a dose figure. Ask what one week costs, what lab checks are scheduled, and what a relapse would mean, before you commit to the full course.

Surgery does not cure FIP. A biopsy is sometimes part of reaching the diagnosis in a dry case, and it is a diagnostic procedure. After a remission, the veterinarian will tell you what to watch: appetite, weight, fever, eyes, and gait. A housemate who never showed disease does not need the antiviral because the sick cat improved. That housemate needs a coronavirus-and-husbandry conversation, which is different from a treatment course.

Money and expectations

Diagnosis can already be costly: fluid analysis, bloodwork, imaging, and sometimes PCR. The antiviral course is a multi-week compounding bill that varies with the cat's size and with whether the disease is neurologic or ocular. Exams and recheck labs sit on top. Ask the prescribing veterinarian which pharmacy they use and what the first week costs before you assume you can finish. Some families will face a quality-of-life choice if the cat is suffering and treatment cannot be obtained safely. That conversation is part of decent medicine. Questions about FIP

What is the difference between wet and dry FIP?

Wet FIP produces a high-protein effusion in the abdomen, the chest, or both, so you may see a pot belly or labored breathing. Dry FIP causes fever, weight loss, and inflammatory disease in organs, eyes, or the nervous system without that large fluid pocket. A cat can show features of both. Fluid can be tested with a Rivalta test and sometimes PCR. Dry disease leans on the exam, the protein pattern in the blood, imaging, and sometimes a biopsy. Neither form is a guess you should treat with a product from social media.

Did I cause this by missing a vaccine?

FIP follows a mutation of feline coronavirus inside an individual cat, usually a young one who met the common intestinal virus in a group setting. Most cats who catch that intestinal virus never develop FIP. Routine household use of the older FIP vaccine is uncommon, and skipping it is a question for your veterinarian. Crowding, stress, and kittenhood exposure matter more than a missed appointment on a dog-and-cat vaccine postcard. Supportive care and any antiviral plan still have to be prescribed for the sick cat in front of you.
