Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Understanding IBD in cats. Covers chronic vomiting and diarrhea, diagnosis, dietary trials, medication options, and long-term management.

Chronic enteropathy is the honest name

Inflammatory bowel disease in cats is a label for long-running inflammation of the stomach and intestines after parasites, infection, and a real diet trial have been accounted for. The signs are ordinary and exhausting: vomiting more than the occasional hairball, diarrhea that returns for weeks, weight loss, a change in appetite that swings from picky to ravenous, and sometimes a cat who still acts almost normal while the muscle over the spine disappears. Veterinarians increasingly say chronic enteropathy, because "IBD" sounds like one disease and the biopsy may later say small-cell lymphoma. Those two conditions can look identical in the waiting room and on an ultrasound. The difference is in the tissue, the stains, and a clonality test, and it changes the drug an internist reaches for.

The intestine also sits beside the pancreas and the bile duct. A cat can have intestinal inflammation, pancreatitis, and cholangitis together. If this cat has already had a pancreatic lipase test, bring it. Repeating pieces of the workup without the old record wastes time, and ignoring the liver while you chase the bowel misses jaundice that should have changed the week.

What has to be ruled out first

  • Parasites, including Giardia and Tritrichomonas in a young cat with large-bowel diarrhea, found on the right fecal tests.
  • Food-responsive disease, which can look exactly like IBD until a strict hydrolyzed or novel diet is fed long enough.
  • Hyperthyroidism in a middle-aged or older cat who vomits and loses weight while eating well.
  • Kidney disease, liver disease, and diabetes, which change thirst, weight, and stool in their own patterns.
  • A foreign body, a mass, or constipation, which radiographs or ultrasound may catch before anyone talks about immune drugs.

Bloodwork for this workup usually includes a chemistry panel, a complete blood count, a thyroid level when age warrants it, and a cobalamin (B12) level. Low B12 is common when the small intestine is diseased, and it makes the cat feel worse until it is supplemented the way the veterinarian prescribes. Folate is sometimes paired with it. Fecal tests come before steroids. An antibiotic trial used to be fashionable. In cats it is a weaker idea than a diet trial and a parasite check, and an internist can explain when a specific antibiotic still has a job, such as a documented infection. Do not start prednisolone from an old bottle because a previous cat "had IBD." Steroids change biopsies, can worsen an infection, and complicate diabetes.

Imaging and the biopsy decision

Ultrasound often shows thickened intestine, enlarged lymph nodes, or changes in the liver and pancreas. Thickening alone cannot be the diagnosis you take home. Food-responsive disease, inflammation, and small-cell lymphoma all thicken bowel. The veterinarian uses the image to decide where to sample and whether a surgical look is safer than endoscopy. Endoscopic biopsies are taken through a scope and reach the inner layers of the stomach and intestine. Surgical biopsies are full thickness and can sample jejunum that a scope may not reach, at the cost of a surgery and a recovery. Either way, the pathologist should be asked about special stains and immunohistochemistry, and about a clonality assay when the lymphocytes look suspicious. Small-cell lymphoma and severe inflammation are famous for resembling each other on a routine slide. Large-cell lymphoma looks more aggressive and is a different conversation, often with an oncologist, about chemotherapy and about how the cat feels this week.

Some cats never go to biopsy. If parasites are clear, a strict diet trial has produced a normal cat, and weight is returning, the veterinarian may stop at food-responsive enteropathy and keep the diet. Biopsy becomes the right spend when weight is still falling, when the trial fails, or when lymphoma has to be excluded before a long course of immune-suppressing drugs. That sequencing is the internist's craft. A cat who is crashing, anemic, or obstructed may need surgery for the crisis itself, with biopsies taken while the surgeon is already there.

Weight loss and a closed mouth

A vomiting cat who stops eating, especially one who is overweight or who is turning yellow, should be seen promptly. Hepatic lipidosis can stack on top of intestinal disease within days. Black stool, frank blood, collapse, or a painful abdomen are emergency signs. Do not fast the cat for the weekend, and do not give a dog's anti-nausea tablet or a human pain reliever. Bring a list of every food and supplement, because the diet history is part of the diagnosis.

Food, B12, and medicines the veterinarian times

A diet trial for suspected intestinal disease uses a hydrolyzed or novel-protein food, with no treats and no flavored extras, for the weeks the veterinarian sets. Some cats respond to a high-fiber approach or to a different veterinary diet when large-bowel signs dominate. There is no universal best bag. The cat who eats the food and gains weight has given you a result. The cat who refuses it needs a palatable alternative the veterinarian accepts, because anorexia is more dangerous than an imperfect protein. B12 injections or oral B12, if the level was low, follow the clinic's schedule. Recheck the number. Steroids such as prednisolone are used when inflammation is the working diagnosis and infection has been addressed. The dose, the taper, and the bloodwork are individualized. If the pathologist reports small-cell lymphoma, the internist or oncologist may add or substitute a chemotherapy drug given by mouth. That decision stays with the clinician who has the report. Asking a pharmacy for the drug by name, without that report, skips the diagnosis. Large-cell lymphoma is treated under an oncology plan, and some families choose comfort care when the cat is already frail. Those are honest forks. Ask what the next month looks like on each fork, including recheck costs and how you will know the plan is failing.

At home, keep a calendar of vomit, stool, and grams eaten. Weigh the cat every week on the same scale. A slow slide of weight with a normal appetite still matters. Hairballs more than once or twice a month in a shorthaired cat deserve a mention at the recheck. They are sometimes what intestinal disease looks like before anyone calls it diarrhea. Raise water intake if the veterinarian is also worried about constipation or kidney values. A fountain helps some cats. It does not treat lymphoma.

Costs along the path

Fecal tests, bloodwork, and B12 are the entry cost. Ultrasound sits in the middle. Endoscopy or surgical biopsy with a thorough pathology read, including special testing to separate inflammation from small-cell lymphoma, is the large and useful bill. Spreading that money across months of diet changes without a trial that was actually strict is the expensive way to stay confused. Ask for a staged estimate: diet and labs first, imaging next, biopsy if the cat is still losing weight. Medication costs continue after the diagnosis and should be quoted for a month. A single bottle hides the real monthly cost.

Questions about chronic intestinal disease

Is every vomiting cat a case of IBD?

No. Hairballs, hyperthyroidism, parasites, kidney disease, pancreatitis, and lymphoma all make cats vomit. IBD, or chronic enteropathy, is what remains after those are considered and, when needed, after biopsies. A strict diet trial sometimes resolves the signs and spares the cat a surgery. Start with an exam, a fecal, and the bloodwork your veterinarian pairs with the age of the cat. Steroids before that workup make the later biopsy harder to read.

Why do small-cell lymphoma and IBD look alike?

Both can cause vomiting, weight loss, and a thickened intestine on ultrasound. Under the microscope the lymphocytes can look similar until immunohistochemistry and a clonality test are added. That extra pathology changes the drug plan. Ask whether the biopsy quote includes those tests or whether they are added only if the first slide is uncertain. Large-cell lymphoma tends to move faster and is discussed as an oncology case.

What should I feed while we wait?

Feed the diet your veterinarian named for the trial, and feed enough that the cat does not skip days. Anorexia can trigger hepatic lipidosis on top of the bowel problem. If the prescribed food is refused, call and ask for an acceptable substitute. Treats, flavored pastes, and the other cat's kibble undo the trial. Weigh the cat weekly and bring the numbers, along with a note of vomiting, so the next visit is about a trend.
