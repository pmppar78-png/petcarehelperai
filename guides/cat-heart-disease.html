Heart Disease in Cats (HCM)

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and other heart conditions in cats. Covers symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, at-risk breeds, and prognosis.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the disease you are usually facing

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, HCM, is the heart disease veterinarians find most often in cats. The muscular wall of the left ventricle thickens, the chamber holds less blood, and the heart can fail to relax between beats. Some cats also develop a clot inside the heart or a rhythm that becomes dangerous. Other cardiomyopathies exist, including dilated and restrictive forms, and an echocardiogram is how they are told apart. A murmur heard with a stethoscope is only a clue. Plenty of cats with serious HCM have a normal exam on a given day, and plenty of soft murmurs turn out to be innocent flow murmurs or disease elsewhere.

Maine Coons and Ragdolls are the breeds with well-described mutations in a cardiac myosin-binding gene, and a breeder can ask a veterinarian about the specific genetic tests for those breeds. A clear genetic test in a Maine Coon does not erase every other way a heart can thicken. Domestic shorthairs make up most of the cats who actually develop HCM, because they make up most of the cats. Middle-aged and older cats are typical, yet the disease shows up in young adults too. Hyperthyroidism and high blood pressure can thicken heart muscle for a different reason, so an older cat needs a thyroid level and a blood pressure reading alongside the heart conversation.

The signs that arrive late

Many cats hide HCM until one of three crises. The first is congestive heart failure. Fluid collects in the lungs or, very often in cats, in the chest space around the lungs. Breathing becomes fast or open-mouthed, the cat hunches, the elbows may lift, and the appetite disappears. Count resting breaths while the cat is asleep: a rate that stays high, roughly above thirty breaths a minute in a cat at rest, is a reason to call. The second crisis is an arterial clot, often a saddle thrombus at the end of the aorta. The cat screams, drags or cannot move the hind legs, and the paws feel cold compared with the front feet. Pulses in those limbs are weak or absent. People mistake this for a sprained back or a broken pelvis. It is a circulatory emergency, and the outlook is guarded even with prompt care. The third presentation is sudden death in a cat who seemed fine that morning.

  • Sleeping respiratory rate climbing and staying high, or any open-mouth breathing.
  • Sudden hind-end paralysis, pain, and cold hind paws.
  • A new gallop sound, an arrhythmia, or a murmur your veterinarian wants imaged.
  • Collapse, fainting, or a cat who hides and will not lie on the side that used to be comfortable.

Emergency breathing or a cold hind end

Open-mouth breathing in a cat is an emergency. So is a sudden inability to use the hind legs with pain and cold paws. Put the cat in a carrier with a towel, keep the car quiet, and go to an emergency hospital. Do not force the legs to move, and do not give a human aspirin, a dog pain tablet, or a leftover diuretic. Oxygen and a plan for fluid and clots have to be chosen after an exam. Stress itself can worsen failure, so skip the bath, the nail trim, and the long wait in a loud room if the clinic can take you straight back.

How a cardiologist confirms it

An echocardiogram is the test that measures wall thickness, chamber size, the left atrium, and whether a clot is already forming. A clinician who images cat hearts regularly will see details a quick abdominal scan was never meant to catch. An NT-proBNP blood test can screen. A high result pushes the cat toward an echo, and a low result makes hidden failure less likely at that moment, without replacing the ultrasound when signs are already present. Chest radiographs look for fluid in the lungs or the pleural space once failure is the question. They cannot measure wall thickness. An ECG is added when the rhythm is irregular. Bloodwork checks kidneys before any diuretic, and it looks for the thyroid and the blood pressure problems that mimic primary HCM.

Treatment stays with the veterinarian who saw the echo

There is no home protocol that shrinks a thick ventricle. If the cat is in congestive failure, the hospital uses oxygen, a diuretic the clinician doses, and quiet. Thoracentesis, a needle drainage of chest fluid, is sometimes what lets the cat breathe. After stabilization, the cardiologist may discuss a drug that lowers the chance of a clot, a drug that supports the heart's squeezing when the echo shows that problem, and a diuretic to continue at home. Asymptomatic cats with mild thickening are sometimes monitored without daily heart drugs, because starting the wrong pill can do harm. Beta blockers and other medicines you have read about for people stay off the plan until a cardiologist ties them to this cat's echo. The dose, the combination, and the follow-up bloodwork belong to the veterinarian who has the images.

At home you count sleeping breaths, keep a written resting rate, and give only the medicines on the label. A sudden jump in that rate, a return of open-mouth breathing, or a new paralysis is a return visit the same day. Fluid therapy for another illness, a long-acting steroid, and a stressful car trip can tip a compensated cat into failure. Tell every veterinarian who sees this cat, including the dentist, that the heart is thick, so fluids and drugs can be chosen with that in mind. Diet changes do not replace an echo. Salt talk is individual, and a cat who is already thin should not have food restricted in the name of heart health.

What heart care costs

The echocardiogram is the pivot expense, and it is cheaper than a week of oxygen and chest taps after a crisis. Clot drugs and diuretics are ongoing costs, with bloodwork to protect the kidneys. Hospitalization for failure or for a saddle thrombus is the large, unpredictable bill, and some families face a quality-of-life decision the same night. Ask the emergency team for a staged estimate: stabilize and reassess, then decide on the next day of care. Ranges shift by region and by whether a traveling cardiologist or a specialty hospital does the ultrasound.

Questions about feline heart disease

My cat has a murmur. Does that mean HCM?

A murmur is a reason to talk about an echocardiogram, and it falls short of proof that the cat has hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Some murmurs are mild flow sounds. Some cats with a thick ventricle have no murmur at all on the day of the exam. If your cat is a Maine Coon or a Ragdoll, ask about breed-specific genetic tests as well as the ultrasound. If your cat is older, ask for a thyroid check and a blood pressure reading before anyone calls the thickening primary HCM.

What does a saddle thrombus look like?

The usual picture is sudden pain, crying, and hind legs that will not support the cat, with paws that feel cold. It is a clot from the heart, lodged where the aorta splits. Carry the cat in a carrier to an emergency hospital. Do not massage the legs and do not give a pain tablet from your own medicine cabinet. Even with fast care, some cats do not regain function, and the veterinarian will talk with you about suffering as well as about treatment.

Can a cat with HCM live a normal life?

Some cats with mild thickening and a normal-sized atrium live for years with monitoring and no daily heart drug. A cat who has already been in congestive failure or has thrown a clot has a more serious disease, and the cardiologist's recheck plan is what tells you how that individual is doing. Home breath counts catch fluid early. They do not replace the next echocardiogram.
