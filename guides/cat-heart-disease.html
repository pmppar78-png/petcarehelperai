Heart Disease in Cats (HCM)

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and other heart conditions in cats. Covers symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, at-risk breeds, and prognosis.

Causes and Risk Factors

Multiple factors can contribute to the development of this condition.

  • Genetic predisposition: Certain breeds are at higher risk due to inherited traits
  • Age: Some conditions are more common in kittens/kittens or senior pets
  • Environmental factors: Diet, toxin exposure, stress, and living conditions play a role
  • Underlying health issues: Other conditions may increase susceptibility
  • Immune system status: Compromised immune function can predispose to disease

Symptoms to Watch For

Early detection significantly improves treatment outcomes. Watch for these signs.

  • Changes in appetite, water consumption, or weight
  • Behavioral changes including lethargy, restlessness, or hiding
  • Physical symptoms specific to the affected body system
  • Changes in bathroom habits (frequency, appearance, straining)
  • Visible discomfort, pain response to touch, or changes in posture
  • Respiratory changes, coughing, or labored breathing

When to See the Vet Immediately

Diagnosis

Cardiomyopathy in cats is diagnosed with an echocardiogram. A murmur or a gallop on the exam is a reason to do that study, and a normal exam does not rule the disease out. An NT-proBNP blood test can screen. Radiographs look for fluid in the lungs or the chest once heart failure is suspected. The ACVIM approach, and the Merck Veterinary Manual, put the ultrasound ahead of a guess from the radiograph. An ECG is added when the rhythm is the question.

Treatment Options

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is managed with drugs, not with a single surgery. A cat in congestive failure needs oxygen, a diuretic, and other cardiac drugs the veterinarian or the cardiologist chooses and doses. A saddle thrombus is an emergency and a different plan from a stable cat who only has a murmur. There is no operation on this page that removes HCM.

Prevention and Management

  • Regular veterinary checkups (as recommended by the AVMA for all companion animals) for early detection (annually, or semi-annually for seniors)
  • Maintain a healthy weight through proper nutrition and exercise
  • Keep vaccinations and preventive medications current
  • Breed-specific health screenings recommended by your veterinarian
  • Provide a low-stress, enriched environment
  • Follow your vet's recommendations for diet and lifestyle modifications

Common Questions

When the household is genuinely fluent in this dimension of Cat Heart Disease care, the reactive decisions tend to drop away. Generic recommendations are a reasonable starting point, but the cat you live with ultimately sets the standard.

Is this condition curable?

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the heart disease this page is about for most cats, is not cured by medication. The thickened muscle stays thick. ACVIM cardiomyopathy guidance uses drugs to reduce clot risk and to treat congestive heart failure when it appears. A few cats have a reversible look-alike, such as hyperthyroidism or hypertension, and those causes can improve when the underlying disease is treated. An echocardiogram is what tells the structural disease from the look-alike.

How can I afford treatment?

The entry cost for feline heart disease is the echocardiogram, not the first month of pills. Ask the cardiologist for the echo fee, and for a separate estimate if chest radiographs and hospitalization for fluid in the lungs become necessary. Clot-prevention and diuretic medicines are the smaller repeating cost after the diagnosis. Screening echoes for a Maine Coon or Ragdoll breeding cat are a planned fee. An emergency visit for a saddle thrombus is not.
