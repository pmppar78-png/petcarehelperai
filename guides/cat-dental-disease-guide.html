Dental Disease in Cats

Feline dental disease including stomatitis, tooth resorption, and periodontal disease. Covers symptoms, dental procedures, and home dental care.

Causes and Risk Factors

Multiple factors can contribute to the development of this condition.

  • Genetic predisposition: Certain breeds are at higher risk due to inherited traits
  • Age: Some conditions are more common in kittens or senior pets
  • Environmental factors: Diet, toxin exposure, stress, and living conditions play a role
  • Underlying health issues: Other conditions may increase susceptibility
  • Immune system status: Compromised immune function can predispose to disease

Symptoms to Watch For

Early detection significantly improves treatment outcomes. Watch for these signs.

  • Changes in appetite, water consumption, or weight
  • Behavioral changes including lethargy, restlessness, or hiding
  • Physical symptoms specific to the affected body system
  • Changes in bathroom habits (frequency, appearance, straining)
  • Visible discomfort, pain response to touch, or changes in posture
  • Respiratory changes, coughing, or labored breathing

When to See the Vet Immediately

Adapt the framework below to the specific animal — weight targets, activity rhythm, and active treatments all inform the personalised values.

Diagnosis

Feline dental disease, including tooth resorption, is diagnosed under anesthesia with a probe and dental radiographs. An awake look can see a red gum or a missing crown and cannot see a resorbing root. Tooth resorption is a radiographic diagnosis. Oral inflammation that might be stomatitis is charted at the same time, because the treatment differs.

Treatment Options

Resorptive teeth that are painful are extracted. The dentist chooses which roots can be left when the radiograph shows they have already been replaced by bone. The veterinarian chooses the drug and the dose. A cleaning without radiographs misses the teeth that need to come out. Stomatitis, when that is the disease, may need extraction of many teeth and is a different plan from a single resorptive lesion.

Prevention and Management

  • Regular veterinary checkups (as recommended by the AVMA for all companion animals) for early detection (annually, or semi-annually for seniors)
  • Maintain a healthy weight through proper nutrition and exercise
  • Keep vaccinations and preventive medications current
  • Breed-specific health screenings recommended by your veterinarian
  • Provide a low-stress, enriched environment
  • Follow your vet's recommendations for diet and lifestyle modifications

Is this condition curable?

Cat periodontal disease behaves like the dog form: gum inflammation can improve, bone loss does not refill. Tooth resorption, the neck lesions common in cats, is not reversed by brushing or by a filling. AVDC guidance is extraction of teeth with painful resorption. Radiographs under anesthesia find roots that look normal on an awake peek. Removing a resorbing tooth treats the pain. It does not stop the next tooth from resorbing later.

How can I afford treatment?

Ask the dentist how many extractions the radiograph fee might add, because cat dentals become expensive when resorption is in every quadrant. The awake estimate undercounts. A cleaning with no extractions and a full-mouth extraction series should be two written quotes. Insurance generally excludes dental disease recorded before the policy. Painful resorptive teeth are not optional cosmetic work.
