Dental Disease in Cats

Feline dental disease including stomatitis, tooth resorption, and periodontal disease. Covers symptoms, dental procedures, and home dental care.

Three mouth diseases that get lumped together

Feline mouths fail in a few repeating ways, and the treatment depends on which one the radiographs show. Periodontal disease is infection and bone loss around the tooth, the same family of problems tartar and red gums suggest on an awake look. Tooth resorption is a cat-specific destruction of the tooth itself. The crown can look almost normal while the root is dissolving, or a cherry-red spot at the gumline can mark a hole that hurts when the tongue touches it. Feline chronic gingivostomatitis is a widespread, angry inflammation, often worst at the back of the mouth where the upper and lower jaws meet, and it is a different disease from a dirty canine tooth. Oral cancer, especially squamous cell carcinoma, also hides in the jaw and under the tongue. A cat who drools and paws at the face needs a diagnosis before anyone reaches for a stronger dental chew.

Resorptive lesions are extremely common in adult cats. Surveys of mature cats find them in a large share of mouths once dental radiographs are taken. The cause is still being studied. What is settled is the pain. Cats chatter the jaw, drop kibble, chew on one side, prefer soft food they used to refuse, and let the coat go because the mouth hurts. Many of them still purr and ask for breakfast. Appetite is a poor measure of a comfortable mouth in this species.

Why an awake peek is only a start

A veterinarian can see tartar, a missing crown, a red gum, and sometimes a fractured tooth while the cat is awake. That look cannot see the root, the bone under the gum, or a resorbing tooth that still has a smooth crown. Tooth resorption is a radiographic diagnosis. The American Veterinary Dental College describes patterns that change the surgery: some roots are still distinct and need to be fully elevated and removed, and some roots are already being replaced by bone, in which case a carefully planned crown amputation can be the right procedure once the radiograph proves it. Guessing that distinction from the surface of the tooth leaves painful root fragments behind or removes bone that did not need to go. Stomatitis is charted at the same anesthetic, because a cat with caudal mucositis may need extraction of many or all of the cheek teeth, a larger surgery than a single resorptive lesion.

Anesthesia-free scraping, the service that pops tartar off a awake cat, does not take those radiographs and does not extract a resorbing tooth. It can make the crown look cleaner while the painful root remains. Cats with heart disease, kidney disease, or hyperthyroidism still need a mouth assessment. The dentist and the primary veterinarian can stage bloodwork, fluids, and monitoring so the anesthetic risk is discussed honestly. Leaving a mouth full of resorptive lesions because anesthesia feels frightening is also a risk, measured in pain every day.

What you notice before the dental

  • Drooling, sometimes with a trace of blood on a toy or a water bowl.
  • Jaw chattering when the mouth is touched, or a head that flinches away from a chin scratch.
  • Kibble dropped, a preference for one side, or a sudden insistence on pate.
  • Bad breath that is new, or a face that looks slightly swollen along one jaw.
  • Weight loss and a messy coat in a cat who used to be fastidious.

Mouth pain that should not wait

A cat who will not eat for a day, a suddenly swollen face, bleeding that will not stop, or trouble breathing around a jaw mass should be seen promptly. Do not give ibuprofen, acetaminophen, or a dog's pain medicine. Those drugs injure cats. Do not try to pull a loose tooth. A root that breaks off becomes a harder surgery, and the jaw may already be thin from disease.

After the radiographs, the treatment is surgical more often than medicinal

Periodontal teeth that still have a sound root may be cleaned above and below the gum, polished, and kept, if the bone loss is modest. Teeth with advanced bone loss, fractures that enter the pulp, or resorption that matches the extraction pattern come out. The veterinarian sends you home with pain control chosen for cats and, when infection justifies it, an antibiotic chosen for that mouth. Stomatitis sometimes calms after the cheek teeth or the full dentition are removed, and some cats still need medical management afterward. That decision is made from the inflammation left at the recheck. A handout cannot promise how the mouth will look. Biopsy a mass, an unusual ulcer, or a swollen jaw that does not match resorption. Squamous cell carcinoma in the mouth is aggressive, and the earlier it is named, the more honest the options are.

Home care keeps the next periodontal visit smaller. A cat who tolerates a toothbrush can use a veterinary toothpaste made for cats, a tiny amount, aimed at the gumline, a few times a week. Human toothpaste can contain fluoride and other ingredients cats should not swallow. A cat who will not be brushed should not be wrestled. Dental diets and dental treats approved by the Veterinary Oral Health Council can reduce tartar in some cats. They do not stop tooth resorption, and they do not treat stomatitis. Water additives and random powders are optional conversations with your veterinarian. Radiographs still have to happen. Recheck the mouth on the schedule the clinic sets, because a new resorptive lesion can appear beside a tooth that was healthy last year.

Planning the dental bill

A complete oral health assessment with anesthesia, cleaning, and full-mouth radiographs is the baseline quote to request. Extractions are usually priced per tooth or per unit of time once the radiographs exist, so the final bill can rise after the cat is asleep and the roots are visible. Ask the clinic how they will contact you if the plan expands, and what pain control and a recheck cost. A stomatitis surgery with many extractions sits well above a single-tooth procedure. Skipping radiographs to save money is how resorptive roots get left behind and a second anesthetic becomes necessary.

Questions about cat dental disease

What is a feline resorptive lesion?

It is a painful process that eats into the tooth, often starting at the gumline or inside the root. The crown may look intact, or you may see a red spot where enamel is gone. Dental radiographs under anesthesia show whether the root must be fully extracted or whether it has already been replaced by bone so a crown amputation is appropriate. An awake look and a tartar scraping cannot make that call.

Is stomatitis just severe gingivitis?

Stomatitis, especially inflammation at the caudal mouth, is a broader and angrier disease than tartar gingivitis. Many of these cats need multiple extractions, and some need medicine after the surgery as well. The veterinarian charts the inflammation at the dental and rechecks it when the mouth has healed. Brushing a stomatitis mouth before that plan is in place usually causes pain and does not settle the disease.

Can I skip anesthesia and still treat the teeth?

Cleaning a crown while the cat is awake leaves resorptive roots, bone loss, and fractured teeth untreated. Radiographs and extractions require anesthesia, monitoring, and pain control selected for cats. If your cat has a heart murmur, kidney disease, or hyperthyroidism, ask for bloodwork and a plan that names those risks. Living with an untreated resorptive mouth is its own daily harm.
