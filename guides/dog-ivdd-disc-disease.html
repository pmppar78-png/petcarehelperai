IVDD Intervertebral Disc

Evidence-based guide to IVDD in dogs. Covers symptoms, at-risk breeds (Dachshunds, Corgis), treatment options from conservative to surgery, and recovery.

What intervertebral disc disease is

Intervertebral disc disease, usually shortened to IVDD, is a failure of the cushion between vertebrae. The disc's soft center pushes or bursts against the spinal cord and the nerves, and the dog's signs come from pain and from cord compression. Hansen type I disease is an extrusion: the center degenerates early, mineralizes, and then shoots out through a torn outer ring, often all at once. It is the form seen in chondrodystrophic dogs, whose skeletons are built on shortened limbs and long backs. Hansen type II disease is a slower protrusion of a bulging disc, more often in older large breeds. Either form can strike the neck or the middle of the back. The thoracolumbar junction, roughly from the last thoracic vertebrae into the upper lumbar spine, is the most common site for a sudden paraparesis.

Plain pain and true spinal-cord injury are different grades of the same family of problems. A dog who only cries when lifted can still walk. A dog who has lost feeling in the toes may already have a severe cord contusion. The grade, judged on a neurologic exam, is what decides whether crate rest is reasonable or whether a surgeon needs to decompress the cord today.

Which dogs are at risk

Dachshunds carry the highest breed risk for type I extrusions, and the risk climbs through middle age. French Bulldogs, Beagles, Corgis, Shih Tzus, Pekingese, Basset Hounds, and Cocker Spaniels share the chondrodystrophic pattern and fill neurology services for the same reason. Type II protrusions show up more in German Shepherd Dogs, Labrador Retrievers, and other large older dogs. Obesity and weekend jumping add load to a disc that is already degenerate. A dog can herniate a disc the first time it hops off a sofa, and a dog can also herniate one while sleeping, because the disc was ready to fail.

Once a dog has had one type I extrusion, another disc can fail later. The repaired or rested space stays a weak point, and neighboring discs may already be mineralized. That is why neurologists talk about lifestyle after the first episode even when the dog is walking again.

Signs owners notice

Neck pain looks like a dog who holds the head low, shakes when the collar is reached for, or cries turning to look upward. Back pain looks like a hunched posture, a tense belly, a yelp when picked up, and a refusal to climb stairs. As the cord is compressed the back legs get wobbly, then weak, then unable to stand. Some dogs drag the toes or cross the hind feet. Loss of bladder control, and a tail that does not wag, are later signs. The emergency line is loss of deep pain: the dog does not even flinch when a toe is pinched hard. Owners often notice the walking problem before they notice the bladder, so check whether urine is still being passed.

  • Crying when lifted, or trembling with a roached back.
  • Unwillingness to jump, climb, or lower the head to the bowl.
  • Wobbly or crossing hind legs, scuffed toenails, or a knucked paw.
  • A dog who can still pull forward with the front legs while the back legs do nothing.
  • Dribbling urine, a full hard bladder, or no urine passed for many hours.

How a veterinarian

Intervertebral disc disease is localized with a neurologic exam. The veterinarian grades pain, limb weakness, and whether the dog still feels a deep pinch on the toes. Plain radiographs do not reliably show the disc that is pressing on the cord. MRI, or a CT myelogram where MRI cannot be obtained, is the test that finds the compressive disc before surgery. That imaging comes after the neurologic exam.

The exam sorts a back problem from a cruciate tear, a hip injury, or a belly pain that makes a dog stand hunched. Reflexes, limb tone, and the cutoff where sensation changes tell the clinician which segment of cord is involved. Radiographs can show narrowed disc spaces or mineralized discs sitting in place, and those findings are common in Dachshunds who feel fine between episodes. They do not name the disc that is compressing the cord today. Advanced imaging is the step before decompression surgery. A dog who is only painful and still walking may skip imaging on the first day if the plan is strict rest, then go to MRI if the legs get worse or the pain will not settle.

Treatment categories

A dog who is only painful, or mildly weak and still improving, is often treated with strict crate rest and pain control the veterinarian doses. A dog who cannot walk, or who has lost deep pain, is a candidate for decompression, and the loss of deep pain is an emergency. Any steroid is the veterinarian's decision. Many dogs never need surgery. The ones who do need a surgeon.

Medical care means a crate, a leash only for a few steps outside to toilet, and drugs for pain and sometimes for muscle spasm. The usual rest period for a walking dog is several weeks, and the neurologist shortens or extends it from the recheck. Anti-inflammatory drugs, gabapentin-type pain drugs, and opioids are categories the clinician mixes. Whether a steroid is used at all belongs to the veterinarian, because it can change infection risk and it can mask a dog who is getting worse. Surgery for a thoracolumbar extrusion is most often a hemilaminectomy: a window is cut in the vertebra so the disc material can be lifted off the cord. Cervical disc disease may need a ventral slot. These operations do not install a new disc. They remove the pressure. Physical rehabilitation starts when the surgeon says the incision and the bone can tolerate it, and it is part of recovery for dogs who were paralyzed.

Home care

Crate rest fails when the dog is "just allowed on the couch." The crate or a small pen is the treatment. Carry a small dog outside, or walk a larger dog on a sling or a short leash for elimination only, then go straight back. Use a harness so a neck lead does not yank a cervical disc. Keep floors from becoming a slip hazard with runners if the dog is wobbly and has been cleared for those few steps. After the veterinarian releases the dog from rest, add stairs and jumping back last, and consider ramps for the bed and the car for good. Weight control lowers the load on the next disc. Expressing the bladder, if the dog cannot urinate, is a skill the clinic should teach you the same day, because a full bladder becomes an emergency of its own.

When it is

When to See

Loss of the ability to walk, sudden paralysis, a dog who does not flinch when you pinch a toe, or a dog who cannot urinate should be seen by an emergency clinic with neurology access if you can reach one. Hours matter once deep pain is gone, and waiting through a weekend on crate rest alone can close the window in which decompression still helps. A crying dog who is still walking should be seen the same day, and sooner if the legs are getting weaker by the hour. Do not give a human pain tablet while you wait. Several of them are poisonous to dogs, and they can hide a change in the neurologic grade.

What to ask

Surgery and rest leave the damaged disc in a weakened state. Mild Hansen type I extrusions, typical of dachshunds and other chondrodystrophic breeds, can regain walking with strict crate rest, yet that disc stays weak and another disc can fail later. Loss of deep pain is an emergency decompression, and some of those dogs never walk. Neurologists quote a prognosis from the grade of the injury.

Get two IVDD numbers the same day. Crate rest with pain medicine is a primary-care plan. MRI or CT followed by hemilaminectomy is a neurology hospitalization, and it cannot wait if the dog has lost feeling in the toes. The estimate should list imaging separately from the decompression. A policy helps only when it was active before the first episode of back pain.

Key Questions

Can a Dachshund with IVDD recover without surgery?

Many dogs who are only painful, or who are weak and still walking, regain comfort and coordination with strict crate rest and pain control. The disc that extruded is still a damaged disc. Dogs who cannot walk, and especially dogs who have lost deep pain sensation, are the group in whom decompression is discussed the same day. The neurologic grade on exam, repeated if the dog declines, is what makes that split. A phone description of a dog who "seems a bit off" cannot tell anyone whether rest alone will be safe.

Why do radiographs miss the compressive disc?

Radiographs can show a narrowed space or a mineralized disc sitting where it belongs. They miss the piece of disc that has already entered the spinal canal. MRI, or a CT myelogram when MRI is unavailable, shows that compressive material and lets the surgeon plan the side and the length of the hemilaminectomy. A dog headed for surgery needs that picture. A dog headed for rest may not, unless the signs worsen.

What does crate rest actually include?

It includes a crate or pen small enough that the dog cannot trot, no couch time, no stairs, and no "just a quick play" with another pet. Potty breaks are short and supported. The length is usually several weeks for a walking patient, set and then adjusted by the veterinarian at rechecks. Pain medicine does not make jumping safe. If the dog is getting weaker during rest, the plan changes to imaging the same day.
