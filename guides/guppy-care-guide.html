Care and Breeding

Complete guppy care guide covering tank setup, feeding, breeding, fry care, color varieties, and common guppy diseases.

Housing and Enclosure

Guppies are small livebearers that reward a planted tank and punish a crowded one. Start with at least 10 gallons for a modest group, with a heater and a filter that will not shred newborn fry. Water in the mid 70s, anywhere from 72 to 82 F, suits them. They are tropical, so a thermostat heater belongs on the tank whenever the room can fall below that band. A thermometer tells you whether the heater is actually doing the job or has stuck on.

Gentle flow matters. A sponge filter or a hang-on-back with a baffle keeps bacteria colonies fed and leaves a quiet corner where fry can hover. Live plants, Java moss, and floating stems do more for guppies than any ornament. Females duck into the leaves when a male will not stop displaying, and the babies spend their first weeks in that same cover. A bare tank with a treasure chest turns into a chase scene.

Keep two or three females for every male. Males court constantly, and a single female in a tank of males loses her fins and her condition. A group of only females is peaceful if you can find fish that are truly female. Pet-store "females" are sometimes late-developing males, so watch the anal fin. A male's gonopodium is a narrow rod. A female's anal fin is a fan. Once you have mixed sexes, assume the tank will fill with fry. Plan the volume for the adults you intend to keep, and decide in advance which babies you have homes for.

Hard, alkaline water is fine for the common fancy guppy. Endler's livebearers and wild-type strains sometimes prefer slightly different mineral levels, so read the source if you are keeping a specific line. Stability beats a perfect number. Change water on a schedule and match temperature so a fish that just gave birth stays steady.

Diet and Nutrition

A quality flake or a micro pellet is the everyday meal. Guppies have small mouths and will spit a pellet meant for a cichlid. Feed a pinch two or three times a day, less than they could cram in. Fry and newly dropped females need the frequent meals. Adult males in a rich planted tank can get fat on two small feeds and the biofilm they pick at.

Frozen or freeze-dried brine shrimp, daphnia, and bloodworms a few times a week bring color up and support females that are constantly producing young. Blanch a slice of zucchini or spinach and clip it where you can remove it before it disintegrates. Live baby brine shrimp are excellent for growing out fry if you hatch them in a bottle at home. Store-bought live foods from unknown water are a disease risk you do not need in a fish this prone to outbreaks.

Breeding females burn through food. A female that looks hollow behind the head, with a squared gravid spot, is either about to drop young or has been chased off the food. Feed in two spots so subordinate fish get a share. Overfeeding the whole tank to save one thin female just grows algae and ammonia.

Health and Veterinary

Ich, fin rot, velvet, and internal parasites are the guppy problems you will actually see. Shop fish often arrive already carrying something, which is why a separate quarantine tank for two to four weeks is worth the extra sponge filter. Clamped fins, flashing, or a male whose tail shrinks day by day should stay out of the display. Fin rot in guppies frequently follows constant male harassment plus a missed water change. Giving the female cover and cleaning the water stops many cases before a medicine is justified.

Ammonia and nitrite at anything above zero are the emergency the Merck Veterinary Manual puts at the top of aquarium-fish medicine. The usual story is an uncycled aquarium, too much food, a dead guppy hidden in the moss, or a filter that stopped. Both readings belong at 0 ppm. Guppies cannot hold a body temperature of their own, so metabolism tracks the water. Use a thermometer, and keep the thermostat heater on this tropical species. Parasites such as ich typically come in with new fish or plants, which is another reason quarantine exists. Bacterial infections, fin rot included, usually follow bad water or a nip. Copper-based treatments harm shrimp, snails, and plants and can knock the filter bacteria down, so plan on testing ammonia and nitrite for weeks if copper ever goes into a tank.

The writeup to read alongside a sick tank is the Merck Veterinary Manual chapter about aquarium fish.

A fish that wastes away while still eating, or that develops a bent spine, may be dealing with internal parasites or a genetic issue common in heavily line-bred strains. A veterinarian who treats fish can help you decide whether the problem is the group or the water. Do not dump a general cure into a tank full of fry and shrimp because a forum list said to. Identify the signs, fix the water, and isolate the sick animal.

Stocking and Fry

  • Add guppies only after ammonia and nitrite have tested at zero for several days.
  • Float the bag, then drip or cup tank water in before you release them.
  • Bring home a few fish at a time so the filter can grow with the bioload.
  • Count males and females at the store, under a light, before you pay.
  • Keep a net handy and use it briefly. A long chase through Java moss does more harm than a quick scoop.

Compatible company includes other peaceful livebearers of similar size, small rasboras, and snails, as long as the temperature overlaps and nobody nips fins. Cichlids and hungry barbs eat guppies. If fry are the goal, a heavily planted species tank outperforms a crowded community. If fry are a problem, a group of only males in a planted tank avoids the population boom, at the cost of more displaying. Either plan is honest. Counting on the adults to ignore the babies fails every time.

Day to day, guppies should be spread through the plants, males flickering at each other without locking onto one torn female, and everyone coming to the glass for a pinch of food. A tank where every fish hangs at the surface with clamped tails needs a test kit the same day.

Males, Females, and a Plan for Fry

Before you leave the shop, look at the anal fin. A male guppy carries a narrow gonopodium. A female carries a fan. The tank at home needs at least two or three females for each male, a heater holding roughly 72 to 82 F, and plants where a harassed female can vanish. A group of only males avoids a population boom and increases displaying. Either plan is honest if you chose it on purpose. Quarantine new fish for two to four weeks because store guppies often arrive with ich, fin rot, or worse.

Assume mixed sexes will produce fry. Java moss and floating plants save some of them. If you do not want dozens of guppies, house only one sex or have homes lined up before the first drop. Feed a small flake or micro pellet two or three times a day, plus frozen brine shrimp or daphnia a few times a week, and remove what they do not finish. Ammonia and nitrite stay at zero. A gentle sponge or a baffled filter protects newborns and still houses the bacteria. A guppy clamped in a corner with a shrinking tail needs clean water and a separate tank the same day, and a fish veterinarian if the school starts to fade together.

Quick Answers

Are guppies good for beginner fish keepers?

Yes. They need at least 10 gallons, gentle filtration, and water at 72 to 82°F. Keep 2 or 3 females per male, and quarantine new fish for 2 to 4 weeks because store guppies often carry disease.

Why do guppies need more females than males?

Males chase females constantly. Two or three females per male spreads that attention out and lowers stress on the females.
