Best Aquarium Filters

Top aquarium filters for freshwater and saltwater tanks. Covers HOB, canister, sponge, and internal filters with tank size recommendations.

What the filter is actually culturing

Fish release ammonia. Beneficial bacteria living on filter media and on other surfaces convert ammonia to nitrite and then to nitrate. That sequence is the biological filter, and it is the reason a brand-new aquarium cannot be filled with fish on day one just because a motor is humming. The motor moves water. The bacteria, once established, do the chemistry. Nitrate leaves the tank when you change water. A filter that only strains mulm, with no porous media for bacteria and no water changes, will polish the view until the animals are poisoned in clear water.

Most aquarists divide media into three jobs. Mechanical media, such as a sponge or floss, catches solids. Biological media, such as ceramic rings, bio-balls, or that same sponge once it is colonized, houses bacteria. Chemical media, such as carbon or a specialized resin, grabs dissolved compounds for a while and then expires. Carbon is optional in a healthy freshwater tank. It is useful after a medication, or to clear a tint, and it is a waste of money if you replace the whole cartridge every two weeks and throw the bacteria away with the carbon. Rinse mechanical sponges in a bucket of tank water you just removed. A blast of tap water, especially where utilities use chloramine, knocks back the colony you spent a month growing.

Hang-on-back filters

A hang-on-back filter sits on the rim, pulls water up a tube, and returns it as a waterfall. The splash is a feature. It drives gas exchange at the surface, which is what keeps oxygen available on a warm day. Cartridges are easy to find, and the units are a fair match for many tanks from about 10 to 50 gallons. The weakness is media volume. A single thin cartridge clogs, the flow collapses, and the instructions often tell you to replace it on a calendar. Keep a sponge in the chamber, rinse that sponge in old tank water, and change carbon on its own schedule if you use it at all. Seed a new sponge beside the old one for a few weeks before you retire anything that is still brown and alive.

Flow from a hang-on-back can shove a betta, a fancy guppy, or a hillstream loach that wanted the opposite kind of current. If fish hide in a corner and clamp their fins, baffle the waterfall with a sponge on the outlet or choose a gentler model. Shrimp tanks lose babies into some intakes. A prefilter sponge over the intake tube fixes that and also keeps plant bits out of the impeller. Clean the impeller monthly. A noisy rattle is often a bit of gravel in the well, and it becomes a burned-out motor if you ignore it until the smell of hot plastic arrives.

Canisters

A canister sits under the stand, holds a stack of sponges and biological media, and returns water through a spray bar or a lily pipe. The media volume is the reason people buy them for tanks past the small-beginner size, for messy goldfish, and for planted tanks where a steady bacterial bed matters. Look for shutoff valves on the hoses so you can pull the canister for a rinse without draining the tank onto the rug. Priming should be something you can do at the tap on the lid or with a simple plunger, because a canister full of air does nothing useful and can run dry.

Leaks happen at hose clamps, at O-rings with a grain of sand on them, and at cracked lids people overtightened. Wipe the O-ring, seat it flat, and leave a drip loop and a water alarm if the canister lives in a room you care about. Maintenance is a rinse of the mechanical sponge when flow drops, usually every few weeks to a couple of months depending on the bioload, while the biological layers stay wet and mostly undisturbed. Replacing every basket on the same afternoon is how a cycled tank cycles again. Canisters are quieter than a waterfall when they are full of water and annoying when they are sucking air. A soft gurgle means you should burp the line.

Sponges, internals, and sumps

A sponge filter runs on an air pump. Water passes through the foam, bacteria colonize the foam, and the current stays gentle. That combination suits betta tanks, shrimp, quarantine, and grow-out tanks for fry who would be sucked into a power filter. Pair it with a pump you can dial down and with airline check valves so a power cut does not siphon the tank onto the floor. Sponge filters do less mechanical polishing. The water can look slightly soft around the edges, and that is acceptable if ammonia and nitrite test at zero. Squeeze the sponge in removed tank water when it clogs. Replace the sponge only after a new one has been seeded beside it.

Internal power filters hide in the corner of a small tank and take swimming space in exchange for a short parts list. They work for a desktop tank and a hospital tank you want to store dry between uses. Sumps are a second tank under a display, common on marine systems and on some large freshwater builds. Water drains down, runs through mechanical socks, a protein skimmer on a reef, live rock or other biological media, a heater, and a return pump. The sump adds volume, hides equipment, and can overflow the room if the return fails and the display's drain siphon has no backup plan. Build a sump from a guide for that style of system, with a flood sensor, before you chase a higher flow rating.

Flow, stocking, and saltwater differences

Box labels quote gallons per hour measured with empty media and zero lift. Real flow is lower once sponges fill and the return has to climb back to the rim. A rough shopping habit is to start with a filter whose advertised rate is several times the tank volume per hour, often in a band people describe as about four to ten times, then watch the fish. River species such as many danios and hillstream fish use current. Long-finned bettas need a near-still pocket. Goldfish are messy and need more media and more water changes than a filter rating can replace. No filter excuses an overstocked tank. Ammonia is a stocking and maintenance problem that a bigger motor only delays.

Marine tanks lean on live rock and a protein skimmer to pull dissolved organics out before they become nitrate, plus a sump or a high-quality hang-on skimmer sized for the water volume. A freshwater cartridge filter on a reef will circulate water and will miss the job a skimmer does. Copper medications and some freshwater "algae fix" products are dangerous to invertebrates. Keep marine and freshwater chemical media in separate, labeled boxes so a carbon pouch dosed for a different problem never migrates. Test ammonia and nitrite while a tank is new or after a big media swap. Nitrate tells you whether water changes are keeping up once the cycle is in place.

Upkeep habits and ordinary prices

Write down the last time you rinsed media and the last time you checked the impeller. A filter left until it trickles will dump a cloud of trapped waste back into the tank the day it fails. Keep spare O-rings, a spare impeller, and a length of airline in the same drawer. During a power outage, the bacteria need oxygen. A battery air pump on a sponge, or careful attention so the canister does not go septic and then get dumped back into the display, saves a tank. If a canister sat off for hours in a warm room, rinse the foul water out of it into a drain before you reconnect it to the fish.

Sponge filters and small air pumps are commonly priced near $10 to $30. Hang-on-back units frequently land around $20 to $70. Canisters commonly run about $80 to $280 depending on media volume and hose hardware. Protein skimmers and sump pumps sit in a wider marine budget. . Media refills are the quiet cost. A plan that rinses sponges and replaces carbon only when you have a reason spends less than a subscription of cartridges that throws away your biological filter every month.

Filter questions

How big a filter do I need for a freshwater tank?

Use the advertised gallons-per-hour figure only as a start, because media and hose height cut the real flow. Many keepers begin with a unit rated around four to ten times the tank volume per hour, then adjust for the fish in front of them. Bettas often need a baffled gentle return. Goldfish need more media and more water changes than a sticker can promise. Test ammonia and nitrite while the tank is cycling.

Should I replace the cartridge every month?

Replacing the whole cartridge on a calendar throws away colonized biological media. Rinse the sponge in removed tank water, swap carbon only when you are using carbon for a reason, and seed a new sponge beside the old one before you discard anything. A quiet motor with a clean impeller matters more than a fresh white pad.

Is a reef filter the same as a hang-on-back?

Reef systems usually rely on live rock, a protein skimmer, and often a sump to move and clean water. A freshwater hang-on-back can circulate a small marine tank and still miss the skimming step. Keep copper and other fish medications away from invertebrates, and give any canister or sump a flood plan under the stand.
