Yorkshire Terrier

Owner-focused guide to the best dog food for Yorkshire Terriers based on their small size, moderate energy level, and health needs including dental disease.

Nutritional Needs of Yorkshire Terriers

As a small toy breed with moderate energy levels, the Yorkshire Terrier has specific nutritional requirements that differ from other dogs. Understanding these needs is key to keeping your Yorkshire Terrier healthy throughout their 11-15 yrs lifespan.

Yorkshire Terriers typically weigh 4-7 lbs and need approximately 400–800 calories per day, depending on age, activity level, and metabolism. With moderate energy levels, most Yorkshire Terriers do well on standard feeding guidelines for their size.

Genetic Health Considerations: The Yorkshire Terrier breed has documented susceptibility to dental disease, luxating patella, collapsed trachea. Awareness of these predispositions is valuable for two reasons: it guides preventive screening decisions, and it helps you recognize early symptoms that might otherwise be overlooked.

Health-Specific Diet Considerations

Yorkshire Terriers are prone to several health conditions that can be managed or prevented through proper nutrition.

  • Collapsed Trachea: Look for foods with nutrients that support collapsed trachea management

Best Protein Sources for Yorkshire Terriers

  • Chicken and turkey: Lean, easily digestible proteins ideal for most Yorkshire Terriers

Foods to Avoid

Never feed your Yorkshire Terrier these dangerous foods.

  • Macadamia nuts and alcohol
  • Cooked bones (splintering risk)

Supplements Worth Considering

Based on Yorkshire Terrier-specific health concerns, these supplements may benefit your dog.

  • Glucosamine: Supports joint health as your Yorkshire Terrier ages

Wet Food vs Dry Food for Yorkshire Terriers

Both wet and dry food have advantages for Yorkshire Terriers.

  • Mixed feeding: Many Yorkshire Terrier owners find success combining both for balanced nutrition and variety

Feeding Mistakes to Avoid

Common feeding errors that Yorkshire Terrier owners make include:

  • Overuse of treats: Treats should make up no more than 10% of your Yorkshire Terrier's daily caloric intake. Many training treats are high in calories, so adjust meal portions accordingly.
  • Ignoring body condition: Use the body condition score (BCS) system to assess your Yorkshire Terrier's weight rather than relying solely on scale numbers. You should be able to feel the ribs without pressing hard.

Age-Specific Nutrition Considerations

Your Yorkshire Terrier's nutritional needs change significantly throughout their life.

Adult stage (1-7 years): Maintain a consistent feeding routine with measured portions. Monitor weight monthly and adjust food amounts based on activity level, seasonal changes, and body condition. Adult Yorkshire Terriers benefit from a protein content of 22-30%.

Senior stage (7+ years): Older Yorkshire Terriers may need fewer calories but higher-quality protein to maintain muscle mass. Senior formulas often include joint-supporting nutrients like glucosamine and chondroitin, plus antioxidants for cognitive health. Watch for changes in appetite.

How much should I feed my Yorkshire Terrier?

Adult Yorkshire Terriers typically need 0.5–1.5 cups of high-quality food per day, split into two meals. Adjust based on your dog's activity level, age, and body condition score.

What is the best food brand for Yorkshire Terriers?

Look for foods that list real meat as the first ingredient, meet AAFCO standards, and address Yorkshire Terrier-specific health needs like dental disease. Brands offering small breed-specific formulas are often a good choice.

Should I feed my Yorkshire Terrier grain-free food?

A liver shunt in a Yorkshire Terrier is managed with a veterinary liver diet and, when chosen, surgery. Toy puppies also need frequent meals so blood sugar stays up, and tracheal collapse is managed with weight control. A grain-free label does not match a veterinary liver diet. Use the diet the veterinarian prescribes. A grain allergy is uncommon in Yorkies and is a separate diagnosis.
