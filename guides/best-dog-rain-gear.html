Best Dog Raincoats and Boots

Top waterproof gear for dogs including raincoats, boots, and paw protectors. Covers sizing, materials, and options for every climate.

Which dogs actually need a coat

Rain gear is a fit problem and a coat-type problem before it is a fashion problem. Short-haired dogs, sighthounds, toy breeds, seniors, and dogs with thin body fat get chilled when rain soaks the skin and the wind picks up. A Whippet or an Italian Greyhound on a 45-degree walk in a steady rain is a candidate for a coat with belly coverage. A northern double-coated dog often sheds rain at the outer coat and suffers more from a coat that traps heat and from a wet undercoat left damp in a crate afterward. Those dogs may need a towel and a dryer more than a shell. Watch the dog. Shivering, a hunched back, and a refusal to walk tell you the human is under-dressed in empathy. Panting in a raincoat on a mild day tells you the shell is too much.

Coverage is the design choice that separates a costume from equipment. A back-only cape leaves the chest and belly open, which is exactly where a short-legged dog collects road water. Look for a panel or a strap system that covers the underside without blocking a male dog's ability to urinate, and without a tight band across the sheath or the groin. Sleeves and full "four-leg" suits exist for tiny dogs in cold rain. They are harder to fit, harder to get on a wet animal, and worth it only if you will actually use them. A coat you dread putting on stays in the closet, and the dog stays wet.

Waterproofing, seams, and visibility

Water-resistant fabric beads light drizzle until the durable finish wears off. Waterproof fabric, with taped or welded seams, holds up to a real soak. The weak points are the stitching at the neck, the hole for the leash, and any breathable panel under the belly that is really just mesh. Mesh is ventilation. It is also a drain that puts water on the skin. Read the product notes for whether seams are taped, and accept that a cheap coating will need re-proofing or replacement after a season of city salt and trail mud. A high collar that sheds water off the neck helps. A collar that funnels rain straight down the chest undoes the shell.

Reflective piping and a light color matter at 5 p.m. in November, when you and a black dog in a black coat disappear between streetlights. A small light that clips to the coat or the leash adds another chance for a driver to see you. Test the reflective strips with a flashlight in the hallway before you trust them. Some prints that look bright in daylight go dull under headlights. Hoods are cute and often block peripheral vision or funnel sound so a dog startles more easily. Prefer a coat the dog can see and hear around, with coverage at the neck that still lets the head turn.

Measuring so the coat stays put

Use a soft tape. Measure the back from the base of the neck, where a collar sits, to the base of the tail. Measure the chest girth at the widest part behind the front legs. Measure the neck if the chart asks. If the dog is between sizes, look at the chart's notes on deep chests and long backs. A Lab and a Whippet with the same back length do not share a cut. You want full coverage of the back and belly panel without a hem that sits in the anus or hobbles the hind legs. Walk the dog in the coat indoors. The fabric should move with the shoulder, and a tug on the leash should pull the harness or collar you intended, through a real leash hole, so the shell stays centered.

Closures decide the morning mood. Wide hook-and-loop panels are fast and collect burrs and hair until they quit. Buckles are slower and more secure. A zipper under the belly is precise and miserable if it jams on wet fur. Practice on a dry dog, with treats, for a few days before a storm. A dog who only meets the coat when thunder is already talking will remember the coat as part of the storm. Pair it with an ordinary walk to the corner on a cloudy afternoon so the garment predicts nothing worse than a sniff.

Boots, paw balm, and salt

Sidewalk salt, ice-melt pellets, and frozen puddles hurt pads and then hurt stomachs when the dog licks the chemical off. Boots block that contact when they stay on. Getting them to stay on is the whole sport. Measure the paw width, and remember dewclaws. A boot that crushes the dewclaw will be hated forever. Soft socks with a rubber sole suit some small dogs. Harder boots with a strap above the stop of the paw suit hiking. Introduce one boot at a time indoors, with a treat, and build to four. A dog who bunny-hops in new boots needs a shorter session, and the boots may be too stiff or too long in the toe.

If your dog flings every boot within half a block, use a paw balm before the walk and rinse or wipe the paws when you come in. Balms that form a light barrier are a common winter tool, and they are less protection than a boot that fits. Wipe the belly and the legs too, because salt rides up onto the coat and gets licked later on the rug. Skip human antibiotic ointment as a daily pad coating unless a veterinarian told you to use it for a specific wound. A cracked pad that is bleeding is a clinic visit and a few days of rest, even when you just bought handsome boots. Boots over an untreated deep crack can rub it worse.

After the walk

Take the coat off at the door and hang it where it can drip and then dry, away from a heater that will cook the waterproofing. Shake mud off before it dries into the fleece lining. Wash on the cycle the label allows, because detergent left in a coated fabric can ruin the water behavior. Dry the dog to the skin if the undercoat got wet around the edges of the shell. A damp double coat in a crate overnight is how hot spots and that kennel smell start. Thin-coated dogs need a towel and, if they are still shivering, a warm room before the next trip out. Check armpits and groin for red rub marks after a new coat's first long walk. A red line means the strap sat wrong, and the next walk should wait until you adjust it.

Gear also includes the human end of the leash. A wet nylon leash soaked in road film stains hands and cars. A waterproof lead, kept by the door with the coat, makes you more willing to go. Reflective gear on you, a predictable short route when lightning is present, and the judgment to turn around when the dog is done are part of the same kit. Thunderstorm panic is a behavior and veterinary topic. A raincoat will not fix it, and forcing a terrified dog into a bright jacket during a storm can make the jacket a fear trigger. On those days, a quick potty under an awning beats a training session in a downpour.

Ordinary prices

Simple rain capes often sell around $15 to $40. Coats with belly panels, taped seams, and reflective trim commonly run about $30 to $80, with extra-small or extra-long sighthound cuts sometimes higher. Boot sets frequently land around $20 to $50. Paw balm is often under $20. Those are typical ranges. Replace a coat when the waterproofing has failed, the closures are packed with fur, or the fit no longer matches the dog's weight. Replace boots when the sole is bald or the strap is stretched. A $25 coat that covers the belly and goes on without a wrestle will get used. A $90 coat that lives in a basket will not keep a single drop off the skin.

Rain gear questions

Does a Husky need a raincoat?

Often the more useful step is a thorough dry-down of the undercoat after the walk, because a shell can hold heat on a dog already built for weather. Use a coat when the dog is shivering, very young, very old, or clipped, and skip it when the dog is panting in mild rain. Belly coverage matters more on short-legged and short-coated breeds whose skin takes the spray directly.

How do I get boots to stay on?

Measure paw width, clear the dewclaw, and practice indoors with one boot and a treat before you try a wet sidewalk. Soft soles suit some small dogs. Stiffer hiking boots need a shorter introduction. If every boot still flies off, switch to a paw balm before the walk and a rinse afterward so salt does not get licked off on the rug.

What should I measure before ordering a coat?

Back length from the collar line to the tail base, and chest girth behind the front legs. Check the coat indoors for a free shoulder, a hem that stays clear of the rear, and a real opening for the leash so you are pulling the harness or collar you meant to pull. Reflective trim should still look bright under a flashlight, which is how headlights will see it.
