Mushroom Poisoning in

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Toxic mushroom species dangerous to dogs, symptoms of mushroom poisoning, emergency response, and preventing mushroom ingestion on walks.

A yard mushroom is an unidentified toxin

Dogs eat mushrooms because they smell interesting and sit at nose height in mulch, lawns, and on walks after rain. Some species only upset the stomach. Some destroy the liver. Some trigger hallucinations, drooling and tearing, or seizures. You cannot tell those groups apart from a photo on your phone with enough confidence to skip the call. A mushroom that "looks like the ones we cook" can be a toxic lookalike. The safe action is to assume the mushroom is dangerous until a veterinarian or a poison hotline, sometimes with a mycologist, says otherwise.

Remove other pets from the area. If you can do it without getting bitten or delaying travel, scoop a sample of the mushroom into a paper bag, including the base that was underground. Photographs of the cap, gills, and the place it grew help. Wash your hands. Do not taste it to "see if it is the edible kind." Human edibility lists are a poor guide for dogs, and a taste wastes time. Note the time the dog was last seen near the patch and whether any pieces are in the teeth or the vomit.

Groups of mushroom toxins, in plain language

Gastrointestinal mushrooms cause vomiting and diarrhea that can be intense and dehydrating even when the liver is spared. Amanita and some Galerina species contain amatoxins that quietly injure the liver and kidneys after an early wave of vomiting. A dog can look better and then become jaundiced, weak, and collapsed a day or more later. That quiet gap is similar in spirit to other delayed toxins and it is a reason to be seen even if the vomiting stopped. Inocybe and Clitocybe species can cause a muscarinic picture: drooling, tearing, vomiting, diarrhea, and a slow heart rate. Isoxazole mushrooms such as some Amanita muscaria relatives cause drunken behavior, tremors, and seizures. Psilocybin mushrooms cause agitation, dilated pupils, and a dog who startles at nothing. Gyromitra species are linked with liver and neurologic injury. You do not need to sort these in the yard. You need the clinic to know that more than one pattern exists so they monitor liver values and the nervous system.

Lawn chemicals and slug bait complicate the story. A dog who vomits after a rainy walk may have eaten a mushroom, a metaldehyde pellet, or both. Tell the hotline about any bait in the yard. Tremors and a high temperature push metaldehyde up the list. Liver failure the next day pushes amatoxin up the list. The sample in the bag is what lets those paths separate.

What to do right away

Call your veterinarian or an emergency clinic, and call a poison hotline if the clinic wants that support. Describe the dog's weight, the time, and the signs you see: vomiting, drooling, wobbliness, tremors, or a seizure. Skip home vomiting unless the mushroom consult says this dog should vomit. A dog who is already sedated, tremoring, or brachycephalic can inhale vomit. Do not give salt, oil, or milk. If the dog is seizuring, go in and call ahead. Keep the dog cool and quiet in the car. Bring the mushroom sample and any vomit in a sealed bag if they ask you to collect it safely.

Hospital care may include decontamination when the timing and the dog's condition allow, activated charcoal in selected cases, fluids, seizure control, and repeated bloodwork to watch the liver and kidneys. There is no universal mushroom antidote you can apply at home. Some specific treatments exist in hospital for specific toxins, and they depend on identification and on how early you arrived. Ask when the next liver test should be, even if the dog looks improved tonight. Amatoxin cases are famous for the false recovery.

Making the yard less of a buffet

Walk the lawn after rain and remove mushrooms with a glove. Bag them for the trash. An open compost becomes a second meal if a dog raids it. Fix the irrigation that keeps a corner soggy. Mulch can grow mushrooms. Rake it back and ask a landscaper about options if the flush is constant. On walks, use a basket muzzle for a determined scavenger while you train a leave-it, and fit the muzzle so panting is easy. Teach the cue with ordinary objects before you need it on a mushroom.

Tell walkers and pet sitters that any mushroom in the mouth is a phone call, with a photo. Children should know the same rule and should not be sent to "clean up the yard" without a bag and a glove. Indoor potted plants can grow mushrooms in wet soil. Scoop those too. If a dog eats one before you notice, the call is the same as for a lawn mushroom. Identification is uncertain, and the cost of waiting is a liver injury you cannot see until tomorrow.

  • Treat every wild mushroom ingestion as a call to a veterinarian or poison hotline.
  • Bag a sample of the whole mushroom, including the base, if you can do it safely.
  • Expect patterns that range from gut upset to liver failure, drooling and slow heart rate, or seizures.
  • Remember that a dog may look better and then develop liver failure.
  • Do not induce vomiting unless instructed, especially if the dog is tremoring.
  • Mention slug bait if it is also in the yard.
  • Bring the sample and ask when liver values should be rechecked.
  • Remove mushrooms after rain and train a leave-it on walks.

The quiet day after a mushroom

Some mushrooms cause an early vomit and then a false calm before liver injury. If the clinic sends the dog home, ask which morning the liver values should be repeated and what gum color, vomiting, or weakness should cancel the wait. Keep the dog from the same lawn. Bag any new mushrooms with a glove and note whether slug bait pellets are mixed into the mulch. A tremor and a high temperature can be a mushroom or metaldehyde. The sample and the bait box let the hotline separate them. Do not taste a piece to compare it with a cookbook photo.

On walks, a basket muzzle fitted for panting can bridge the weeks while a leave-it cue gets reliable. Children should be told that yard mushrooms are a phone call, with a photo, and that they should not kick the mushrooms into the compost the dog raids. Indoor pots that sprout mushrooms get the same rule. Identification apps are wrong often enough that the call still happens. The cost of an early visit is smaller than the cost of a liver failure found on day two.

Questions about mushroom poisoning

Can I identify a safe mushroom from a photo?

No. Toxic and edible species look alike, and apps are wrong often enough to be dangerous. Call a veterinarian or a poison hotline after any wild mushroom is eaten. A bagged sample and clear photos help experts. Do not taste the mushroom yourself.

Why do some dogs seem fine and then get worse?

Amatoxin mushrooms can cause early vomiting, a quieter period, and then liver and kidney injury a day or more later. Other groups cause tremors or heavy drooling sooner. Because you cannot know the group in the yard, early care and follow-up bloodwork matter.

Should I make my dog vomit after eating a mushroom?

Only if a veterinarian or poison hotline says so. A dog who is seizuring, very sedated, or short-faced can inhale vomit. Bring a mushroom sample if you go to the clinic, and mention any slug bait used in the same yard.
