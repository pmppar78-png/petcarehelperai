Cataracts in Dogs

Understanding cataracts in dogs including causes, stages, surgery options, costs, and managing vision loss in senior dogs.

What a cataract is

A cataract is an opacity in the lens. The lens sits behind the pupil and should be clear so light reaches the retina. When lens proteins clump, the pupil looks white, gray, or blue-white, and vision fades in that eye in proportion to how much of the lens has turned opaque. A mature cataract fills the lens and the dog is blind in that eye. An immature cataract still lets some light through. A hypermature cataract is an older opacity that may start to shrink and wrinkle, and it is more likely to inflame the eye.

Nuclear sclerosis is the look-alike that fools people. It is a normal hardening of the lens center with age, a pearly haze, and the tapetal reflection still shines back when a light is shone in. Dogs with nuclear sclerosis usually navigate familiar rooms. A cataract blocks that reflection in the opaque zone. Telling the two apart takes an eye exam. It cannot be done from a phone photo taken in a yellow kitchen.

Which dogs develop cataracts

Inherited cataracts show up in many breeds and at different ages. Miniature and Standard Poodles, Cocker Spaniels, Bichon Frises, Boston Terriers, Siberian Huskies, Labrador Retrievers, Golden Retrievers, Miniature Schnauzers, and Australian Shepherds are among the breeds ophthalmologists name often. Some are juvenile and some arrive in middle age. Diabetes mellitus is the great acquired cause in dogs: high sugar pulls water into the lens, and both eyes can cloud over days to weeks. Trauma, uveitis, a retinal disease such as progressive retinal atrophy, electric-cord shock, and occasional nutritional problems in hand-raised puppies are the other routes. Age alone can produce cataracts, and age alone also produces nuclear sclerosis, which is why every cloudy eye in a senior deserves a real look before anyone says it is "just old."

A diabetic dog who bumps into furniture and has two rapidly whitening pupils needs a glucose check and an eye exam. Tight control of diabetes cannot clear a lens that has already opacified. It can slow some early change and it protects the rest of the body. The lens decision is separate.

Signs owners notice

Owners see a new whiteness in the pupil, clumsiness in dim light, hesitation on stairs, and a dog who startles when approached from the cloudy side. Some dogs cope so well in a familiar house that blindness is only obvious on a night walk. Redness, squinting, tearing, or a suddenly painful eye means the cataract is no longer a quiet opacity. Lens-induced uveitis and glaucoma are the complications that hurt. A luxated lens can make the pupil look odd and the eye painful. Sudden blindness with a big pupil and a rock-hard globe is a pressure emergency even if a cataract is also present.

  • A white or gray pupil that was clear in last year's photos.
  • Bumping into chairs, missing treats tossed to one side, or refusing dark hallways.
  • Both eyes clouding quickly in a dog who is drinking and urinating more, a diabetic pattern.
  • Squinting, redness, or rubbing the eye, which suggests uveitis or high pressure.
  • A bluish haze in an old dog who still sees well, which may be nuclear sclerosis and still needs an exam to be sure.

How a veterinarian diagnoses it

A cataract is diagnosed by looking through the pupil. The ophthalmologist separates it from nuclear sclerosis, the normal aging haze that does not usually blind a dog. When the lens is too opaque to see the retina, an electrical recording of retinal function and an ultrasound of the globe are done before anyone discusses removing the lens. The questions on this page already cover what those tests decide. Eye pressure is measured at the same visit because glaucoma can sit behind a cataract.

A slit-lamp exam shows how much of the lens is involved and whether the capsule is intact. Tonometry measures intraocular pressure and is the screen for glaucoma. If surgery is even a possibility, an electroretinogram checks that the retina still generates an electrical response, and an ocular ultrasound looks for a detached retina or a lens that has started to shift. Those two tests are how a surgeon avoids removing a lens from an eye that cannot see anyway. Fluorescein stain is added if the surface looks painful, to rule out a corneal ulcer that would change the plan for the week. Bloodwork, including glucose and a fuller panel in a senior, looks for diabetes and for anesthetic risk before anyone books phacoemulsification.

Treatment categories

No drop clears a cloudy lens. Removing the lens can restore vision only when the retina still works, and the ophthalmologist decides whether that dog is a candidate. A dog who cannot have lens surgery can still live well if the eye stays comfortable and pressure is rechecked. Pain control and pressure-lowering drops, when glaucoma is present, are dosed by the ophthalmologist.

The operation used in dogs is phacoemulsification: the lens is broken up and aspirated through a small incision, and an artificial lens is placed when the capsule can hold it. Anti-inflammatory drops are used around the surgery and also when a cataract is causing uveitis in a dog who will not have surgery. Pressure-lowering drops are a separate prescription if glaucoma is present. They protect comfort and the optic nerve. They do not clear the lens. A blind dog with comfortable eyes and controlled pressure can use scent, sound, and a stable floor plan. Surgery is declined when the retina is dead, the eye is already glaucomatous and painful beyond salvage, or the dog cannot safely undergo anesthesia.

Home care

Keep furniture where the dog has memorized it, block stairs until you know how the dog handles them, and speak before you touch a blind dog. Lights help whatever vision remains. Use a harness and a short leash outdoors, and skip off-leash time near roads or pools. If diabetes is the cause, give insulin and feed exactly as the internal-medicine plan says, and keep the glucose rechecks. Give eye drops on the schedule the ophthalmologist wrote, and wait the interval they asked for between two different drops so each one stays on the eye. An Elizabethan collar may be needed after surgery so a paw does not open the incision. Call the same day if the eye turns red, the dog squints, or the pupil looks suddenly different after a surgery or during a watch-and-wait plan.

When it is an emergency

When to See the Vet Immediately

A painful red eye, a bulging eye, sudden loss of vision with a dilated pupil, clouding that appears over hours with squinting, or trauma to the globe should be seen immediately. Glaucoma can blind an eye permanently in a short window, and uveitis needs treatment before the lens and the retina suffer more damage. A diabetic dog who is vomiting, weak, or breathing with a sweet odor is a medical emergency on top of any cataract. The lens can wait a day. Diabetic crisis cannot.

What to ask about the bill

No drop dissolves a cataract. The American College of Veterinary Ophthalmologists states that advertised medical remedies do not reverse, slow, or prevent a lens opacity, and that surgery is the only treatment shown to restore vision. Phacoemulsification removes the cloudy lens and, when the capsule allows, an artificial lens is placed. An electroretinogram and an ocular ultrasound have to show a working retina first. Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center reports success as high as 80 to 90 percent in ideal candidates, and a lower chance once the lens is hypermature or the retina is already damaged. Dogs that cannot undergo surgery can live well with blindness if the eye stays comfortable and is checked for glaucoma.

Cataract spending is an ophthalmologist's per-eye quote plus the electroretinogram and ultrasound that decide whether phacoemulsification can help. Operating an eye whose retina is already dead spends the surgical fee and does not restore sight, so price that testing before the surgery. Ask what the second eye adds. Pressure-lowering drops, if glaucoma is already present, are a smaller bill that continues either way.

Key Questions

Is the cloudy look in my old dog always a cataract?

No. Nuclear sclerosis is a common aging change that leaves a bluish center and usually spares useful vision. A cataract blocks light and can progress to blindness in that eye. Only a direct exam, often with the pupil dilated, separates them. If the dog is also drinking heavily, ask for a glucose check the same visit, because diabetic cataracts move fast and the diabetes itself needs treatment.

Can drops dissolve a dog cataract?

No drop on the market has been shown to clear a canine cataract. The American College of Veterinary Ophthalmologists has said as much about advertised remedies. Surgery, by phacoemulsification, is the procedure that can restore vision when an electroretinogram and an ultrasound show the retina is still working. Drops still have a job when the eye is inflamed or the pressure is high. That job is comfort and pressure, and the lens stays cloudy.

What if surgery is off the table?

Many blind dogs live well when the house stays predictable and the eyes stay comfortable. Rechecks for glaucoma and uveitis still matter, because a cataract can hurt even when vision is already gone. Painful eyes that cannot be kept comfortable are sometimes removed so the dog can stop living with an ache. That decision belongs to the ophthalmologist and the family after pressure checks, and it is about pain, which is separate from whether the dog can still find the food bowl by scent.
