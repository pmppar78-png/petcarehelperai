Complete Care Guide

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Everything about betta fish care including tank setup, water parameters, feeding, common diseases, tank mates, and debunking betta myths.

Housing and Enclosure

A betta is a tropical labyrinth fish from slow, warm water, and the tank has to copy that. Five gallons is the smallest filtered, heated volume that stays stable for one fish. Ten gallons is easier to heat evenly and gives long-finned males room to turn without shredding their fins on the glass. A bowl, a vase, or an unfiltered cup cools overnight and loads up with ammonia. That setup is the usual reason a new betta fades in the first month.

Hold the water at 76 to 82 F with a heater that has its own thermostat, and check it with a separate thermometer. Bettas cannot warm themselves. Appetite, color, and fin healing all slow down when the room drops. Place the heater near the filter outflow so the warm water actually circulates, and keep the heater fully submerged the way the manufacturer specifies. A tight lid matters because bettas jump, especially when the water is low or a reflection startles them.

Flow should be gentle. Their natural ditches barely move, and a powerful hang-on-back filter can pin a long-finned fish against the glass. A sponge filter, a baffled outflow, or a filter turned down with a valve keeps the surface rippling enough for gas exchange without making a treadmill. Leave a quiet patch of surface where the fish can gulp air. The labyrinth organ lets a betta breathe atmospheric air, which is useful in still water and useless as an excuse to skip filtration.

Furnish the tank with silk plants or live plants such as Anubias, Java fern, and floating stems. Plastic plants with sharp seams cut flowing fins. A broad leaf or a betta hammock just under the surface gives a resting shelf, which long-finned fish use when they tire. Dim the room at night. Constant light plus a mirror glued to the glass keeps a male displaying until he is exhausted. One male per tank is the rule. Females can sometimes live in a carefully planned group, and that project needs more volume, more cover, and experience with aggression.

Diet and Nutrition

A named betta pellet is the daily food. Choose a pellet the fish can take in one bite. Two or three pellets twice a day is enough for most adults, and anything still circling after about two minutes should come out. Leftover food is the fastest way to foul a small tank. Fast one day a week if the fish is thick through the middle. A betta's stomach is roughly the size of its eye, so a heaping pinch is a feast it cannot finish.

Rotate frozen or freeze-dried bloodworms, brine shrimp, and daphnia two or three times a week. Thaw frozen food in a little tank water and rinse it so the packing juice stays out of the aquarium. Live foods are optional and should come from a clean culture you trust. Skip feeder fish. They carry disease and are too large a meal. Peas are a poor staple. A betta is a carnivore, and plant matter does little for swim-bladder trouble that actually comes from overfeeding or cold water.

Color-enhancing foods are ordinary pellets with extra carotenoids. They will not repair a fish kept in ammonia. If the betta ignores food, check temperature and test the water before you buy a new brand. A hungry betta in clean, warm water usually hunts the pellet as it sinks.

Health and Veterinary

Fin rot, ich, velvet, swim-bladder trouble, and dropsy are the problems keepers name most often. Fin rot looks like ragged, receding edges, sometimes with a white or red line, and it usually follows ammonia, a torn fin, or a chill. Ich shows as discrete white spots like salt grains, with flashing against plants. Velvet is finer, like a dusty gold film, and it is easy to miss until the fish clamps its fins and hides. Dropsy is a swollen body with raised scales, a sign of internal failure, and it needs a fish veterinarian quickly. Swim-bladder trouble shows as listing or a struggle to leave the bottom. Warm, clean water and smaller meals fix many mild cases. Pineconing and gasping at the surface with normal temperature do not.

Test ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate on a schedule, and change about a quarter of the water every week with a conditioner that treats chlorine and chloramine. Match the new water to the tank temperature. A gravel vacuum pulls waste out of the plant bases where pellets hide. The Merck Veterinary Manual's aquarium-fish chapter treats ammonia and nitrite poisoning as the most common aquarium emergency. It follows an uncycled tank, overfeeding, a dead fish left in the water, or a filter that stopped. Both readings should sit at 0 ppm. Because a betta cannot set its own body temperature, every body process follows the water, so the thermometer and the thermostat heater stay on the equipment list for this tropical species.

Ich and other parasites usually arrive on new fish or new plants. Quarantine arrivals in a separate tank before they join a display. Ragged fins from bacteria usually show up after dirty water or a tear. Copper treatments are toxic to shrimp, snails, and plants, and they can set the filter bacteria back, so ammonia and nitrite need watching for weeks afterward. If you keep invertebrates anywhere the net or the bucket is shared, keep copper out of that gear. See a veterinarian who actually treats fish when the betta stops eating for more than a couple of days, breathes hard in clean warm water, or swells.

Reference: Merck Veterinary Manual aquarium-fish management.

Tank Mates and a Calm Routine

Most bettas do best alone. Bright, long-finned tank mates get mistaken for rivals. Nippy tetras and barbs shred a halfmoon tail. Shrimp and snails may be safe with a slow female and unsafe with a hunting male, so watch the first evening. If you add fish, add them to a tank that is already planted and cycled, a few at a time, so the filter can catch the extra waste. Float the bag to match temperature, then mix in tank water slowly before release. Chase the fish with a net only when you must. The chase itself is a stressor.

  • Cycle the tank until ammonia and nitrite read 0 ppm, then acclimate the betta.
  • Keep the heater at 76 to 82 F and confirm it with a thermometer morning and night for the first week.
  • Feed a measured pellet meal twice a day and remove leftovers.
  • Change about 25 percent of the water weekly and rinse filter media in old tank water.
  • Wash your hands before they go in the tank. Soap and lotion stay on the skin.

A healthy betta flares at a reflection now and then, builds a bubble nest if it is a male in soft water, and meets you at the front at feeding time. Clamped fins, faded color, or a fish that lies in a plant all day are reasons to test the water the same hour. Waiting until next weekend wastes the window.

When you bring a betta home, dim the lights and skip rearranging the plants for a few days. The fish is learning where the surface is and where the current dies down. Bubble nests on the filter intake or under a leaf are a sign a male feels steady enough to display. They are optional. Plenty of healthy males never build one in a busy room. What you want to see is upright fins, a fish that can hold position in the gentle flow, and a pellet taken on the first or second pass.

Quick Answers

Are betta fish good pets for beginners?

Yes, if they get a heated, filtered tank of at least 5 gallons kept at 76 to 82°F. A bowl without a heater or filter is the most common reason a beginner loses a betta.

How often should I change a betta's water?

Change about 25 percent of the water weekly and test the parameters regularly. Feed only what the fish eats in about two minutes so leftover food does not foul the water.
