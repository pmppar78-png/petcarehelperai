African Grey Parrot Care Guide

Comprehensive African Grey parrot care guide covering diet, mental stimulation, talking ability, common health issues, and lifespan expectations.

Housing and Enclosure

African greys are large parrots with a long memory, and the cage is a piece of furniture you will live with for decades. A Congo grey needs a cage at least 36 inches wide, 24 inches deep, and 48 inches high, with bar spacing around 3/4 to 1 inch. Timneh greys are a bit smaller and still need a large flight cage. A cockatiel hand-me-down is far too small. Width lets the bird move between perches without a wing striking steel. Place the cage in the social center of the house and out of the kitchen. Fumes from overheated nonstick pans, self-cleaning ovens, and many aerosols are fatal. Greys also dislike chaos at eye level. A quiet corner of a busy room works better than a hallway where every coat brushes the bars.

Perches must vary. Natural branches of several diameters, a grooming perch used part of the day, and a comfortable sleeping perch prevent the pressure sores greys get from standing on one dowel. Stainless bowls, a separate water dish, and foraging toys that actually take time are the rest of the fit-out. Greys shred less manically than macaws and still destroy soft wood. Rotate puzzles. A bored grey plucks. A full-spectrum bird light on a timer can set day length in a dark room. Calcium and vitamin D status in greys is a diet and veterinary issue. Indoor greys get little UVB through window glass. Ask an avian veterinarian about a bird-specific full-spectrum lamp and blood calcium (Association of Avian Veterinarians; Merck Veterinary Manual, Nutrition in Pet Birds).

Plan for 50 years or more with a Congo grey. The cage, the stand, and the person who will take the bird if you cannot are part of housing. A single grey bonds hard. It needs hours of interaction, training, and out-of-cage time in a bird-proof room. Another parrot fails as an automatic solution and can become a second welfare problem.

Diet and Nutrition

A formulated pellet should be the majority of the diet, with vegetables and some fruit every day, and nuts as measured training rewards. Greys are prone to calcium deficiency. Broccoli, kale, cooked squash, and almonds contribute calcium-rich foods alongside the pellet. A seed mix as the whole diet is a known path to deficiency, low blood calcium, and seizures. If a grey is on seed now, convert slowly, weigh the bird, and involve an avian veterinarian if the animal is already weak or a hen that lays.

Avoid avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, onion, and high-salt or high-sugar snacks. Fresh water daily. Greys dunk food, so expect to wash the bowl. Foraging belongs on the daily plan as required work. A grey that finishes a bowl of pellets in five minutes has the rest of the day to invent a problem. Hide a portion of the meal in paper, a puzzle, or a kabob. Pull fresh food before it spoils. These birds are too smart to "not notice" a rotten berry, and they will eat it anyway.

Body condition hides under dense feathers. Learn to feel the keel with an avian veterinarian's guidance, and keep a gram scale. A slow loss over months is easy to call "mood" and hard to reverse once the bird is on the cage floor. Treats from the table should be tiny and counted, because a grey will train you faster than you train it.

Health and Veterinary Care

Feather destructive behavior, calcium-related seizures, aspergillosis, and psittacine beak and feather disease are the grey-specific fears. Plucking can be medical, environmental, or both, and it needs an avian workup before a collar or a spray. Seizures and weakness can follow low calcium, especially in birds on seed or hens that lay. Aspergillosis is a fungal respiratory disease linked to moldy bedding, poor ventilation, and a stressed immune system. It looks like a long, quiet decline in breathing. PBFD is viral. Buy from sources that test, and quarantine any new parrot away from a grey you already love.

Annual exams with an avian veterinarian are the minimum, and many grey owners do bloodwork on a schedule because these birds mask disease. A fluffed grey, a change in droppings, or a voice that goes hoarse is a call today. Find the clinic, the after-hours plan, and a carrier that a grey will enter for a treat, long before an emergency. Dog-and-cat dosing charts are unsafe here.

Behavior is medical when it destroys feathers or sleep. A grey that screams for hours may be under-slept, under-stimulated, or rehearsing a scream that once brought you running. Cover the needs first: 10 to 12 hours of dark, foraging, and training. Then get help from a person who understands parrots if the pattern stays. Punishment makes a grey afraid and does not make it quiet.

Living with a Grey

  • Budget daily out-of-cage time with fans off and cookware watched.
  • Teach step-up and a station behavior while the bird is young or newly calm.
  • Expect mimicry of alarms, coughs, and arguments. They repeat what the house repeats.
  • Keep one person consistent on bedtime so the bird can settle.
  • Write down who inherits the bird. A grey can outlive the purchase decision by half a century.

A stable African grey talks or whistles, works a toy, eats pellets and vegetables, and lets you see a bright eye and a smooth chest. A silent, plucking, or seizing bird needs an avian veterinarian immediately. This is a poor first bird for most households and an extraordinary companion for someone who already lives like a parrot keeper.

A Week That Keeps a Grey Steady

Pick a bedtime and defend it. Greys unravel when the living room stays bright until midnight and the morning starts with a shout. Ten to twelve hours of dark, a covered cage or a dim room, and the same person saying goodnight gives the bird a clock. In the morning, uncover the cage, offer water you washed, and put the vegetable bowl in before the nut. Training for ten minutes, a foraging toy, and a shoulder or a stand session later in the day cover the social need without turning every hour into a performance.

Write down three foods the bird ate and one behavior you liked. Greys train their people with silence and screams, and a notebook shows whether the scream bought attention. If the chest feathers look rough, photograph them weekly in the same light so you can tell a molt from real plucking. Keep the carrier in sight with a treat inside it twice a week so a clinic trip is familiar. Ask the avian veterinarian which calcium-rich foods fit this bird, and bring the diet list to the annual exam along with a gram weight.

Travel, houseguests, and renovations need a plan. A grey that can go to a quiet room with its own stand survives a party better than a grey left in the middle of the noise. Never cook with the bird in the kitchen, and never run a self-clean oven cycle with a parrot in the home. Those are the weeks owners remember for the wrong reason. The ordinary weeks, with pellets, kale, a puzzle, and a dark night, are the ones that add up to decades.

Quick Answers

Are African Grey Parrots good for first-time bird owners?

Usually they are a second bird, or a first bird only for someone who has already arranged a large cage, a pellet diet, hours of daily interaction, and an avian veterinarian. Their lifespan and sensitivity punish a casual setup.

How much does African grey care cost?

The cage alone is often a four-figure purchase. Year one should also include foraging toys and an avian exam with a conversation about blood calcium, because greys are prone to hypocalcemia. A 2026 exotic-clinic roundup lists the exam itself at about $95 to $185 before the chemistry panel (findanexoticvet.com). AAV recommends those regular visits and publishes no national fee schedule (aav.org).
