Crested Gecko Care Guide

Complete crested gecko care including diet, housing, humidity, handling, health issues, and why they are popular low-maintenance reptile pets.

Housing and Enclosure

Crested geckos are arboreal lizards from New Caledonia, and the cage has to be tall. One adult does well in an enclosure about 18 inches wide, 18 inches deep, and 24 inches high, and a larger vertical tank is better if you can heat and mist it evenly. Front-opening glass or PVC makes misting and feeding less of a scramble. A screen top helps ventilation. A fully sealed tub holds humidity and also holds stale air, which is how respiratory trouble starts.

These geckos handle a brief rise poorly. Sustained heat above about 82 F is dangerous, so skip the basking lamp that a bearded dragon needs. If the room already sits in the 70s, you may need no heat at all. If the room falls into the 60s, a low-wattage ceramic heater or a thermostat-controlled panel on the side, aimed so the gecko can move away, is enough. Night drops into the upper 60s are acceptable. A thermometer at the top and another near the floor tell you whether the plants are creating a pocket of hot air.

Humidity should swing. Mist heavily in the evening so the cage climbs toward 70 or 80 percent, then let it dry toward 50 or 60 percent by the next afternoon. Constant soggy substrate grows mold and skin infections. Live plants such as pothos, or sturdy artificial vines, plus cork tubes and horizontal branches, give the gecko somewhere to sleep and somewhere to drink. Cresties often ignore a water bowl and lick droplets off leaves, so keep a small dish anyway and refresh it. Babies need more cover than adults. A bare tank with one stick is a stressed gecko that refuses food.

UVB is useful and should stay mild. A low-output linear UVB, the sort sold for shade-dwelling forest species, over a mesh top gives the animal a choice. Crested geckos do not need the intense desert tube a bearded dragon basks under, and a strong lamp close to a branch can burn a thin-skinned gecko or overheat the cage. Provide leaves so the animal can sit in shade. Replace the bulb on the manufacturer's schedule, because UVB output fades while the lamp still looks bright.

Diet and Nutrition

The staple is a complete powdered crested gecko diet, mixed to the consistency the brand describes and offered in a small cup. Commercial diets formulated for this species, including the well-known powdered mixes keepers stir with water, already contain calcium and vitamins. Refresh the cup every other day, sooner if it smells sour or dries out. A gecko that only eats insects will eventually run short of the balance those powders were built to provide. Insects are the supplement: gut-loaded crickets or dubia roaches once or twice a week, no wider than the space between the gecko's eyes.

Dust those insects with a light calcium supplement if they are more than an occasional snack, and keep a small dish of plain calcium in the cage for females that are producing eggs. Do not turn the whole feeding plan into a reptile multivitamin shake on top of a complete powdered diet. Doubling up fat-soluble vitamins is its own problem. Fruit puree from the kitchen is a treat at most. It is sugary, it spoils, and it teaches some geckos to ignore the complete diet. Skip avocado and anything seasoned.

Juveniles eat a fresh mix several times a week and grow fast if the temperature is right. Adults can look "off food" for days and still be healthy, especially males in breeding season or anyone kept too warm. Weigh the gecko every week or two on a gram scale. A slow decline tells you more than one skipped cup. Offer the food cup up in the foliage, where a crestie actually hunts, and lead with a smaller cup if ants and fruit flies are raiding it.

Health and Veterinary

Metabolic bone disease shows up when calcium, vitamin D, or UVB is missing for a long stretch, usually in a gecko fed only insects or kept with no UVB and no complete diet. A soft jaw, kinks in the tail, and a reluctance to climb are late signs. Floppy tail syndrome is a posture problem seen in some cresties that sleep upside down with the tail bent over the back. More horizontal perches and fewer steep glass sleeps may help, and a reptile veterinarian should look at any animal that seems painful. A dropped tail does not grow back. Cresties drop the tail when grabbed by it, when the cage is too hot, or during a rough shed. The stump heals. The balance changes. Never hold a crested gecko by the tail.

Respiratory infections follow cold, wet cages with no airflow: open-mouth breathing, bubbles at the nose, and a gecko that stays low. Stuck shed on the toes can cut off circulation. Raise humidity for a day and check the toes under a light. A blackened toe tip needs a veterinarian. Parasites show up in wild-caught animals and in groups kept on soggy substrate. A fecal exam at the first wellness visit is worth more than a shop dewormer. Find a reptile veterinarian before you need an emergency, and bring a photo of the cage temperatures if the animal stops climbing.

Handling stays short. Let a new gecko settle for a week or two, eating and using the hides, before you offer a hand. Scoop from below. A crestie that leaps will stick to your shirt and then panic if you peel it off by the tail. Wash hands before and after. These lizards are hardy when the room is cool and the diet is a complete powder. They decline quickly in a hot glass box with only crickets.

Daily Pattern

  • Check the top temperature on warm afternoons. If it crowds 80 F, cool the room or raise the lamp.
  • Mist in the evening and watch the hygrometer fall again by the next day.
  • Replace the diet cup on a steady schedule and note whether the gecko licked it clean.
  • Glance at toes and tail tip during shed week.
  • Keep males apart. Two males in one tall tank will fight, and the loser stops eating.

A settled crested gecko sleeps plastered to a cork tube all day, eyes open a slit, and hunts the branches after dark. Crests up, body filled out, and a clean vent are the picture you want. A gecko on the floor in the morning, day after day, is either too hot, too cold, or ill, and the thermometer is the first tool you pick up.

Heat, a Cup of Diet, and a Tail That Stays On

Check the top of the cage on warm afternoons. Crested geckos want the low to mid 70s F, and a room that feels pleasant to you can still push the upper branches past 82 F. If the thermometer climbs, cool the room or raise any lamp. These geckos do not need a desert basking site. A low UVB tube over mesh, with leaves for shade, is enough light for a forest species, and a complete powdered diet already carries calcium. Insects once or twice a week are the extra, dusted lightly if they become frequent, and kept smaller than the space between the eyes.

Weigh the gecko in grams every week or two. A skipped food cup for a few days can be normal. A slow slide on the scale is the signal to recheck temperature and to call a reptile veterinarian. Never grab the tail. A dropped tail does not return, and the stump heals into a different balance. Scoop from below after the animal has eaten for a week in the new cage. Mist in the evening so humidity rises, then let it fall by the next afternoon. A gecko on the floor every morning is too hot, too cold, or ill, and the thermometers are the first thing you read.

Quick Answers

Are crested geckos good for beginner reptile owners?

They suit a beginner who can keep a tall cage in the low to mid 70s F, mist it so humidity rises and then falls, and feed a complete powdered diet. They do poorly in a hot room or on insects alone.

The cage, branches, and a starter supply of powdered diet are the opening cost, often a few hundred dollars. A mild UVB tube, if you use one, follows Zoo Med's one-year replacement note (zoomed.com). Crested geckos need a tall enclosure — minimum 18"x18"x24" for one adult.

Maintain temperatures of 72-78°F (they do not tolerate temperatures above 82°F).

Provide live or artificial plants, climbing branches, and cork bark hides.

Mist the enclosure twice daily to maintain 60-80% humidity.

Feed a complete crested gecko diet (Pangea, Repashy, or similar) as the staple, offered in a small dish refreshed every other day.

Supplement with gut-loaded insects like crickets or dubia roaches 1-2 times per week.

Always provide fresh water.

They are generally hardy when kept at proper temperatures.

Watch for kinked tails, soft jaw, or lethargy.
