Pet Turtle Care

Complete pet turtle care guide covering Red-Eared Sliders, Painted Turtles, and Box Turtles. Includes aquatic and land habitat setup, diet, and health.

Housing and Enclosure

Pet turtles split into aquatic animals and box turtles, and the cage for one will harm the other. Red-eared sliders and painted turtles need a large aquarium with water deep enough to swim and a completely dry basking dock. A common planning figure is about 10 gallons of water volume per inch of shell length, with extra floor space as the turtle grows. A hatchling slider in a 20-gallon tank becomes a large animal that needs a stock tank or a 75- to 125-gallon aquarium. The filter has to be rated for two or three times the actual water volume because turtles eat and defecate in the same water. Canister filters are the usual answer. A small internal filter will look busy and still leave the tank cloudy.

Basking is mandatory for aquatic turtles. The dock must be dry, easy to climb, and warm. A basking lamp should put the shell surface around 85 to 90 F, measured with an infrared thermometer on the dock. Brightness is a poor guess. Water temperature for sliders often sits in the mid 70s F, warmer for hatchlings, with its own heater if the room is cool. UVB is a linear tube over the basking area, at the distance the manufacturer charts for a basking reptile, replaced on schedule. Glass and most mesh block UVB. A turtle that can never dry off and never sit under UVB will develop a soft shell and swollen eyes even if the water is clean. Night heat is rarely needed if the room stays comfortable. The dock lamp goes off so the animal has a dark period.

Box turtles are a different build. They need a terrestrial pen with shade, a humid hide, a shallow water dish they can sit in, and outdoor time when your climate and local wildlife laws allow. They still need UVB and a warm spot if they live indoors. An aquatic tank with a tiny dock is the wrong house for a box turtle, and a dry tortoise table is the wrong house for a slider. Know the species before you buy the tank. Also know the law. Red-eared sliders are restricted in some regions because released pets become invasive.

Any turtle setup needs a lid or a room the animal cannot walk out of. Sliders climb better than people expect. Electrical cords and splash do not mix. Use a drip loop and a ground-fault outlet near the tank.

Diet and Nutrition

Aquatic turtles are omnivores, and the balance shifts as they age. Hatchlings and juveniles need more protein: a commercial turtle pellet, plus insects, earthworms, or a little frozen fish or shrimp. Adults, especially red-eared sliders, need a pile of greens. Pellets stay in the diet and stop being the only diet. Calcium comes from a cuttlebone in the water, a calcium dust on insects, and the UVB that lets the turtle use that calcium. Without UVB, the dust sits unused and the shell softens.

Feed aquatic turtles in a separate bin if you can. It keeps the swimming water cleaner. If you feed in the tank, remove leftovers promptly. Feeder fish from a pet store often carry parasites and should not be the staple. Box turtles eat a mix of insects, worms, and plant matter, with fruit as a small portion. A strawberry every day and no worms is a thin, sweet-fed turtle. Vitamin A deficiency, the swollen-eyes problem, follows a pellet-only or meat-only menu that never includes orange vegetables and dark greens.

Portion size is what the turtle finishes in a few minutes, several times a week for adults and more often for juveniles. A begging slider is a poor hunger gauge. They will eat until the water is soup. Weigh or at least photograph the shell from above every couple of months so you notice a pyramid forming or a waist that disappears into fat rolls around the limbs.

Health and Veterinary

Shell rot, respiratory infection, vitamin A deficiency, and metabolic bone disease are the four problems this husbandry is built to prevent. Shell rot looks like soft, smelly, pitted areas and follows dirty water or a wound. Respiratory infection looks like a tilt while swimming, bubbles from the nose, or a turtle that cannot submerge. Swollen, shut eyes suggest vitamin A trouble or poor water. A soft shell suggests missing UVB, missing calcium, or a diet of only protein. All four of those signs belong with a veterinarian who sees reptiles, the same week, with a water test and a photo of the basking dock.

Turtles carry salmonella. Wash hands after every water change, keep the tank out of the kitchen, and do not let a child kiss the shell. That risk is managed with hygiene. It is a reason for a routine, and it is also why very young children and immunocompromised people need a serious conversation before a slider comes home. Annual exams catch beak overgrowth, ear abscesses, and the early shell disease that owners call "hard water stains."

Quarantine new turtles. Respiratory disease and parasites move with animals that shared a shop tub. Never release a pet turtle outdoors. It is illegal in many places and it harms wild populations. If you cannot keep an adult slider, contact a reptile rescue that accepts turtles.

Water and Light Checklist

  • Test ammonia and nitrite and keep both at zero with a filter rated well above the tank size.
  • Change a large portion of water weekly, and more often if you feed in the swimming area.
  • Confirm the dock is fully dry and the shell surface reaches the mid 80s to about 90 F.
  • Run UVB over that dock for the day length the bulb chart expects, then give the turtle a dark night.
  • Offer greens and pellets on a schedule that matches the turtle's age.

A healthy slider basks with all four feet tucked or sprawled, dives when you approach, and has clear eyes and a firm shell. One that lists, gasps at the surface, or stays in the water under a cold lamp needs the equipment checked immediately and a clinic if the posture does not improve once the dock is actually warm. These animals outlive casual setups. Plan for decades.

Dock, Filter, and a Shell That Stays Firm

Look at an aquatic turtle from above once a week. Clear eyes, a firm shell, and a dock that is actually dry are the three checks that prevent the classic failures. The basking surface should read about 85 to 90 F with an infrared thermometer, under a linear UVB tube at the distance on the bulb chart. Water for a slider often sits in the mid 70s F. The filter should be rated for far more than the tank, because the animal eats and defecates in the same pool. Change a large volume of water every week and test ammonia and nitrite, which belong at zero.

Feed greens as the turtle grows, and keep pellets and insects in the rotation without letting protein become the only menu. Swollen eyes and a soft shell are veterinary problems, often tied to vitamin A, calcium, UVB, or filthy water. Wash your hands. Salmonella is managed by hygiene and by keeping the tank out of the kitchen. Box turtles need land, a humid hide, and UVB, and they suffer in a slider aquarium. Know which animal you have, plan for a much larger tank as the shell grows, and never release a pet turtle outside. A reptile veterinarian who sees chelonians should meet the animal before a respiratory tilt becomes the first introduction.

Quick Answers

Are turtles easy to care for?

Aquatic turtles such as red-eared sliders need a large filtered tank, a dry basking dock around 85 to 90 F, UVB, and a diet that shifts toward greens as they grow. They are a poor match for a small unfiltered tub.

How much does pet turtle care cost?

Filter, tank, and basking lamp for a slider are a serious startup and grow again when the shell outgrows the first aquarium. The UVB tube over the basking dock should be dated and replaced at one year, which is Zoo Med's instruction (zoomed.com). October 2026 prices for a 22-inch T5 HO 5.0 tube were about $22 to $26. Electricity and pellets continue, and a reptile exam is about $95 to $185 before tests on the 2026 exotic-clinic roundup (findanexoticvet.com).
