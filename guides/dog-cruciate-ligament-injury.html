ACL/Cruciate Ligament Injury in Dogs

Guide to cranial cruciate ligament (CCL/ACL) tears in dogs. Covers symptoms, surgical options (TPLO, TTA), recovery timelines, and costs.

What a cruciate tear is

The cranial cruciate ligament is the band inside the knee that stops the shin bone from sliding forward and that limits rotation. People know the human version as the ACL. In dogs the injury is usually a degenerative fray that finally gives way, sometimes during an ordinary step, sometimes during a turn. A partial tear leaves some fibers. A complete tear leaves the knee unstable. The medial meniscus, a cartilage pad in the joint, often tears at the same time or later, and that tear is what produces a click and a sharper pain. The ligament does not knit itself back into a tight band. Surgery and rehab change the mechanics of the knee. They do not regrow the original ligament.

Because the failure is often degenerative, the opposite knee is at real risk over the next few years. Teaching-hospital orthopedic series find that a large share of dogs tear the second cruciate within that window. Weight, straight-legged conformation, and an early neuter have all been discussed as contributors. A single athletic injury can still be the moment the owner notices.

Which dogs tear it

Any size of dog can tear a cranial cruciate ligament. The surgeries fill up with Labrador Retrievers, Golden Retrievers, Rottweilers, Newfoundlands, Staffordshire Terriers, and other medium and large dogs, especially if they are overweight. Small dogs tear the ligament too, and some of them stay functional with a conservative plan. Young dogs with a straight rear and a steep tibial slope are set up for the injury. Older dogs tear a ligament that has been weakening for years. A sudden limp after a fall off a deck can be a cruciate tear, and it can also be a fracture or a luxating patella. The exam has to sort those before anyone promises a brace will do.

Signs owners notice

The usual story is a sudden hind-limb lameness. The dog holds the leg up, then toe-touches, then looks better for a few days, then worsens after a play session. Sitting with the affected leg kicked out to the side, the "sit test," is a clue. The knee may feel thickened on the inside, a medial buttress of scar, if the tear is weeks old. A click during walking suggests a meniscal tear. Muscle on that thigh shrinks if the dog has been favoring the leg. Some dogs are only stiff after rest and then warm out of it, which is easy to call arthritis and miss the instability underneath. A dog who cannot use either back leg needs a spinal emergency exam. That visit is separate from a routine cruciate appointment, and the pattern stays a spinal emergency until a neurologist says otherwise.

  • Sudden lameness in one hind leg, often worse after rest.
  • Toe-touching, or a short stride that never returns to normal.
  • Sitting with one leg angled out.
  • A click, or a sharper yelp, days to weeks after the first limp.
  • Thigh muscle loss on the injured side when you compare the two legs.

How a veterinarian diagnoses it

A cranial cruciate ligament tear is diagnosed on the orthopedic exam. Cranial drawer and tibial thrust are the maneuvers. Radiographs look for arthritis and for other bone disease, and they do not show the ligament itself. The hands find the tear. The films plan the surgery and rule out a bone tumor or a fracture. Sedation is sometimes required before the drawer test is trustworthy.

Cranial drawer is a forward slide of the tibia relative to the femur. Tibial thrust, or a tibial compression test, produces that slide when the hock is flexed. A tense, painful dog can hide a partial tear, so sedation or anesthesia is used when the awake test is equivocal and the history still fits. Radiographs of the knee often show fluid in the joint and, later, arthritis on the downhill side of the femur and tibia. They also look for osteosarcoma and for a fracture of the tibial crest, which can mimic a knee injury in a young dog. MRI and arthroscopy are available at some hospitals and are uncommon as the first test, because the hands and the films already answer the surgical question for most complete tears.

Treatment categories

A partial tear in a small, quiet dog is sometimes managed with rest, weight loss, and pain control the veterinarian doses. A complete tear, especially in a medium or large dog, is usually stabilized with surgery. Tibial plateau leveling, tibial tuberosity advancement, and a lateral suture are different procedures for that tear. The surgeon chooses among them. Pain medication without a stability plan does not rebuild the ligament.

Tibial plateau leveling osteotomy, TPLO, cuts the tibia and rotates the plateau so the knee no longer relies on the missing ligament during weight bearing. A plate holds the cut while bone heals. Tibial tuberosity advancement, TTA, moves the patellar tendon's attachment forward to change the same forces. An extracapsular lateral suture, sometimes called a lateral fabellar suture, places a heavy line outside the joint to limit the slide, and it is used more often in smaller dogs. Each procedure has a different incision, implant, and activity timeline. Meniscal inspection or release is part of many of these operations. A brace is an occasional compromise for a dog who cannot undergo anesthesia, and it is a weak substitute for a stable knee in a young athletic dog. Rehabilitation, weight loss, and leash-only activity are part of every plan, surgical or not. The anti-inflammatory and the other pain drugs are chosen by the veterinarian and stopped or changed if the dog vomits or seems dull.

Home care

After surgery, or during a conservative trial, the dog lives on a leash. No ball, no dog-park sprint, no stairs if you can carry a small dog or use a ramp. The typical restricted period is measured in weeks to a few months and is written on the discharge sheet, with recheck radiographs for osteotomy procedures to confirm the bone is healing before activity increases. Use a sling under the belly on slick floors for a large dog in the first days. Keep the incision dry and stop licking with a collar or a suit. Ice and passive range of motion only if the rehab plan includes them. Weight loss, if the dog is heavy, is treatment. Every extra kilogram stays on the repaired knee and on the opposite one, which may already be fraying. Call if the leg suddenly becomes non-weight-bearing again after a period of improvement. That can be a meniscal tear or an implant problem.

When it is an emergency

When to See the Vet Immediately

A dog who will not bear any weight after a trauma, a dog with a wound over the knee, or a dog whose leg sits at a wrong angle should be seen the same day to separate a fracture or a dislocation from a ligament tear. A dog who cannot get up on either hind leg, or who is dragging the back end, needs an emergency neurologic exam for disc disease. Do not wait a week on crate rest you invented for a "pulled muscle" if the dog is paralyzed. A stable toe-touching limp can be seen by your regular veterinarian within a day or two, and sooner if the dog is crying at rest. Human pain tablets are a poor bridge. Several are poisonous to dogs.

What to ask about the bill

A ruptured cranial cruciate ligament does not heal back into a tight band. TPLO, TTA, and extracapsular suture change the mechanics of the knee. They do not regrow the ligament. Teaching-hospital orthopedic series find that a large share of dogs tear the opposite cruciate within a few years. Weight loss and rehab improve how the knee is used.

Ask the surgeon to price this knee and to say, in the same note, what the other knee would cost if it tears later. A TPLO quote should include the plate, anesthesia, radiographs, and the rehab visits. An extracapsular suture is a different operation with a lower fee. Compare the written plans.

Key Questions

Is a cruciate tear the same as an ACL tear?

It is the closest equivalent. The cranial cruciate ligament in the dog does the job of the anterior cruciate ligament in people. The way dogs tear it is usually a slow degeneration that finishes in one bad step, which is different from many human sports injuries. The name on the discharge paper may say CCL or cranial cruciate. Owners who search for ACL are looking at the same joint problem.

Which surgery is the right one?

The surgeon matches the procedure to the dog's size, tibial slope, activity, and the clinic's experience. TPLO and TTA are osteotomies used widely in medium and large dogs. A lateral suture is often chosen for smaller, lighter dogs. No page can rank those for an individual knee. Ask what the written plan includes, how activity returns, and what the opposite knee will cost if it fails later. Radiographs after an osteotomy confirm healing before the dog is allowed to run.

Can rest fix a complete tear in a large dog?

Rest, weight loss, and pain control sometimes carry a small, quiet dog with a partial tear. A complete tear in a medium or large dog usually stays unstable, and the arthritis progresses while the dog is "getting by." Surgery changes the mechanics so the knee can bear weight without that ligament. A brace and pain medicine can be a compromise when anesthesia is unsafe. They do not rebuild the band that tore.
