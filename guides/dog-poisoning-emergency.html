Dog Poisoning Emergency

What to do if your dog eats something toxic. Covers when to induce vomiting, when not to, poison control numbers, and emergency vet protocols.

The first minutes

If you think your dog ate or rolled in something toxic, separate the dog from the source and keep other pets out of the room. Look in the mouth only if you can do it safely. Wipe visible powder or liquid off the fur so grooming does not continue the exposure, using mild soap and water only if a professional has said that product can be washed. Put the package, the plant, or a photo in a bag. Note the time as closely as you can. A dog who is seizuring, collapsing, struggling to breathe, or unconscious should be carried to a car and driven to an emergency clinic while another person calls ahead. Do not start home treatments on a dog who cannot swallow.

Call your veterinarian or the nearest emergency hospital. If you need a toxicologist, two services widely used in the United States are the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at 888-426-4435 and the Pet Poison Helpline at 855-764-7661. Have the product name, the active ingredients, an estimate of how much is missing, the dog's weight, the time, and the signs you see. A case number from a hotline lets the hospital continue the plan without starting from zero. Fees for those consults are real. So is the cost of guessing.

Vomiting is a medical decision

Do not induce vomiting unless a veterinarian or a poison hotline tells you to do it for this dog and this substance. Some ingestions are made worse by vomiting. Caustic products, including drain cleaner, oven cleaner, toilet bowl acid, and some batteries, burn the esophagus a second time on the way up. Petroleum products such as gasoline, kerosene, and lighter fluid can enter the lungs and cause aspiration pneumonia that outlasts the original exposure. Sharp objects, including bones, skewers, and broken ornaments, can lacerate tissue coming back up. A dog who is already sedated, tremoring, seizuring, or too weak to stand can inhale the vomit. Brachycephalic dogs, including bulldogs, pugs, and French bulldogs, have a higher aspiration risk and usually need the procedure done in a clinic with airway control.

When a clinician does clear vomiting, they will name the method and the amount for that dog. Do not invent a volume from an old blog. Do not use table salt, mustard, or syrup of ipecac. Salt can cause dangerous sodium changes and seizures. Ipecac is a poor and sometimes harmful choice in dogs. If the instruction was for a specific approach and nothing happens, call back before you repeat anything. Collect what comes up in a bag if they want to see it. Then follow the next instruction, which may be "come in now" even after the dog vomits. Vomiting removes some material and still leaves work for the clinic.

Timing clues that change the plan

Different toxins declare themselves on different clocks, which is why "he looks fine" is a weak reason to wait. Xylitol can drop blood sugar within a short time and can injure the liver later. Chocolate and caffeine often cause vomiting and restlessness over several hours, and theobromine lingers. Grapes and raisins may show kidney injury a day or two later, and any amount should be reported. Anticoagulant rat poisons can sit quietly for days before bleeding starts. Bromethalin rat poison affects the nervous system and does not respond to vitamin K. Cholecalciferol rat poison raises calcium and damages kidneys. Ethylene glycol antifreeze causes a drunken phase, a quieter gap, and then kidney failure, and the antidote window is early. Onions and garlic damage red cells over several days. Sago palm seeds injure the liver. THC edibles cause a wobbly, drowsy dog and may hide chocolate or xylitol in the same bite.

You do not need to diagnose the clock. You need to name the substance so the clinic can. If the trash held several items, list all of them. Trail mix, a purse, and a weekly pill box are combination scenes. Bring every bottle. A hotline that hears only "rat poison" cannot tell anticoagulant from bromethalin from cholecalciferol, and those three treatments diverge. The package is the difference between the right blood test and the wrong one.

What the emergency hospital may do

If the ingestion is recent and the dog is stable enough, the team may induce vomiting under their control or, less often, empty the stomach another way. Activated charcoal is sometimes given to bind certain drugs and toxins still in the gut. It is a poor match for some substances, including ethylene glycol, xylitol, and heavy metals, so the clinician chooses. Intravenous fluids support blood pressure and the kidneys. Bloodwork at arrival and again later tracks liver, kidney, blood sugar, and electrolytes. Specific antidotes exist for some problems: vitamin K1 for anticoagulant rodenticides, hospital drugs for acetaminophen, fomepizole or ethanol protocols for ethylene glycol, and other targeted care for seizures or heart rhythm changes. Those are veterinary procedures. A home cabinet is the wrong place to assemble them.

Ask, before you leave, what signs mean a return trip and when the next lab test is. A dog sent home after chocolate or after a hotline calculation still needs a watcher overnight. A dog sent home after an anticoagulant exposure may need a prescription and a recheck of clotting. Write the instructions down. Stress makes memory slippery. If cost is a barrier, say so early. Clinics can sometimes stage care, and delaying the conversation until the dog is critical removes options. Pet insurance and payment plans are administrative. They should be mentioned. They should not stall the first phone call.

Mistakes that cost time

Waiting for a seizure or for pale gums is the most expensive mistake, because several important toxins are quiet at first. Inducing vomiting for a caustic, a petroleum product, or a sedated dog is the second. Feeding milk, oil, or bread to "soak it up" usually fails to bind the toxin and fills the stomach before charcoal or imaging. Forgetting the package forces the team to guess among lookalike pills. Punishing the dog, or a child who confesses, teaches everyone to hide the next event. The confession is the useful part. Thank the child and call.

Another mistake is treating a dog advice sheet as if it applied to a cat in the house. Cats are more vulnerable to lilies, acetaminophen, concentrated permethrin, and many essential oils. A dog who ate a lily leaf and a cat who ate a lily leaf are different emergencies. Say the species out loud. If both animals had access, make that clear. Finally, do not post the case on social media and wait for comments. Comments disagree. The hotline does not.

A kit and a habit before you need them

Save both hotline numbers in your phone today. Tape the emergency clinic address inside a cabinet. Keep a small folder for product photos you take when you buy rodent bait, antifreeze, or a new cleaner, so the label still exists if the bottle is destroyed. Latch cabinets. Hang purses. Read peanut butter and gum labels for xylitol before they enter the house. Walk the yard for sago palms, mushroom flushes, and slug bait after rain. Tell pet sitters the clinic name and the rule: call first, bring the package, and skip home vomiting unless a professional directs it.

When the event happens anyway, your job is short. Remove the source, collect the label, call, and transport if they say transport. Stay on the line long enough to hear whether vomiting is forbidden. That single answer prevents a large share of the harm owners add with good intentions. The rest of the care is medicine, done by people who can monitor a heart and a kidney. Your speed is what gets the dog into that room while the toxin is still in the stomach or still early in the blood.

  • Separate the dog, save the package, and note the time.
  • Call a veterinarian or a poison hotline before home remedies.
  • Do not induce vomiting for caustics, petroleum, sharp objects, or a dog who is sedated or seizuring.
  • Do not use salt, mustard, or ipecac.
  • Name every ingredient if several toxins were in the same meal or purse.
  • Expect different clocks: fast blood-sugar drops, delayed bleeding, delayed kidney or liver injury.
  • Ask when to recheck labs before you leave the hospital.
  • Store the hotline numbers and the clinic address before an emergency.

Questions for a poisoning emergency

Who should I call if my dog ate something toxic?

Your veterinarian or an emergency clinic, plus a poison hotline if they want a toxicologist. In the United States, ASPCA Animal Poison Control is 888-426-4435 and Pet Poison Helpline is 855-764-7661. Have the label, the time, the dog's weight, and the signs ready. Bring the package with you.

When is it dangerous to make a dog vomit?

When the substance is caustic, petroleum-based, or sharp, and when the dog is seizuring, sedated, or a short-faced breed with a high risk of inhaling vomit. Do not induce vomiting unless a veterinarian or poison hotline clears it for this dog. Skip salt, mustard, and ipecac entirely.

Why call if my dog still looks normal?

Xylitol can drop blood sugar quickly, while anticoagulant bait, grapes, onions, sago palm, and antifreeze may injure organs after a quiet delay. Looking bright does not clear the exposure. The label tells the clinic which clock you are on and whether an antidote exists.
