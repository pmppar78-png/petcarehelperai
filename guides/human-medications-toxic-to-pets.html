Human Medications Toxic

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Common human medications dangerous to dogs and cats including ibuprofen, acetaminophen, antidepressants, and ADHD drugs. Emergency response guide.

Human pills are a leading pet poisoning

Dogs eat dropped tablets because they taste like a treat, and cats are exposed when a pill sticks to a paw or when a flavored human medication is left on a counter. The same milligram that is routine for an adult person can injure a pet because body size differs and because pets lack some of the liver pathways people use. This page names the common groups and the kind of harm they cause. Tablet counts and strengths belong to the clinician on the phone. Any swallowed human medication should be discussed with a veterinarian or a poison hotline, with the bottle in your hand.

Move the pet away from the spill, count the pills if you can do it quickly, and put the bottle in a bag. Phone your veterinarian, an emergency hospital, or a poison hotline about the pills. Read the generic name, the strength printed on the tablet or bottle, and the time. Wait for the medication consult before any attempt to make the pet vomit. Some drugs make vomiting dangerous, and a pet who is already sedated can inhale the vomit. Do not give a second human drug, such as a pain tablet, while you wait.

Pain tablets: NSAIDs and acetaminophen

Ibuprofen and naproxen are nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. In dogs and cats they can ulcerate the stomach and injure the kidneys. Signs include vomiting, sometimes with blood, black stool, a painful belly, thirst, and lethargy. Kidney failure can follow. Cats are particularly vulnerable. A single dropped tablet is enough to justify the call. Do not offer these drugs for a limp, a fever, or a toothache. Veterinary pain medicine is a different set of products chosen for the species.

Acetaminophen is processed poorly by cats and can also harm dogs. In cats it damages red blood cells, causing chocolate-brown gums, swelling of the face or paws, weakness, and breathing difficulty, and it injures the liver. Dogs can develop liver injury and red-cell damage as well. There is a hospital antidote used for acetaminophen. It has to be given by a veterinary team, on time, and it is one more reason to travel quickly with the bottle. Combination cold medicines often contain acetaminophen plus a decongestant or an antihistamine. Read every active ingredient out loud. The decongestant can cause agitation and a fast heart on top of the acetaminophen problem.

Antidepressants, ADHD medicines, and blood pressure pills

Antidepressants, including many SSRIs and SNRIs, can cause agitation, vomiting, tremors, a high heart rate, and serotonin syndrome: hyperthermia, rigidity, and seizures. Sleep medicines and benzodiazepines cause profound sedation and a wobbly walk. Some are more dangerous when the pet is a cat or a small dog. ADHD stimulant tablets cause hyperactivity, a racing heart, tremors, and hyperthermia. Blood pressure and heart medicines, including beta blockers and calcium channel blockers, can drop heart rate and blood pressure to dangerous levels. A chewable or a patch left on a bedside table is a common story. Bring the patch if the pet may have eaten one. A used patch can still hold drug.

Vitamin D supplements and some psoriasis creams drive calcium up and injure kidneys, a pattern similar in spirit to cholecalciferol rodenticide. Iron tablets cause severe gut injury and liver damage. Thyroid replacement tablets, in a large ingestion, can cause agitation and a fast heart. Birth-control packets are often a gut upset and a foreign-body problem from the plastic case, and you should still call because some hormones and some packets include other drugs. When several drugs were in the same weekly organizer, bring the organizer and list every name. The hotline would rather sort a full list than guess from a single pill color.

Prevention that matches real kitchens

Weekly pill boxes tip over. Store them in a cabinet with a latch, and sweep the floor after you fill them. Purses and lunch bags hide ibuprofen. Hang them up. Do not set a pill on a piece of cheese "for later." Cats walk through bathrooms and lick sinks. Rinse the sink if a tablet dissolved there. Guests need a sentence: human medicine stays zipped, and a dropped pill is announced immediately. A dog who is trained to catch treats will catch a tablet you fumble. Close the mouth-targeting game when you are handling medicine.

If exposure happens at night, go to an emergency hospital when the hotline or your clinic says to go. Waiting until morning is how ulcers and liver injury get a head start. Ask what signs should change the plan: vomiting blood, tremors, collapse, brown gums, or a seizure. Write them down. Keep the bottle even if it is empty, because the imprint code on a tablet can identify the drug when the label was destroyed. Prevention and speed are the whole strategy. There is no list of human pain tablets that are handy for pets.

  • Call a veterinarian or poison hotline for any swallowed human medication and bring the bottle.
  • Treat ibuprofen and naproxen as stomach-ulcer and kidney risks in dogs and cats.
  • Treat acetaminophen as a red-cell and liver emergency, especially in cats.
  • Expect agitation or serotonin signs from many antidepressants, and a racing heart from ADHD stimulants.
  • Treat blood pressure and heart pills as causes of a dangerous drop in heart rate.
  • Do not induce vomiting unless you are told to.
  • Latch the pill box, hang purses, and announce dropped tablets.
  • Skip human pain medicine for limps and fevers. Ask the clinic for a species-appropriate plan.

Organizers, patches, and night spills

Weekly pill organizers spill into a pattern of several drugs at once. Bring the organizer and list every compartment that is empty or scattered. A single chewed tablet may be identifiable by the imprint code if you still have a sister tablet from the bottle. Used pain patches and nicotine patches still contain drug. If a patch is missing from an arm or a bathroom shelf, tell the clinic and search the dog's mouth and bedding. Chewable vitamins and flavored human medicines are more likely to be finished than a bitter tablet. Count them.

At night, go to the emergency hospital when the hotline says the drug can ulcerate the gut, drop blood sugar, slow the heart, or damage red cells. Waiting for morning office hours is how some of those injuries deepen. Ask what vomiting blood, brown gums, tremors, or collapse should change. Latch the cabinet before you sleep, even if you are exhausted. A second pet often finds what the first pet scattered. Sweep with a flashlight, including under the bed.

Questions about human medications

Which human pain tablets are especially dangerous?

Ibuprofen and naproxen can ulcerate the gut and damage kidneys in dogs and cats. Acetaminophen can damage red blood cells and the liver, and cats are particularly sensitive. Call a veterinarian or poison hotline for any exposure and bring the bottle. Do not give these drugs on purpose.

What about antidepressants or ADHD medicine?

Many antidepressants can cause agitation, tremors, and serotonin syndrome. Stimulant ADHD tablets can cause a racing heart, hyperactivity, and hyperthermia. Blood pressure pills can slow the heart severely. These are emergency calls. Do not induce vomiting unless a professional directs it.

What information should I have when I call?

The generic name, the strength on the label, how many tablets may be missing, the time, and the pet's approximate weight. If a weekly organizer spilled, list every drug in it. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center and Pet Poison Helpline can help your clinic plan care.
