Boston Terrier Health Issues

Common health problems in Boston Terriers including brachycephalic syndrome, cataracts, luxating patella. Prevention, symptoms to watch for, and treatment options.

Boston Terriers are predisposed to several health conditions including brachycephalic syndrome, cataracts, luxating patella. Understanding these risks allows you to screen early, prevent where possible, and catch problems before they become emergencies.

July noon is a bad hour for this short nose, so the long walk moves to dawn and the pavement gets a hand test first.

A red lump at the inner corner of the eye is how cherry eye introduces itself, usually in the juvenile months. The gland that belongs under the third eyelid pops forward and stays angry. Rubbing the face along the rug makes the lump angrier. I book the doctor who handles eyes, and I ask about tucking that gland back, because it helps produce tears. Taking it out and hoping the eye stays comfortable is a plan I want said out loud before any steel is used.

Juvenile cataracts cloud the lens early. A puppy who misses a treat tossed across a dim kitchen, or whose pupil looks smoky, needs an ophthalmologist. I want that exam during the growing year and again if the eye changes. Luxating patellas read as a hop, a hind foot held clear, then a trot that looks ordinary. A kneecap grade belongs on paper. Hemivertebrae turn up in some lines. Wedge-shaped vertebrae, often near the twist of the tail, can leave a dog sore along the back or unsteady behind. Once the knees are cleared and the rear gait still looks neurologic, spinal films are the pictures I request.

The muzzle is short and the skull is brachycephalic, so heat is a stress I plan around. Noisy breathing, a tongue that stays wide, a player who plants all four feet in the sun: that dog comes indoors. Facial wrinkles have to be kept dry. A damp fold grows yeast, and then he scrubs his face on the carpet until skin splits. I dry the fold after water and after meals with a cotton round, and I show the clean cotton so the job has a picture attached to it.

Weight splits into three classes, with a ceiling of 25 pounds. The light class is under 15. A dog at 12 pounds lives in it. Middle is 15 and under 20. The heavy class runs from 20 up to 25. Crossing 25 puts the airway and the knees under a load they were not drawn for. Years I quote for this breed often sit from 11 through 13. Inside that stretch I book knees and eyes by year 3, and I look at the eyes again around year 5 when the first certificate was clean. Tuxedo markings, black laid over white, change where dirt shows. They do not change which tests I ask for.

The CHIC patella guidance for small breeds is the orthopedic frame I open when the hop shows up. I keep the eye note in the same folder, because a clear knee and a cloudy lens are two different afternoons at the clinic.

Brachycephalic Airway Considerations

As a brachycephalic (flat-faced) breed, the Boston Terrier requires special attention to respiratory health. The shortened skull structure that gives the breed its distinctive appearance also narrows the airways, making breathing more labored — particularly during exercise, in warm weather, or under anesthesia. The Brachycephalic Obstructive Airway Syndrome (BOAS) spectrum ranges from mild snoring to life-threatening respiratory distress. Veterinary assessment using the BOAS grading scale (Grade 0-III) helps determine whether surgical intervention such as nares widening or soft palate resection may improve quality of life. Owners should monitor for exercise intolerance, cyanosis (blue-tinged gums), and sleep apnea patterns.
