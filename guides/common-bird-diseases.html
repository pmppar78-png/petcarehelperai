Common Pet Bird Diseases

Guide to common diseases in pet birds including PBFD, psittacosis, aspergillosis, and avian flu. Covers symptoms, treatment, and prevention.

Start with an avian veterinarian

A parrot, a cockatiel, a budgie, or a finch who fluffs and stops eating needs a veterinarian who treats birds. A canine vaccine series, a kitten booster calendar, and the dewormer dosed for a puppy have no role in that appointment. Birds hide illness until they are weak, and a gram-scale weight taken at the same time each week is often the first honest number you will own. Weigh in the morning, before feeding if that is your habit, and write it down. A drop of a few grams in a budgie, or a steady slide in a cockatoo, is a reason to call an avian veterinarian while the bird is still perching.

The exam includes that weight, the choana in the roof of the mouth, the vent, the feathers, and the breathing. Tests are picked for the disease in front of the bird: a chlamydia test, a circovirus test for beak and feather disease, a polyomavirus test in a young bird, a fecal exam, and radiographs when metal or air-sac disease is the question. Tell the clinic about every new bird, every cheap toy, and every person in the house who has a fever or a cough.

Psittacine beak and feather disease

Psittacine beak and feather disease is caused by a circovirus. Young birds may show abnormal feathers that break, retain sheaths, or grow in the wrong color, and the beak can become soft, overgrown, or necrotic. Older birds sometimes show a slower feather dystrophy or a failing immune system with repeated infections. Some infected birds shed virus and look fairly normal for a long time, which is why a test before you add a bird to an aviary matters. There is no drug that clears the virus. Care is supportive: nutrition, treatment of secondary infections the avian veterinarian identifies, pain control when the beak is diseased, and isolation from birds who have tested negative. Feather dust and feces carry the virus. Hand-washing, separate air space when you can manage it, and no shared perches between a positive bird and a breeding pair are the practical controls. A positive result in a single pet who already lives alone is a long conversation about quality of life, and the avian veterinarian should lead it.

Polyomavirus in young birds

Avian polyomavirus hits nestlings and fledglings hardest. Budgerigar breeders know an acute form that kills chicks, sometimes with abdominal swelling, bleeding under the skin, and delayed feather growth. Other psittacines can die suddenly, stop emptying the crop, or show similar bruising. Adults may carry the virus with milder or absent signs and still contaminate a nursery. A breeder who has had a run of dead chicks should stop selling and have an avian veterinarian run the right PCR and give a hygiene plan between clutches. A vaccine exists for use in some breeding situations. Whether it belongs in a given aviary is the avian veterinarian's decision. It is unrelated to any dog or cat vaccine schedule, and a pet-store puppy shot does not protect a clutch.

Chlamydiosis, also called psittacosis

Chlamydia psittaci causes chlamydiosis in birds and a flu-like illness, sometimes pneumonia, in people. Birds may be lethargic, thin, and fluffed, with nasal discharge, labored breathing, or droppings that turn bright green. Some birds shed the organism and look only a little off. Because people can get sick, this diagnosis is also a household health issue. Tell your physician if you develop fever or a cough and a bird in the home has been ill. The avian veterinarian chooses the antibiotic and the length of treatment. Do not split a human prescription into a seed bowl, and do not stop early because the bird perked up. Public-health rules in some states ask veterinarians to report positive cases. Cleaning during treatment should avoid blowing dried droppings into the air. A mask and damp wiping are safer than dry sweeping.

Egg binding

Egg binding is an emergency in a hen. She may sit on the cage floor, strain, hold a wide stance, bob her tail, look swollen in the abdomen, and stop passing droppings. Chronic layers, birds on poor diets, and hens who have never been given enough calcium are at higher risk, and even a well-fed hen can bind. Put her in a warm, quiet, dim carrier and go to an avian veterinarian or an emergency clinic that sees birds. Do not press on the belly, and do not try to pull a visible egg. Calcium, fluids, lubrication, and sometimes surgery are decisions made after an exam and often a radiograph, because a crushed egg in the coelom is worse than the delay of a careful plan. After she is stable, ask about the laying cycle. Environmental changes, diet, and, when the veterinarian advises it, a hormone implant or other medical control can reduce the next clutch. A seed-only diet is a poor foundation for a hen who lays.

Heavy metal

Lead and zinc poisoning still show up in pet birds. Sources include bells with lead clappers, galvanized wire or clips, some cheap cage paints, costume jewelry, stained glass, curtain weights, and United States pennies minted from 1983 onward, which are mostly zinc. A bird may become weak, regurgitate, pass loose droppings, seizure, or, in some species, produce red urine. Radiographs can show metal densities in the gastrointestinal tract. Removal and chelation are avian-veterinary procedures. Do not make a bird vomit at home. While you travel, take the suspect toy or the cage photo with you. Prevention is boring and effective: stainless or nickel-plated hardware from a known bird supplier, no access to remodeling dust, and a habit of picking jewelry up off the floor.

Aspergillosis and avian influenza, briefly

Aspergillosis is a fungal disease, often of the airway and air sacs. African greys, amazons, and macaws appear in case series, especially when vitamin A is lacking, the room is dusty, or the bird has been stressed by another illness. The voice may change, breathing becomes a tail bob after mild effort, and weight falls. Diagnosis can require radiographs, endoscopy, or lab tests the avian veterinarian chooses, and treatment lasts weeks to months. It is a fungus in the environment, so a single antibiotic for bacteria will not be the whole plan.

Avian influenza is a flock and public-health concern when wild birds are dying in your region. Keep pet birds indoors, away from wild waterfowl, their droppings, and outdoor aviaries that wild birds can visit. Do not bring a sick wild bird into the house to nurse it beside a cockatiel. If you suspect influenza, call an avian veterinarian and follow local animal-health instructions before you walk into a waiting room full of other birds. A dog vaccine visit is the wrong appointment for that question.

  • Weigh birds weekly on a gram scale and call an avian veterinarian when the number slides.
  • Test new psittacines for beak and feather disease and discuss polyomavirus if you breed.
  • Treat chlamydiosis as a risk to people as well as to the flock, with the drug the avian veterinarian selects.
  • Carry an egg-bound hen in a warm quiet box the same day, without pushing on her abdomen.
  • Retire galvanized clips, lead-weighted toys, and loose pennies.

Same-day avian care

Labored breathing, bleeding, a seizure, egg binding, a bite from a dog or cat, or a bird on the cage floor who cannot perch should be seen the same day by an avian veterinarian or a clinic that agrees to see birds. Call on the way. Keep the carrier warm, dark, and stable. Do not offer over-the-counter pain tablets made for people or for dogs.

What avian care costs

An avian exam is often higher than a quick dog-vaccine visit because the training and the time are different. PCR tests for chlamydia, circovirus, or polyomavirus each add a lab fee. Radiographs for metal or an egg, hospitalization, and chelation move the bill into a range that should be quoted before you approve it. Prevention, meaning a gram scale, a safe cage, and a quarantine period for new birds, is the smaller number. Ranges below are broad and move with the city. Questions bird owners ask

Who should treat a sick parrot?

An avian veterinarian should direct diagnosis and treatment. A canine or feline vaccine calendar cannot be copied onto a cockatiel, a macaw, or a finch. If your usual clinic does not see birds, ask them for the nearest avian practice or an emergency hospital that will accept a bird, and go there while the bird is still standing.

Can beak and feather disease be cleared with a medicine?

No drug clears psittacine beak and feather disease virus. The avian veterinarian treats secondary infections, supports nutrition, and helps you decide on isolation so other birds are protected. A test on new birds, before they share air with a collection, is the useful prevention. Polyomavirus is a separate virus and needs its own test, especially in a nursery.

What if I think my bird ate metal or is egg bound?

Both are same-day problems for an avian veterinarian. Egg binding looks like straining, a wide stance, tail bobbing, and a hen on the floor. Heavy metal can look like sudden weakness, regurgitation, or seizures after access to galvanized parts, bells, or pennies. Do not press on an egg and do not induce vomiting. Carry the suspect object or a photo of the cage with you.
