Pet Drowning and Water Safety

Emergency response for pet drowning, CPR for dogs and cats, pool safety, and water hazards every pet owner should know about.

Overview

Emergency Situation

If your pet is in immediate danger, call your nearest emergency veterinary hospital right now. This guide provides first aid information but is not a substitute for professional emergency veterinary care.

"Drowning" vs Submersion Injury — Why the Terminology Matters

The WHO and the Utstein drowning consensus updated the language in 2015: drowning is "the process of experiencing respiratory impairment from submersion in liquid." Outcomes are no longer labeled "wet," "dry," "secondary," or "near" drowning; they are drowning events that resolve, require resuscitation, or result in death. Why this matters for you: a pet pulled from the water and breathing looks fine but can develop life-threatening pulmonary edema 4–24 hours later. Every submersion event needs a vet check, period.

Species and Breed Risk Profile

  • Bulldogs, Pugs, Frenchies, Bostons, Basset Hounds, Dachshunds — top-heavy, short-legged, or brachycephalic. Poor or dangerous swimmers. Flotation vests mandatory around water.
  • Deep-chested athletes (Greyhounds, Whippets) — low body fat, sink easily, tire fast.
  • Puppies and geriatric pets — weak, cold-susceptible, disorient easily.
  • Cats — able swimmers in theory, panic-driven drownings in pools and bathtubs in practice, especially Sphynx and hairless breeds that chill rapidly.
  • Retrievers and water dogs still drown — often from exhaustion, undertow, hypothermia in cold water, or cardiac event while swimming.

The First 60 Seconds After Removal From Water

  1. Get the pet out safely — your safety first. Pool edges are slippery and a panicked dog will climb you. Use a skimmer pole, leash-lasso, or the shepherd's-crook pool hook to reach them without entering the water when possible.
  2. Clear the airway. Lay the pet on their side, extend the neck, pull the tongue forward, and sweep the mouth clear of debris, vomit, or vegetation. Dogs and cats can aspirate pool chemicals, algae, or silt — all matter.
  3. Drain water from the airway — briefly. For small dogs and cats, hold them head-down by the hind legs for no more than 10–15 seconds, with a gentle shake. For large dogs, lift the hindquarters higher than the head and compress the chest side-to-side to drain passively. Old advice to "hang them upside down for a minute" is no longer recommended — it delays ventilation and can cause cardiac arrest.
  4. Check for breathing and pulse — watch the chest rise for up to 10 seconds; feel the femoral pulse (inside the thigh) simultaneously.

CPR for a Non-Breathing Pet (RECOVER 2024 Guidelines)

Per the Reassessment Campaign on Veterinary Resuscitation (RECOVER), drowning victims need combined ventilation-compression CPR, not compression-only:

  1. Lay the pet on their right side on a firm surface.
  2. Give 2 rescue breaths first — close the mouth, seal your mouth over the nostrils, and blow until you see the chest rise. This is critical for drowning patients because hypoxia, not cardiac arrest, is the driver.
  3. 30 chest compressions over the widest part of the chest (keel-down position for deep-chested dogs, over the heart behind the elbow for barrel-chested and small dogs). Compress to one-third to one-half the chest depth, at 100–120 per minute.
  4. Cycle 30 compressions : 2 breaths.
  5. Check for pulse and spontaneous breathing every 2 minutes (5 full cycles). If absent, continue until spontaneous return or arrival at the vet.
  6. Transport while ventilating — do not pause CPR to load the pet; one person drives, one performs CPR in the back seat.

Delayed Pulmonary Edema — The Reason All Events Need a Vet

Even if your pet is breathing normally and seems fine, fluid leaks into the alveoli for 4–24 hours after aspiration. Watch for: increased breathing effort, flared nostrils, rapid shallow respiration, productive or wet-sounding cough, pale or blue-tinged gums, restlessness, or a pet that would rather stand than lie down. If any of these appear in the 24 hours post-event, go to the ER immediately — the window to treat is narrow.

Water Type Changes the Problem

  • Freshwater (pool, lake, river): hypotonic — dilutes blood, damages alveolar surfactant, causes hemolysis at large-volume aspiration.
  • Saltwater: hypertonic — draws fluid into the lungs from the bloodstream. Pulmonary edema is often worse.
  • Pool water: chlorine and stabilizers irritate airways; algaecides can be toxic.
  • Stagnant pond/river: risk of leptospirosis, giardia, blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) toxicity. Cyanobacteria can kill within 30 minutes of ingestion.
  • Hot tub / Jacuzzi: hyperthermia on top of aspiration.

Hypothermia Protocol

Cold-water drowning causes rapid core cooling. If the pet's rectal temp is <99°F (37.2°C):

  • Remove wet fur contact — blot with dry towels, do not rub vigorously
  • Wrap in blankets; place warm (not hot) water bottles near (not touching) the skin with a towel barrier
  • Turn car heater up and aim vents at the pet
  • Do not immerse in warm water at home — rewarming too fast causes "afterdrop" and ventricular fibrillation
  • Let the ER do controlled rewarming with IV fluids and forced-air warmers

When to Skip First Aid and Drive

All submersion events require a vet visit. Go immediately, do not "observe at home," if:

  • Pet was unconscious, needed any rescue breathing, or required CPR
  • Coughing, wheezing, or any respiratory effort above normal
  • Gums pale, blue, gray, or muddy
  • Body temp below 99°F
  • Known exposure to blue-green algae or seizure after pond swim
  • Pet swallowed significant pool water — chlorine and cyanuric acid can cause GI upset
  • Submersion longer than 60 seconds, even if pet now seems fine

What the ER Will Do

  • Oxygen supplementation via flow-by, mask, or oxygen cage
  • Pulse oximetry and arterial blood gases to quantify hypoxia
  • Thoracic radiographs at admission and again at 6–12 hours — findings often lag clinical decline
  • IV fluids, cautiously — over-hydrating a patient with pulmonary edema worsens it; judicious rates are key
  • Bronchodilators (terbutaline, albuterol) and sometimes diuretics (furosemide) for severe aspiration pneumonitis
  • Antibiotics only if pneumonia develops — prophylactic antibiotics are not standard and breed resistance
  • Rewarming protocols for hypothermic patients
  • Minimum 12–24 hour observation for any symptomatic pet; 72 hours for severe cases

Typical cost: Asymptomatic event, 6-hour observation and radiographs: $400–$900. Moderate aspiration pneumonitis with oxygen therapy and overnight: $1,800–$4,000. Severe ARDS requiring ventilator support: $8,000–$20,000+.

Owner Mistakes

  • "He coughed it out — he's fine." Delayed pulmonary edema is the killer, not the initial submersion.
  • Hanging the dog upside down for 60+ seconds. Old advice; delays ventilation, causes cardiac strain.
  • Rubbing hypothermic pets vigorously to warm them. Causes peripheral vasodilation and cold-blood return to the core (afterdrop) — ventricular fibrillation risk.
  • Skipping the vet visit because the pet "recovered." Every drowning event is a vet visit.
  • Not putting a flotation vest on a brachycephalic dog around water. Single biggest preventable cause of pool drowning.

How do I know if it's a real emergency?

Any submersion is a vet visit. Breathing, coughing, altered gums, or hypothermia means go now; an apparently normal pet still needs examination within 4–12 hours for delayed pulmonary edema.

How much does an emergency vet visit cost?

Outpatient observation with radiographs: $400–$900. Overnight with oxygen therapy for aspiration pneumonitis: $1,800–$4,000. ICU with ventilator for ARDS: $8,000–$20,000+. Standard insurance accident coverage reimburses 70–90% after deductible.
