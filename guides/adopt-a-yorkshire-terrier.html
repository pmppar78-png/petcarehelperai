Finding a Yorkshire Terrier to Adopt

Yorkies are among the most commonly surrendered toy breeds. People fall for the tiny puppies without realizing how much grooming, dental care, and training a Yorkshire Terrier actually needs. Puppy mills also overproduce them, which means rescues always have Yorkies available.

Organizations like United Yorkie Rescue and Save a Yorkie Rescue place dogs across the country. Most foster their dogs in homes, so you will get detailed information about the dog's temperament, housetraining status, and how it handles being alone.

Genetic Health Considerations: The Yorkshire Terrier breed has documented susceptibility to dental disease, luxating patella, collapsed trachea. Awareness of these predispositions is valuable for two reasons: it guides preventive screening decisions, and it helps you recognize early symptoms that might otherwise be overlooked.

Breed-Specific Rescues

Breed rescues typically provide more background than shelters. They can tell you whether a Yorkie gets along with cats, handles children well, or has any resource guarding tendencies. At 4-7 pounds, a Yorkie that snaps can seem harmless, but it still needs proper behavior assessment before placement.

  • Size: small (4-7 lbs)
  • Energy Level: Moderate
  • Shedding: Minimal
  • Common Health Issues: Dental Disease, Luxating Patella, Collapsed Trachea
  • Lifespan: 11-15 yrs

Shelter Adoption

Shelters sometimes list Yorkies as "terrier mix" in their databases, so broaden your search terms. A shelter Yorkie adoption typically costs $100-$250, while breed rescues charge $300-$600, which usually covers dental cleaning, spay/neuter, and full vetting.

Ask specifically about dental history. Yorkies are prone to severe dental disease, and many rescue dogs need extractions before or shortly after adoption. Knowing the dental situation upfront helps you budget for any immediate care.

What to Expect

Despite their tiny size, Yorkies have bold terrier personalities. They were originally bred to catch rats in Yorkshire textile mills, and they still carry that feisty, confident attitude. They will alert-bark at everything, demand to be near you at all times, and try to boss around dogs ten times their size.

  • Provide 30–60 minutes of daily exercise appropriate to their energy level
  • Feed a high-quality diet formulated for small breed dogs (400–800 calories/day)
  • Maintain an occasional grooming routine
  • Schedule breed-appropriate health screenings for dental disease
  • Invest in pet insurance early to cover breed-specific conditions

Preparing Your Home

Yorkie-proof your home at floor level. Electrical cords, small objects they could swallow, and gaps behind furniture are all hazards for a dog this small. Their size also makes them vulnerable to being stepped on or sat on, so teach everyone in the household to check before sitting and to watch their feet.

If you have young children, supervise all interactions. A 5-pound dog can be seriously injured by a toddler who falls on it or grabs too hard.

Set up a grooming station from day one. Yorkshire Terrier coats mat quickly if neglected, and many rescue Yorkies arrive with coat in rough shape. Daily brushing keeps the silky coat manageable, or you can opt for a short "puppy cut" that requires less daily maintenance.

First Days Home

Keep everything low-key for the first week. Yorkies attach to people quickly but can be overwhelmed by too much stimulation at once. Give them a small, defined space with a bed, water, and a few toys. Let them expand their territory gradually as they gain confidence.

Housetraining is a common struggle with rescue Yorkies. Their tiny bladders mean they need to go out more frequently than larger dogs. Every two hours is a reasonable starting schedule, with immediate trips after meals and naps. Pee pads work well as a backup, especially in bad weather -- many Yorkies flatly refuse to go outside in rain.

Schedule a vet visit within the first week and ask about dental assessment, kneecap stability (luxating patella), and tracheal health. These are the three areas where Yorkies run into trouble most often, and getting a baseline now saves headaches later.

  • Veterinary care: $300–$700 annually for routine visits, plus potential emergency costs
