Colitis and IBD

Guide to inflammatory bowel disease and colitis in dogs. Covers chronic diarrhea, diagnostic workup, dietary trials, medications, and management.

What colitis and IBD mean

Colitis means inflammation of the large intestine. The stool comes often, in small amounts, with mucus, fresh blood, and straining. An acute bout can follow a dietary indiscretion, stress, or whipworms, and it may settle once the trigger and the parasites are handled. Inflammatory bowel disease is the older name for a chronic enteropathy: gastrointestinal signs that last more than about three weeks, with inflammation on biopsies, after parasites, infection, and a simple diet problem have been ruled out. Many dogs who get called "IBD" in the waiting room actually have food-responsive enteropathy and improve when the diet changes, without ever needing a biopsy.

Chronic enteropathy is sorted by what works. Food-responsive disease improves on a hydrolyzed or novel-protein diet. Antibiotic-responsive disease improves during a drug trial the veterinarian designs. Immunosuppressant-responsive disease needs a drug that damps the immune system after those simpler steps fail. Non-responsive disease keeps going and forces a harder look for lymphoma, a pancreatic problem, or a missed parasite. Boxers and French Bulldogs have a separate colon disease, granulomatous colitis, driven by an invasive E. coli in the colon wall. That one is an infection to culture, and steroids alone miss it.

Which dogs develop it

Acute colitis can hit any dog who raided the trash or picked up whipworm eggs in a shared yard. Chronic enteropathy is more often a middle-aged dog, and German Shepherds, Basenjis, Soft Coated Wheaten Terriers, Yorkshire Terriers, and Shar-Peis appear often in referral clinics. Basenjis can have a severe inherited enteropathy. Wheatens are watched for protein-losing disease. Boxers and French Bulldogs with chronic large-bowel diarrhea and weight loss should be considered for granulomatous colitis before they spend months on the wrong drug. A young dog with blood in the stool still needs a parvovirus test and a fecal. Age does not make diarrhea "just stress."

Protein-losing enteropathy is the severe end. The gut leaks albumin, the blood protein falls, and fluid may gather in the belly or under the skin. That pattern needs a faster workup than intermittent soft stool in a dog who is holding weight.

Signs owners notice

Large-bowel signs are urgency, straining, mucus, and fresh red blood on otherwise formed or soft stool, often with the dog still hungry. Small-bowel signs are weight loss, vomiting, a bigger volume of diarrhea, and sometimes black stool if blood is digested. Many dogs mix both. Borborygmi, flatulence, and a dull coat show up in chronic cases. A dog with low albumin may look pot-bellied from fluid, and the legs can swell. Sudden bloody diarrhea with vomiting and collapse belongs in a different conversation from a three-month history of soft stool. It can be acute hemorrhagic diarrhea syndrome, parvovirus, pancreatitis, or a foreign body.

  • Small, frequent stools with mucus and bright red blood.
  • Straining that looks like constipation while liquid stool is actually passing.
  • Vomiting, weight loss, and a ravenous or absent appetite in a longer illness.
  • Black, tarry stool or pale gums, which mean bleeding or protein and blood loss that cannot wait.
  • A swollen belly from fluid in a dog who has been losing weight.

How a veterinarian

Chronic enteropathy in dogs, the disease often called IBD, is what remains after parasites, infection, and a diet problem are ruled out. The workup starts with fecal tests, bloodwork including B12, and abdominal ultrasound. A diet trial comes before intestinal biopsies in a stable dog. Biopsies, endoscopic or surgical, are how inflammation is separated from lymphoma when the diet trial and the ultrasound have not answered the question.

The minimum lab set usually includes a complete blood count, a chemistry panel, and a urinalysis. Cobalamin (B12) and folate speak to whether the small intestine is absorbing. A trypsin-like immunoreactivity test looks for exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, which mimics small-bowel diarrhea. A baseline cortisol, and an ACTH stimulation test if that screen is low, looks for Addison's disease, because Addison's can look exactly like chronic gut disease. Fecal flotation and a Giardia test come before any steroid. Ultrasound looks at intestinal wall thickness, wall layering, and lymph nodes. In a stable dog the next step is a strict hydrolyzed or novel-protein diet for several weeks with no other foods. Endoscopic biopsies are the usual way to sample the stomach and intestine when diet and ultrasound have stalled. Full-thickness surgical biopsies are used when lymphoma is a serious concern or when the outer layers of bowel need to be seen. Histopathology, sometimes with a clonality assay, separates inflammation from cancer.

Treatment categories

Many dogs with chronic enteropathy improve on a hydrolyzed or novel-protein diet fed with no other foods. If signs continue, the veterinarian may add an antibiotic trial or an immunosuppressive drug, and sets that dose. Surgery is for the biopsy or for a complication such as an obstruction. Diet and medication are the routine treatment of colitis. B12 is supplemented when the blood level is low, again at a dose the veterinarian chooses.

Fiber helps some large-bowel cases, either as a veterinary gastrointestinal diet or as a supplement the clinic names. Deworming for whipworms is often done even when the first fecal is quiet, because those eggs are easy to miss. A probiotic is an optional add-on. It cannot stand in for the diet trial. If a drug trial is used, tylosin or metronidazole are among the antibiotics clinicians have historically chosen, for a defined period, with the understanding that long courses have their own costs. Immunosuppressive options include glucocorticoids, budesonide, cyclosporine, and chlorambucil, picked for the dog's protein level and other diseases. For granulomatous colitis, colonic biopsies are cultured for adherent-invasive E. coli and the antibiotic follows that sensitivity result. Cobalamin injections or oral B12 correct a documented deficiency. None of these are doses to copy from another dog's bottle.

Home care

Feed only the prescribed diet. That includes no dental chews, no flavored drugs unless the veterinarian changed them, no table food, and no "just a bite" from the other dog's bowl. Transition onto the new diet over several days unless the clinician wants a faster switch because the dog is sick. Keep a stool diary: frequency, mucus, blood, and appetite. Pick up the yard so whipworms do not recycle. For an acute colitis that the veterinarian has already examined, a short bland plan may be recommended, and then the dog returns to the long-term diet. If vomiting starts, or the dog becomes quiet and refuses water, stop the home experiment and call. Weigh the dog every week during a diet trial so a slow loss is visible before the recheck.

When it is

When to See

Collapse, repeated vomiting, black stools, pale gums, a painful tight belly, or bloody diarrhea in a puppy or a dull adult should be seen the same day. Those presentations can be parvovirus, acute hemorrhagic diarrhea syndrome, pancreatitis, intestinal obstruction, or Addisonian crisis, and fluids matter more than another day of chicken and rice. A dog who is straining and passing nothing, with a swollen abdomen, needs a blockage ruled out. Chronic soft stool in a bright, hydrated dog can be booked as a regular workup, and it still should not be parked for months on repeating over-the-counter remedies.

What to ask

Ask for the cost of the first tier and the cost of biopsies as separate numbers. Fecal tests, bloodwork with a B12 level, and a diet trial are the plan for a stable dog. Abdominal ultrasound is the imaging step when weight loss or abnormal labs appear. Endoscopy with biopsies is a sedation procedure with a pathologist's fee, and surgical biopsies cost more because they are an operation. Protein-losing disease and granulomatous colitis push dogs toward the bigger workup sooner. A prescription diet continued for life is the repeating cost when the dog turns out to be food-responsive, and it is cheaper than months of the wrong immunosuppressive drug.

Key Questions

Is chronic colitis the same as IBD?

Colitis describes large-bowel inflammation and can be a short illness. IBD, better called chronic enteropathy, is the longer disease left after parasites, infection, pancreas disease, and Addison's disease are off the list. A dog can have colitis signs for years and still be food-responsive, which means the bowel calms on the right diet. Biopsy-proven lymphoplasmacytic inflammation is the picture people mean by classic IBD, and even then diet remains part of treatment.

How long does a diet trial need to run?

For intestinal signs, many dogs show a change within a few weeks if the diet is going to work, and the veterinarian sets the length before anyone calls it a failure. Skin signs, if they are also present, need a longer trial than gut signs. The trial is void if the dog gets treats, flavored toothpaste, or another pet's food. A challenge with the old protein, done on purpose, is what confirms the food was the driver.

When are biopsies worth it?

When a stable dog has already had fecal tests, bloodwork, ultrasound, and a strict diet trial without an answer, or sooner if albumin is falling, a mass is suspected, or lymphoma is plausible from the ultrasound. Endoscopy samples the inner lining. Surgery samples the full wall and can reach segments a scope misses. The pathologist's report is what separates inflammation from cancer and what keeps a dog off steroids that would muddy that result.
