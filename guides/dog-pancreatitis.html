Pancreatitis in Dogs

Causes, symptoms, and treatment of pancreatitis in dogs. Covers acute vs chronic pancreatitis, dietary management, and long-term care strategies.

What pancreatitis is

Pancreatitis is inflammation of the pancreas, the gland that makes digestive enzymes and insulin. In an acute flare the enzymes activate too early and damage the pancreas and the fat around it. The dog vomits, refuses food, and hunches in pain. Chronic pancreatitis is a lower-grade or repeated inflammation that can scar the gland and, over time, contribute to diabetes mellitus or to exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, the state in which the dog can no longer digest food. One fatty meal can trigger a flare. Plenty of dogs flare with no feast anyone can identify. The disease is medical in the great majority of cases. Surgery is for a complication. The first afternoon of vomiting is a medical problem.

Severity runs from a dog who vomits twice and perks up with outpatient care to a dog in shock, with clotting problems, jaundice, and days of intravenous fluids. No drug reverses the inflammation the way an antibiotic clears a simple infection. Fluids, anti-nausea treatment, and pain control support the dog while the pancreas quiets.

Which dogs get

Miniature Schnauzers are the breed everyone names, partly because many of them run high blood triglycerides, and those fats are tied to pancreatic flares. Yorkshire Terriers and other small breeds are also common patients. Middle-aged and older dogs, overweight dogs, and dogs with Cushing's disease, hypothyroidism, or diabetes show up more often. A trash raid, holiday ham, gravy, or a bone-in fatty leftover is a classic trigger and is absent from many histories. Some drugs, including azathioprine, potassium bromide, phenobarbital, and certain chemotherapy agents, have been associated with flares. Trauma to the abdomen is an uncommon cause. A dog of any breed who vomits after a fatty table meal still deserves this disease on the list, beside a foreign body and an intestinal obstruction.

Signs owners notice

Vomiting, a poor appetite, and a dog who stands with the front legs down and the hindquarters up, the prayer posture, are the picture owners remember. Diarrhea, fever, a tense belly, and dehydration follow in a more serious flare. Some dogs only act nauseated and quiet. Jaundice, yellow gums or whites of the eyes, suggests the swollen pancreas is squeezing the bile duct or that the liver is involved. Bloody stool, collapse, and a racing or weak pulse mark a severe attack. Repeated flares look like "sensitive stomach" until a pancreatic test is finally run. A dog who has had one confirmed episode and then gets another fatty meal is a dog at high risk to do it again.

  • Vomiting, sometimes with bile, and a refusal to eat.
  • A hunched back or a prayer posture, and pain when the belly is touched.
  • Diarrhea, fever, or marked lethargy.
  • Yellow gums or eyes, which mean the bile flow or the liver needs a look.
  • Collapse, bloody diarrhea, or vomiting that will not stop.

How a veterinarian

Canine pancreatitis is diagnosed from the exam plus a pancreatic lipase test. A Spec cPL, or a cage-side cPL used as a screen, supports the diagnosis when the dog is vomiting, painful, or not eating. Ultrasound can show an abnormal pancreas, and a normal ultrasound does not clear the dog. Routine chemistry may be normal. Biopsy is rarely needed to diagnose it.

The cage-side canine pancreatic lipase test is a screen. A negative result in a mildly affected dog makes pancreatitis less likely. A positive result should be confirmed with a laboratory specific canine pancreatic lipase when the case is uncertain or severe. Ultrasound looks for an enlarged, hypoechoic pancreas and for fluid or peritonitis around it, and it also hunts for a foreign body, a tumor, or a bile obstruction that would change the plan. A normal ultrasound does not clear the diagnosis when the lipase test and the exam agree. Amylase and lipase on a routine chemistry panel are unreliable for this disease in dogs. A complete blood count and chemistry still matter because they show dehydration, electrolyte loss, and whether the liver and kidneys are in trouble. Biopsy is reserved for a mass or a case that will not declare itself any other way.

Treatment categories

Uncomplicated pancreatitis is medical: intravenous fluids, pain control, anti-nausea drugs, and food as soon as the dog can hold it. The veterinarian chooses every drug and the amount. A low-fat diet is the usual follow-up for dogs. Surgery is reserved for a complication such as a pancreatic abscess or a bile blockage, which ultrasound or a worsening exam would suggest.

Fluids correct dehydration and keep circulation to the pancreas. Anti-nausea drugs such as maropitant or ondansetron are typical categories, and pain control is often an opioid because a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory can be a poor fit in a vomiting, dehydrated dog. Food is offered early once vomiting is controlled. Leaving a dog without calories for days is an old habit that has fallen out of favor. A low-fat, highly digestible diet is the usual discharge plan, and triglyceride control matters for Schnauzers and other dogs with documented hyperlipidemia. Antibiotics are reserved for suspected sepsis or another bacterial problem. They do not treat sterile pancreatic inflammation. Plasma and intensive nursing are for the severe, coagulopathic dog in a hospital. If a bile duct stays blocked or an abscess forms, a surgeon joins the case.

When it is

When to See

Repeated vomiting, collapse, a hard painful belly, bloody diarrhea, yellow gums, or a dog who cannot keep water down should be seen immediately. Severe pancreatitis kills dogs through shock, clotting disorders, and bile blockage. A single vomit in a bright dog who then eats and plays can be watched for a short time and still should be reported if it repeats. Do not offer a fatty "bland" meal such as a skin-on chicken to a dog you suspect has pancreatitis. Water in small amounts, while you travel to the clinic, is the safer bridge unless the dog is too weak to swallow.

What to ask

Acute canine pancreatitis is an inflammatory flare. No drug reverses it the way an antibiotic clears a simple infection. Many dogs recover from a single episode. Dogs that keep getting fatty meals, or that have untreated Cushing's disease, often flare again.

Ask the emergency clinic for a 24-hour pancreatitis estimate and a 72-hour estimate before the second day of IV fluids. Outpatient anti-nausea medicine is a small invoice. A cPLI, an ultrasound, and several days on fluids are the large one. After discharge, the repeating cost is the low-fat diet your veterinarian names, which is cheaper than another admission.

Key Questions

Did the table scrap cause this?

Sometimes yes, and often nobody finds a trigger. A fatty meal is a known risk, especially in a Miniature Schnauzer or an overweight dog, and plenty of flares start in dogs who ate their normal kibble. The practical point is the same either way: after a confirmed episode, fatty food is retired. Blaming one ham slice should not delay fluids if the dog is still vomiting.

Why did the ultrasound look normal?

Because the pancreas can be inflamed and still hard to see, and gas in the abdomen hides it. A normal ultrasound does not clear a dog whose exam and specific pancreatic lipase say otherwise. Ultrasound remains useful because it looks for the complications and the look-alikes: a foreign body, a bile blockage, a mass, or free fluid. The lipase test plus the physical exam carry the diagnosis when the image is quiet.

What should the dog eat afterward?

A low-fat diet the veterinarian selects, in measured meals, with no gravy and no leftovers. Some dogs stay on that diet for life. Others are retried on a moderate plan only after a long quiet stretch and a conversation about triglycerides. Treats have to fit the fat limit too. A dental chew or a piece of cheese can be as fatty as the meal that started the last flare. Ask for a brand or a home recipe the clinic has already approved.
