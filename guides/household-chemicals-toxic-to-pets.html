Household Chemicals Toxic to Pets

Household Chemicals Toxic to Pets belong in the care list for the Household Chemicals Toxic to Pets. Covers safe alternatives and poison-proofing your home.

Cleaners, drains, and the mistake of mixing products

Household chemicals injure pets by burning tissue, by fumes, or by a systemic poison after a lick. The response depends on which of those you are facing. A corrosive drain opener, oven cleaner, or concentrated toilet bowl acid burns the mouth and esophagus. Vomiting that product brings the burn back up. Do not induce vomiting for caustics. Rinse the mouth with gentle water if the pet allows it and a veterinarian agrees, and go to a clinic with the bottle. Mixing bleach and ammonia, or bleach and an acid toilet cleaner, releases irritating gas. If you already mixed them, air the room, leave with the pets, and call for advice before you re-enter. The fumes hurt lungs. A curious cat in the closed bathroom is the usual victim.

Laundry detergent pods are concentrated and attractive. A bite can cause drooling, vomiting, coughing, and chemical burns, and the film can be swallowed. Dishwasher pods are similar. Keep both in a latched cabinet. Ordinary dish soap in a tiny lick often causes milder drooling and stomach upset, and you should still call if the product was a concentrate, a disinfectant, or a "looks like candy" pod. Read the word concentrate on the label. Concentrates are a different event from a dilute mop water, and even mop water should be dumped where a dog cannot drink it.

Batteries, petroleum, and alcohols

Button batteries and punctured lithium batteries are emergencies. A battery in the esophagus can burn a hole quickly because of electrical current and alkaline contents. A dog who chews a remote or a round light-up toy needs radiographs the same day. Waiting is the wrong plan. Do not induce vomiting. Bring the device. Cylindrical batteries can obstruct even when they do not leak. Petroleum products such as gasoline, kerosene, lamp oil, and lighter fluid injure the lungs if they are inhaled during vomiting. Do not induce vomiting for those either. Skin contact needs a wash with mild soap if the hotline agrees, and the pet must be kept from licking the residue. Call a poison hotline and describe the product.

Alcohols show up as drinks left on tables, as hand sanitizer, and as some antifreeze substitutes. Ethanol causes drunken walking, low body temperature, and low blood sugar, especially in small pets. Isopropanol in rubbing alcohol is more potent. A cat who walks through a spill and grooms will ingest it. Xylitol may be in some sweetened products near the same cabinet. Name every chemical on the label. Antifreeze from the garage is a separate, kidney-centered emergency and should be handled on that guide's terms: call immediately and bring the jug.

Glues, paints, and rodent or insect products in the house

Expanding polyurethane glues, the kind used for construction and some crafts, swell into a large mass in a stomach that contains moisture. A dog who chews a bottle can develop an obstruction that surgery has to remove. The bottle on the workbench is the clue. Do not induce vomiting if the hotline says the glue will swell on the way up. Follow their instruction and travel. Paint thinners and strippers belong with the petroleum and caustic rules. Dried latex paint on a wall is a different, usually milder exposure than a lap from the open can. Call anyway if the amount was large or the pet is vomiting.

Insect baits, mothballs, and essential-oil cleaners deserve their own reading of the label. Mothballs come in different chemicals with different risks, including liver injury or blood-cell damage depending on the type. Bring the package so the clinic knows which mothball it was. Flea foggers can cause tremors, especially in cats, and the house has to be aired before anyone returns. A dog product that contains a high level of permethrin, applied to a cat, is a classic tremor emergency. Bathing may be part of decontamination only when the clinic tells you how, because a cold wet cat can crash. Essential oils in "natural" cleaners are covered on the oil pages. If the label lists tea tree or wintergreen, say those words to the hotline.

A cabinet that matches the species in the house

Latch the under-sink doors. Store chemicals in their original bottles so the label survives. A sport bottle full of cleaner looks like a drink to a guest and like a toy to a dog. Use the original cap. Wipe paw prints off the floor after you mop, and block the room until it is dry. When a spill happens, take a photo of the label before the bottle is washed, remove the pet from the room, and call. Do not offer milk or oil as a universal neutralizer. Those myths delay care and sometimes worsen aspiration. If the eyes were splashed, ask about a gentle water flush on the way and protect yourself from bites while you do it.

Symptoms that mean you stop watching and drive include trouble breathing, burns on the tongue, repeated vomiting, tremors, seizures, and collapse. Caustic injuries can look mild in the mouth and be severe in the esophagus, so a normal-looking tongue does not clear the pet. The clinic may scope the esophagus later. Ask them what follow-up looks like before you leave. Prevention is latches, original bottles, and a house rule that nobody mixes cleaners for a "stronger" bathroom. Stronger is how toxic gas gets made.

  • Do not induce vomiting for drain cleaners, oven cleaner, batteries, or petroleum products.
  • Air the house and leave if bleach was mixed with ammonia or acid.
  • Treat button batteries and expanding glues as surgical emergencies.
  • Call for detergent pods, mothballs, and permethrin products on cats.
  • Bring the original bottle and read every active ingredient.
  • Latch cabinets and keep chemicals out of sports bottles.
  • Skip milk and oil as home neutralizers.
  • Drive in for breathing trouble, burns, tremors, or collapse.

Pods, mixes, and the original bottle

Detergent pods and dishwasher pods look like toys. A bitten pod can burn the mouth and cause coughing if product is inhaled. Bring the container and describe whether the film was swallowed. Drain cleaner and oven cleaner are caustics. Vomiting them repeats the burn. Petroleum products such as lamp oil and gasoline have the same vomiting prohibition because the lungs are at risk. If you already mixed bleach with ammonia or with an acid cleaner, leave the house with the pets and call from outside. Air the room from a safe place. Do not add a third chemical to "cancel" the gas.

Expanding glue and button batteries need imaging. Appetite alone is a weak reason to stay home. A dog who is eating can still have a battery in the esophagus. Original bottles matter because a sport bottle of blue liquid could be a drink or a cleaner. When the label is gone, tell the clinic what the product was supposed to be and bring whatever container you have. Latch the cabinet after the crisis. The next spill is prevented by the latch you install tonight.

Questions about household chemicals

Should I make my pet vomit after licking a cleaner?

Not for caustics such as drain opener, oven cleaner, or toilet acid, and not for petroleum products or batteries. Vomiting can worsen burns or send petroleum into the lungs. Call a veterinarian or poison hotline, bring the bottle, and follow their instruction for that specific product.

What if a dog chewed a battery or a glue bottle?

Go to a clinic. Button batteries can burn the esophagus quickly, and expanding polyurethane glue can form a blockage that needs surgery. Do not induce vomiting unless the clinic says the material will not swell or burn on the way up. Bring the package.

Why is mixing bleach and ammonia a pet emergency?

The mix releases irritating gas that injures lungs. Leave the room with the pets, air the space from a safe place, and call for advice before returning. A cat closed in the bathroom is at high risk. Do not try to neutralize the mix with another chemical.
