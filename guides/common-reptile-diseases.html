Common Reptile Diseases

Guide to common reptile health problems including MBD, respiratory infections, scale rot, parasites, and shedding issues with treatment guidance.

The enclosure is part of the diagnosis

Most sick reptiles are sick because the box they live in has been wrong for weeks. A bearded dragon with a soft jaw, a leopard gecko that cannot shed its toes, and a snake with bubbles at the nostrils can share a waiting room and still need three different fixes. Dog and cat antibiotics, grabbed from a bathroom cabinet, do not cover that spread. Name the species, the age, and the exact equipment before you ask for a drug.

Bring photos of the enclosure, the bulb packaging, and a fresh dropping if you can. Reptile veterinarians use those details with the exam. A fecal test looks for parasites. Radiographs look for thin bone, eggs, and objects that were swallowed. Blood uric acid matters in species that excrete uric acid, because dehydration and the wrong protein load show up as gout risk. The plan that follows is for that animal, under a thermostat and a light that actually still produces ultraviolet B.

Metabolic bone disease, ultraviolet B, and calcium

Metabolic bone disease is what happens when a reptile cannot build or hold bone. The usual reasons are a missing or expired ultraviolet B source, too little calcium in the diet, a calcium-to-phosphorus balance that favors phosphorus, and, in some species, a lack of vitamin D. Insect-eating lizards are classic cases because crickets and roaches are naturally low in calcium unless they are gut-loaded and dusted. Green iguanas and many other herbivores develop it on diets heavy in lettuce and fruit. Turtles and tortoises show soft shells and deformed scutes when the same pieces are missing. Snakes that eat whole prey are less often affected, because a whole rodent already contains bone.

Early signs are a lizard that stops hunting, trembles when it walks, or has a jaw that feels rubbery. Later you see bowed legs, fractures from ordinary climbing, a swollen limb, or a turtle whose shell flexes under a gentle finger. The reptile veterinarian confirms the suspicion with radiographs and with the husbandry story. Severe animals need hospital care, fluids, and calcium the veterinarian gives. That is a dosing decision, and it is unsafe to invent at home. At the same time the enclosure has to change, or the bones will fail again after discharge.

Ultraviolet B bulbs keep producing visible light long after the ultraviolet output has collapsed. Replace them on the interval the manufacturer prints, often measured in months, and mount them at the distance that bulb was tested for. Screen lids block a large share of the output, so a bulb sitting on top of fine mesh may not deliver a useful dose to the animal underneath. There is no single bulb for every species. A forest gecko and a desert dragon need different intensities, and a reptile veterinarian or a current care sheet for that exact species should set the target. Calcium for an insectivore is a light dusting of a calcium product on feeder insects on the schedule that veterinarian sets, plus gut-loading those insects so the dust is only part of the meal. A multivitamin is used less often than plain calcium, because vitamin A and vitamin D can accumulate. Herbivores need calcium-rich greens and a cut in phosphorus-heavy foods, with a supplement chosen for that species. Heat belongs under a thermostat. Hot rocks burn reptiles and should leave the enclosure.

Dysecdysis, the shed that will not come off

Dysecdysis means a shed that started and stalled. Snakes may keep a dry, patched skin, and the eye cap, the clear scale over the eye, may stay in place so the eye looks cloudy or wrinkled after the rest of the body has cleared. Leopard geckos and other lizards often retain skin on toes, tail tips, and around the hemipenal bulges. A ring of old skin on a toe works like a tourniquet. The toe swells, darkens, and can be lost. Low humidity, dehydration, old scars, and mites are common reasons. A healthy shed in a snake usually comes off in one piece after the animal has soaked and rubbed.

A shallow lukewarm soak and a moist hide can be part of a veterinarian's plan. Do not peel dry skin off toes or eyes. Pulling a retained eye cap can tear the cornea. If a toe is already swollen or dark, that visit is urgent, because circulation is failing. After the skin is free, fix the humidity and the water bowl so the next cycle can finish. Mites, which look like tiny black or red specks and often drown in the water dish, cause repeated bad sheds and need treatment of both the animal and the cage under veterinary direction. Do not apply a dog or cat flea product to a reptile.

Respiratory infection

Open-mouth breathing in a lizard, bubbles or mucus at the nostrils, a wheeze, or a snake that holds its head up and breathes with effort are respiratory signs. They often follow weeks of temperatures below the range that species uses to digest and to run an immune response. A dirty, dusty, or soaking-wet cage adds irritation. The reptile veterinarian listens, may take radiographs, and chooses an antibiotic for the bacteria involved in that animal. A course aimed at a dog's cough is the wrong drug, the wrong interval, and often the wrong route. While you wait for the appointment, keep the animal in the clean, quiet, thermostat-controlled temperature range it is supposed to have. Do not crank an unregulated heat source until the cage is hot to the touch. A reptile that is too cold cannot use a drug well. A reptile that is cooked on a heat rock is burned.

Mouth rot, infectious stomatitis, can travel with a respiratory problem or stand alone. The gums and lips look red, cheesy, or swollen, and the animal stops eating. That mouth needs a veterinarian's cleaning plan and pain control. Home peroxide and human ointments delay that visit and can harm tissue.

Parasites, from pinworms to cryptosporidium

Intestinal parasites are found on a fecal exam, a float plus a direct smear, and sometimes a specific test. Pinworms are common in bearded dragons. A small number in a bright, eating dragon may be watched, and a heavy load in a thin dragon is treated with a drug the reptile veterinarian doses for that species. Coccidia show up in young animals and in crowded collections. Cryptosporidium is the serious one in leopard geckos: weight loss until the tail is a stick, regurgitation, and a failing appetite. It spreads easily on equipment, it is hard to clear, and the veterinarian should guide any decision about treatment versus isolation, because a casual dewormer for pinworms leaves crypto in place. Quarantine every new reptile and keep separate tools for that tub.

External mites on snakes are a collection problem. You may see the snake soaking constantly, specks moving on the rim of the water bowl, or small black dots under the scales. Treating only the snake and putting it back into a mite-filled cage fails. The veterinarian can recommend a product that is safe for that species and a way to clean the enclosure. Permethrin products sold for dogs can be lethal to cats and are unsafe experiments on reptiles. Wild-caught animals and animals from crowded sales tables deserve a fecal test before they join a healthy group.

Scale rot and other problems the opening description names

Ventral scale rot, a moist dermatitis, comes from lying on wet, soiled substrate. The belly scales blister, stain, and then ulcerate. Snakes in soggy tubs and dragons on damp, dirty reptile carpet both get it. Move the animal onto a clean, dry, simple surface the veterinarian accepts, and have the ulcers assessed. Deep lesions need more than a substrate swap. Burns from a lamp that was too close, or from a heat rock, can look similar and need pain control and infection management. Eggs that will not pass, dystocia, are a separate emergency: a female who strains, digs without result, or collapses may need calcium, fluids, or surgery. Do not try to pull an egg.

  • Replace ultraviolet B bulbs on the manufacturer's interval, and check distance and screen mesh.
  • Dust feeder insects with calcium on the reptile veterinarian's schedule, and gut-load those insects.
  • Give every species a thermostat-controlled warm side and a cool side, and retire hot rocks.
  • Treat a retained toe shed or a swollen digit as urgent.
  • Run a fecal test on new animals, and keep crypto suspects isolated with their own tools.

Go the same day

Open-mouth breathing, a seizure or constant tremor, a black or swollen toe, a prolapse, a female straining to pass eggs, or a reptile that has not eaten and is losing weight fast should be seen promptly by a reptile veterinarian. Call ahead and ask for an exotics appointment. If that clinic is closed, an emergency hospital that will see reptiles is the right door. Do not wait out a weekend with a lizard that cannot lift its head.

What the bill tends to cover

A first reptile visit with a fecal test is often the smallest charge. Radiographs for bone density or for eggs cost more, and hospitalization for metabolic bone disease or a respiratory infection costs more again. Ultraviolet B equipment is a recurring supply cost, smaller than a fracture repair and easy to skip until the jaw is soft. City and clinic change these figures. Ask for a written estimate that separates the exam, the tests, and any stay.

Questions reptile keepers ask

Does a bright bulb still provide ultraviolet B?

Visible light can keep shining after ultraviolet B output has faded. Follow the replacement interval on that bulb, mount it at the tested distance, and account for any screen between the lamp and the animal. A reptile veterinarian can help you match intensity to the species. Calcium dusting and gut-loaded insects still have to be in the diet of an insectivore, because light alone does not put mineral into a cricket.

Should I pull a stuck shed off at home?

Dry peeling of toes, tail tips, or eye caps causes tears and lost digits. A veterinarian can loosen a retained shed after the animal is hydrated, and a dark swollen toe should be seen urgently. Fix humidity, offer a moist hide, and look for mites if every cycle fails. A dog or cat flea product does not belong on a snake or a lizard.

Are pinworms and cryptosporidium handled the same way?

They are different organisms. Pinworms in an otherwise strong bearded dragon may be a small finding the veterinarian treats or monitors. Cryptosporidium in a leopard gecko is a wasting disease that spreads on shared tools and a routine pinworm drug leaves it in place. Isolate that gecko and let the reptile veterinarian decide the next step after a specific test.
