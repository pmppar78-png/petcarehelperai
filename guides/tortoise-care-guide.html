Pet Tortoise Care Guide

Complete tortoise care guide covering popular species (Russian, Sulcata, Hermann), enclosure, diet, hibernation, and lifespan considerations.

Housing and Enclosure

Tortoises are grazing reptiles that need floor, sun, and a species name on the care sheet. A Russian tortoise, a Hermann's tortoise, and a sulcata are three different adult sizes and three different commitments. Russians and Hermann's can live in a large indoor table, often starting around 4 feet by 8 feet for a single adult, with outdoor time when the weather is warm and safe. A sulcata becomes enormous and needs a secure outdoor pen with a heated house in any climate outside a hot desert. Buying a sulcata hatchling for an apartment is the decision rescues spend the next 40 years undoing.

Indoors, build a tortoise table with walls the animal cannot see over and climb. Open floor is for walking. A basking lamp creates a hot spot around 95 to 100 F on the shell, measured with an infrared thermometer, and the rest of the table can sit in the mid 70s to mid 80s F. Night temperatures depend on the species. Russians and Hermann's tolerate cooler nights. Sulcatas need a warm night house and should not be left in a cold garage. UVB is required whenever the tortoise is indoors. A linear tube over the basking zone, at the correct distance and replaced on schedule, is the tool. A window fails as a UVB source. Glass filters it out. Real outdoor sun, with shade available so the tortoise can leave the light, is better than any bulb and still needs a secure fence and protection from dogs and raccoons.

Substrate indoors can be a soil and coconut-fiber mix deep enough for a shallow dig, kept dry on top for species that pyramid in wet stuff and with a humid hide for youngsters of species that need moisture to grow smooth shells. Pyramiding, the stacked scutes, is linked to a dry start and a poor diet in many popular species. Humidity for hatchling Greeks, Hermann's, and sulcatas is a researched topic. Read the species, and give babies a moist retreat even when the adult care sheet sounds dry. Water is a shallow tray the tortoise can walk into and a soak a few times a week for animals that live indoors. Outdoor tortoises still need a water dish they cannot tip.

Diet and Nutrition

Most pet tortoises are herbivores that should graze. The plate is weeds and greens: dandelion, plantain, clover, endive, escarole, chicory, and grassy hay for species that need fiber. Variety across the week matters more than a single "tortoise pellet" poured into a dish. Pellets can be a small part of the diet for some species and a problem when they become the whole diet. Fruit is a rare treat for Russians and Hermann's. Red-foot and yellow-foot tortoises, a different group from the usual Mediterranean pet, take more fruit and some protein. If you have a red-foot, follow a red-foot sheet. Feeding one like a sulcata, or a sulcata like a red-foot, produces a sick animal.

Calcium belongs on the greens a few times a week, and a cuttlebone in the enclosure lets the tortoise self-serve. UVB or real sun is what makes that calcium useful. Spinach and beet greens show up on avoid-lists because of oxalates. They can appear as a small part of a wide mix and should not be the daily salad. Iceberg lettuce is water and little else. Grocery flowers and produce that were sprayed are a risk. Grow a pot of weeds or buy unsprayed greens. Protein from dog food or meat does not belong in a Russian or Hermann's diet. It grows a heavy shell and stressed kidneys.

Feed a pile the tortoise can graze, and let it walk between bites. A tortoise in a small tub with a bowl of pellets becomes obese and pyramided. Outdoor grazing on safe, pesticide-free plants is the best feeder you can build. Learn the toxic ornamentals in your yard, including rhubarb and many bulbs, before you open the door.

Health and Veterinary Care

Metabolic bone disease, pyramiding, respiratory infection, and parasites are the core problems. Soft shell and a weak bite point back to missing UVB or missing calcium. Pyramiding is permanent once the scutes have stacked, so the humidity and diet of the first years matter more than any later cream. Respiratory infections bring a runny nose, bubbly mucus, and a tortoise that gapes. They need a reptile veterinarian and a review of night temperatures. Parasites are common in wild-caught and group-housed animals. Bring a fecal sample to the first exam. Stones from dehydrated tortoises that are fed a dry pellet diet show up as straining. Soaks and a watery green diet prevent more of them than any supplement.

Hibernation is species-specific and dangerous when done badly. Hermann's and some Russians can brumate if they are healthy, at a verified weight, and at a stable cool temperature a veterinarian has signed off on. Sulcatas do not hibernate. A sulcata in a cold shed is a dead sulcata. If you are unsure, skip hibernation and keep the tortoise awake, warm, and under UVB for the winter. Never put a sick or underweight tortoise away to "sleep it off."

Find a veterinarian who has radiographed a tortoise before you need an egg-bound female or a beak trim. Overgrown beaks usually mean the diet is too soft and the animal never grazes. Annual checks, including a weight in grams, catch the slow decline owners miss because tortoises move slowly even when they are well.

Before You Choose a Species

  • Russian or Hermann's: large indoor table plus outdoor time, grazing diet, UVB whenever they are inside.
  • Sulcata: plan the adult pen and the heated night house before you look at hatchlings.
  • Measure the basking shell temperature and provide shade so the tortoise can leave the lamp.
  • Feed weeds and greens, calcium, and hay where the species needs fiber. Keep fruit rare for Mediterranean species.
  • Ask a reptile veterinarian about hibernation for your exact species, and skip it for sulcatas.

A well-kept tortoise walks a lot, grazes with a strong beak, and basks with its legs sprawled, then retreats to shade. One that bubbles at the nose, sits with the head out and the eyes shut, or grows a shell like a mountain needs the temperatures, the sun or UVB, and a clinic. They are decades-long animals. The enclosure you build in year one should already imagine year twenty.

Quick Answers

Are tortoises good for beginner reptile owners?

A Russian or Hermann's tortoise can work for a beginner who builds a large table, offers real grazing, and uses outdoor sun or a linear UVB tube. Sulcatas overwhelm most first-time keepers because of adult size.

How much does tortoise care cost?

The table or outdoor pen, UVB, and basking heat cost more than the hatchling. Greens are an ongoing grocery bill, and veterinary care plus a lifespan measured in decades make the cheap pet-store price misleading.
