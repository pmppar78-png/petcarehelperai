Best Dog Shampoos

Top dog shampoos for every coat type and skin condition. Covers medicated, hypoallergenic, deodorizing, and puppy-safe formulas.

Dog skin and the bottle that belongs on it

Dog skin tends to sit closer to neutral than the more acidic surface human shampoos are blended for. A shower gel that leaves your hair feeling "squeaky" can strip the oils a dog's coat uses to stay flexible, and the dog then scratches for days. Choose a shampoo with dogs on the label, a scent level you can tolerate in a small bathroom, and a job that matches the coat in the tub. A short single coat with a roll in the mud needs a gentle cleanser and a thorough rinse. A double-coated northern breed needs a product that rinses out of the undercoat completely, plus time with a dryer, because a damp undercoat is how hot spots and sour smell start.

Read past the front adjective. Oatmeal, aloe, and colloidal oatmeal products aim at mild itch and dry skin. Fragrance-free and dye-free formulas suit dogs who get red after strongly scented baths. Whitening shampoos for white coats often rely on optical effects or stronger detergents. They can brighten a Bichon for a photograph and they can irritate the same dog if you use them every week. Puppy formulas are usually milder and tear-conscious for faces. They are still soap. Keep them out of eyes, and keep any insecticidal chemical out of a young puppy unless the veterinarian named that product for that age.

Medicated washes

Chlorhexidine, benzoyl peroxide, and antifungal ingredients such as ketoconazole show up in veterinary shampoos for bacterial skin infection, greasy skin, and yeast. Those are real tools, and they belong on a schedule a veterinarian sets after looking at the skin, sometimes after a cytology slide. Bathing with a leftover bottle because a dog smells yeasty can calm odor for a day and miss a thyroid problem, an allergy, or an ear disease that is seeding the rest of the body. If you are sent home with a medicated shampoo, follow the contact time. A lick-and-rinse does little. Many of these products need several minutes on the coat before they rinse, and the clinic should tell you how often, because daily medicated baths will dry a coat that only needed twice a week.

Do not mix a medicated shampoo with a flea chemical, an essential oil, or another medicated product in the same bath unless the prescriber said to. Layers of active ingredients are how skin gets burned by good intentions. Wear gloves if the label suggests them, and keep the dog from licking the lather. Rinse until the water runs clear and then rinse the chest and the armpits again. Residue in the folds of a Bulldog or a Shar-Pei will sit on irritated skin all night.

How often, and what to do about mud

Many pet dogs do well with a full shampoo every four to six weeks, plus a rinse of the legs after a muddy walk. Dogs who swim in pools may need more frequent bathing because chlorine and salt dry the coat, followed by a conditioner made for dogs if the coat is long. Dogs with a veterinary skin plan may be on a tighter calendar for a few weeks and then a maintenance pace. Overbathing a healthy dog "to stay ahead of the smell" often creates the dry skin that smells worse. If a freshly bathed dog smells off again within a day, look at ears, anal glands, dental disease, and skin folds. Shampoo cannot cover an ear infection.

Mud rinses with lukewarm water and a little shampoo only where the dirt is stuck. Hot water makes itch worse and can burn skin in a deep sink the dog cannot jump out of. Test the spray on your wrist. Brush out loose undercoat and mats before the coat gets wet. Water tightens mats, and a matted Poodle or doodle coat will felt into a pelt that a shampoo cannot save. Those dogs need a groomer and sometimes a clip-down before any fancy product will reach the skin. Dry to the skin on double coats, using a dryer the dog has been taught to accept, or a stack of towels and time in a warm room. Leaving a Husky damp in a crate overnight is unfair and smelly.

Flea shampoos, oils, and human additives

Flea shampoos knock down some insects that are on the dog during the bath and do little about the eggs in the house or the next flea that jumps on during the evening walk. A veterinarian-recommended preventive, matched to the dog's weight and to the other pets in the home, does the ongoing work. Some "natural" shampoos add tea tree, citrus, pennyroyal, or other essential oils. Tea tree in particular has sent dogs to emergency clinics when it was concentrated or when a dog licked a coated coat. Skip essential-oil baths unless a veterinarian has approved a specific product. The same caution covers homemade vinegar rinses at strong dilution guesses and any essential oil dropped into an otherwise fine oatmeal bottle.

Human dandruff shampoos, dish soap, and laundry detergent are emergency degreasers people reach for when a dog meets a skunk and then regret when the skin peels. A skunk wash sold for dogs, or the peroxide-baking-soda-soap recipe your veterinarian confirms, beats a bottle of dishwashing liquid used every day for a week. If you must remove motor oil or a similar contaminant, call a clinic or a poison line before you improvise. Some solvents and some "home hacks" are worse than the original mess, and a dog will lick whatever you leave behind.

Technique that makes a cheap shampoo work

Wet the dog all the way to the skin, through the outer fluff and the undercoat beneath it. Apply shampoo along the back and work outward, including feet, sanitary area, and under the collar line. Keep a hand shield at the eyes and use a face cloth with a damp corner of shampoo for the cheeks. Leave the product on for the labeled minutes if it has a job beyond soap. Rinse longer than you think. Then feel the coat. Slippery residue means rinse again. Conditioner, if you use one, goes on the lengths of a long coat and stays off any medicated plan unless the clinic approved it. Silicone-heavy conditioners can build a film that makes the next bath harder to rinse. A light dog conditioner used occasionally on a spaniel is plenty for most houses.

Brush after the coat is dry, or blow-dry while brushing a double coat, so you see skin you could not see when it was wet. Note redness, bumps, or a yeasty brown stain in thin-haired areas and take a photo for the next visit. Tub setup matters for safety: a non-slip mat, a leash loop or a second person for a large dog, and no standing water a puppy can inhale. Ear canals should stay free of a direct jet of water. If the dog shakes and soaks the room, you still resist the urge to swab deep into the canal with a soapy cloth.

Bottles, dilution, and typical prices

Concentrates cost less per bath once you follow the dilution line. A cap poured straight from a "dilute me" bottle is how a mild shampoo becomes harsh. Mark the dilution on the spray bottle you actually use, and wash that bottle when the product changes so chlorhexidine and oatmeal never share a sprayer. Store flea products and skin products in different bins. Typical retail bands often put a gentle oatmeal shampoo around $8 to $20, a larger concentrate around $15 to $35, and veterinary medicated bottles higher, sometimes $20 to $45 depending on the drug and the size. . A $9 bottle used with a full rinse on a sensible schedule beats a $30 bottle left on the coat for thirty seconds once a season.

Shampoo questions

Can I use my own shampoo on my dog in a pinch?

Dog skin prefers a dog shampoo, because human formulas are built for a more acidic scalp and can leave a coat dry and itchy. If a dog is covered in something hazardous, call a veterinarian or a poison hotline before you grab dish soap or a solvent. For ordinary mud, wait until you can use a dog product and rinse it all the way out of the undercoat.

How often should a healthy dog get a full bath?

Every four to six weeks is plenty for many house dogs, with leg rinses between baths when the yard is wet. Dogs on a medicated plan follow the clinic calendar, which may be more frequent for a short stretch. Swimming dogs may need a rinse after the pool. Bathing a clean dog every few days often creates the flake and itch people then try to wash off again.

Do flea shampoos replace a monthly preventive?

They remove some fleas that are on the coat during that bath and they miss the life cycle in the carpets and the flea that arrives later the same day. Use a preventive your veterinarian matches to the dog's weight and household. Skip tea tree and other essential oils in the flea shampoo aisle unless the clinic has approved a specific bottle.
