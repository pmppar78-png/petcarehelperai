Adolescent Dog Behavior Guide

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Covers regression in training, hormonal changes, exercise needs, and surviving canine adolescence.

What adolescence looks like in a dog

Adolescence in dogs usually stretches from roughly six months to somewhere between 18 months and two years, and giant breeds often run later than toy breeds. The dog who sat in puppy class starts to pretend deafness at the park. Recall that worked in the kitchen falls apart when a squirrel exists. Mouthiness can return. Some dogs become more cautious of strangers they greeted happily at 14 weeks. Others surge toward every dog with a play style that is now too rough for the puppy they still think they are. Sexual maturity adds urine marking in many males and, in females, a first heat if they are still intact.

This stretch is a brain and a body project at the same time. Bones are still closing in larger dogs, so endless jumping and forced weekend hikes can irritate joints that will have to last a decade. The need for mental work goes up while the willingness to work for you, in public, goes down. Families often describe the dog as "stubborn." What you are seeing is a normal conflict between a developing brain and a cue that was learned in a quiet living room. The cue still exists. The distractions got louder.

Training that survives the teenage months

Short sessions beat long lectures. Five minutes of recall practice in a boring hallway, paid with something the dog actually wants, rebuilds the behavior before you test it at a soccer field. Raise the difficulty one notch at a time: backyard, then sidewalk, then a quiet field with a long line. A long line is a safety tool, and it lets you reinforce the dog for coming while the choice is still real. Off-leash at the dog park is a poor test of a teenage recall. You will practice the failure.

Restack the manners that slipped. Sit at the door, a hand target, a settle on a mat while you cook. Reward the dog for looking at you when a bicycle passes. If jumping on guests returned, greet the dog only after four paws are on the floor, and have guests do the same. Punishment for an ignored recall, delivered when the dog finally arrives, teaches the dog that arrival is risky. Pay the arrival. Prevent the rehearsal of the chase with a line, a fence, or a closed door.

Fear periods can show up as a sudden spook at a trash can the dog has passed for months. Give distance, pair the object with food tossed on the ground, and leave before the dog is overwhelmed. Dragging the dog closer to "get over it" often cements the fear. A dog who becomes globally terrified, who snaps when touched, or who stops eating on walks needs a veterinary exam and a qualified trainer. Pain in a growing dog, an ear infection, or a stomach ache can look exactly like a bad attitude.

Exercise, chew needs, and sleep

Adolescent dogs need movement and they need recovery. A useful day includes a sniff walk where the dog sets a lot of the pace, a training game, and a chew. It does not need an hour of repetitive ball throwing on pavement. Sprint games on concrete are a common way young dogs hurt shoulders and toes. Swimming, trick training, and nose work burn energy with less impact. A crate nap or a pen break in the middle of the day prevents the evening zoomies that families mistake for a need for even more exercise. An overtired teenager is mouthy and rude.

Chewing returns when adult teeth are in and the dog is bored. Rotate safe chews and put away anything that splinters or that you cannot dent. Supervise edible chews. A dog who gulps a large piece needs a different product and a slower setup, such as a chew held in a rubber toy. Counter surfing and trash digging peak in this age because the dog is tall enough and optimistic. Management is part of training: a closed kitchen door works while the leave-it cue is still fragile.

Social life, intact dogs, and the clinic

Dog-dog play should be interrupted while it is still friendly. A teenage dog's body is heavier and the play style is faster. Watch for a dog who cannot be called out of play, who targets the neck repeatedly, or who leaves the other dog stiff and hiding. Those sessions should end. Parallel walks with a calm adult dog teach more than a chaotic daycare hour. Daycare, if you use it, should be able to describe rest periods and should not warehouse a barking group all afternoon.

If the dog is still intact, talk with the veterinarian about the timing of spay or neuter for this breed and this body size. The decision involves joints, certain cancers, mammary risk, pyometra risk in females, roaming, and marking. There is no single age that fits a Chihuahua and a Great Dane. Until the surgery, prevent unwanted litters with management. A fence and a leash are the plan during a heat. Male dogs can smell a heat across a neighborhood and will forget every cue you like.

Book a wellness visit if the dog is limping for more than a day, coughing, losing weight, having repeated diarrhea, or showing a personality change that training does not touch. Hip and elbow pain can start quietly in fast-growing dogs. A sudden aggression toward familiar people, especially around sleep or food, deserves an exam before a training label. Keep the vaccine and parasite plan current. Adolescence is when some families stop the heartworm preventive because the puppy package ended. Put the refill on the calendar.

Home rules that keep a teenage dog employable

Decide which furniture is allowed and keep the rule every day, because an adolescent dog treats exceptions as the new law. A tether to your belt while you cook, a mat with a chew, and a closed door to the guest room prevent rehearsal of counter surfing and trash raids. Visitors get a one-line instruction card: ignore jumping, greet only when paws are down, and skip the rough wrestling that winds the dog up. Kids' games of chase through the house teach the dog to grab clothing. Move that game outside with a toy, or end it.

Alone time should be practiced in the middle of a good week. Leave for five minutes, return before distress, and build from there. A camera shows whether the barking is a two-minute protest or a panic. Destruction at the door, drooling, or a dog who cannot settle after you return deserves a trainer who understands separation distress and a veterinary check for pain. Crate training that was solid at four months can be rebuilt with meals fed inside and short absences. A teenage dog left alone for nine hours with no midday break will find a hobby you dislike.

  • Expect recall and manners to wobble from about six months into the second year.
  • Practice cues on a long line before you trust them around squirrels.
  • Reward the dog for coming. Save corrections for prevention, such as a fence.
  • Give distance during a sudden fear and skip forcing the dog toward the trigger.
  • Trade endless ball throwing for sniff walks, training, and rest.
  • End dog play while it is still mutual and easy to interrupt.
  • Ask the clinic about spay or neuter timing for this breed and size.
  • Examine pain, gut trouble, and hearing or vision changes before you call a dog stubborn.

Questions about adolescent dogs

When does dog adolescence start and end?

Many dogs shift around six months and settle somewhere between 18 months and two years. Giant breeds often mature later. The visible signs are a weaker public recall, rougher play, new caution, and, in intact dogs, marking or a first heat.

How should training change for a teenage dog?

Short, well-paid sessions in easy places come first. Add distraction with a long line so the dog can succeed. Pay the recall when the dog arrives. Use management, such as doors and lines, while the cue is being rebuilt.

How much exercise does an adolescent dog need?

Daily sniff walks, a training game, and a chew fit most dogs better than long sessions of hard fetching on pavement. Build in a midday rest. Limping, refusal to jump into the car, or a personality change should be checked by a veterinarian.
