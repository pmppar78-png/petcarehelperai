Small Animal Nutrition Guide

Proper nutrition is the single most important factor in determining the health and longevity of your small pet. Each small animal species has evolved with dramatically different dietary needs, and feeding the wrong diet is one of the most common causes of illness and premature death in pocket pets. This guide provides species-specific nutritional information for rabbits, guinea pigs, hamsters, ferrets, chinchillas, rats, mice, hedgehogs, and gerbils, covering everything from daily staples to safe treats and dangerous foods to avoid.

Understanding Small Animal Dietary Categories

Small animals fall into several dietary categories, and understanding which group your pet belongs to is essential for proper feeding:

  • Herbivores (plant-eaters): Rabbits, guinea pigs, chinchillas. Require a hay-based diet with fresh vegetables and limited pellets.
  • Omnivores (plant and animal eaters): Hamsters, rats, mice, gerbils. Need a mixed diet of grains, seeds, vegetables, and some animal protein.
  • Obligate carnivores: Ferrets. Require a high-protein, high-fat, meat-based diet similar to cats.
  • Insectivores: Hedgehogs. Need a diet primarily of insects and high-quality cat food, supplemented with vegetables and fruits.

Rabbit Nutrition

A rabbit's diet is deceptively simple but must be precisely balanced. The majority of health problems in pet rabbits stem directly from improper diet.

The Ideal Rabbit Diet

  • 80% unlimited timothy hay: The absolute foundation of rabbit nutrition. Hay provides the long-stem fiber essential for proper gut motility and continuous dental wear. Without it, both GI stasis and dental disease become inevitable. Orchard grass and meadow hay are acceptable alternatives. Offer fresh hay in unlimited quantities daily.
  • 10-15% fresh leafy greens: Approximately 1 packed cup of greens per 2 pounds of body weight daily. Safe options include romaine lettuce, cilantro, parsley, basil, bok choy, carrot tops, dandelion greens, endive, and watercress. Avoid iceberg lettuce (no nutritional value, causes diarrhea) and limit high-oxalate greens like spinach and kale.
  • 5% plain timothy pellets: About 1/4 cup per 5 pounds of body weight. Choose plain timothy-based pellets without added seeds, dried fruits, or colorful pieces. Oxbow Essentials Adult Rabbit Food is the veterinary gold standard.
  • Treats (very limited): 1-2 tablespoons of fruit per day maximum. Safe options include banana, apple (no seeds), blueberries, strawberries, and raspberries. Fruit is sugar-rich and should be an occasional reward, not a daily staple.
  • Fresh water: Available at all times via a heavy ceramic bowl or bottle. Bowls are preferred as they encourage more natural drinking posture.

Young Rabbits (Under 7 Months)

Baby rabbits should receive alfalfa hay and alfalfa-based pellets, which provide the extra calcium and protein needed for growth. Transition gradually to timothy hay around 7 months of age. Introduce vegetables slowly starting at 12 weeks, one type at a time, watching for digestive upset.

Guinea Pig Nutrition

Guinea pig nutrition is similar to rabbits with one critical difference: guinea pigs, like humans, cannot synthesize their own vitamin C and must receive it daily through their diet or supplementation.

The Ideal Guinea Pig Diet

  • 80% unlimited timothy hay: Same as rabbits, hay is the essential foundation. Provide fresh hay daily in a hay rack or pile.
  • Fresh vegetables with vitamin C: 1 cup of vegetables per pig daily, emphasizing vitamin C-rich options. Bell peppers (especially red and yellow) are the single best source. Other good options include kale, parsley, mustard greens, broccoli, and Brussels sprouts.
  • Timothy pellets with vitamin C: 1/8 cup per pig per day. Choose pellets specifically formulated for guinea pigs with added stabilized vitamin C. Oxbow Essentials Guinea Pig Food is highly recommended.
  • Vitamin C supplementation: 25-50 mg per day per pig. Bell peppers alone can provide this, but a liquid vitamin C supplement added to food (not water, as it degrades quickly) provides insurance.
  • Treats: Small amounts of fruit (strawberries, blueberries, apple, melon) a few times per week. Limit to 1-2 small pieces per serving.

Hamster Nutrition

Hamsters are omnivores with fast metabolisms. Their dietary needs vary slightly between Syrian (larger) and dwarf hamster species.

The Ideal Hamster Diet

  • Quality seed mix or pellet food: A balanced commercial hamster food serves as the base diet. Seed mixes offer variety but some hamsters selectively eat only fatty seeds. Pellet foods ensure balanced nutrition but are less enriching. Many owners offer a combination of both.
  • Fresh vegetables: Small amounts every other day. Broccoli, cucumber, carrot, spinach, bell pepper, and zucchini are safe options. Introduce one at a time and remove uneaten portions within a few hours.
  • Protein sources: Small amounts of cooked chicken, boiled egg, plain cooked tofu, mealworms, or crickets 2-3 times per week. Protein is especially important for pregnant or nursing hamsters.
  • Timothy hay: While not the dietary staple it is for herbivores, hay provides beneficial fiber and nesting material. Offer a small amount regularly.
  • Treats: Small pieces of fruit (apple, banana, strawberry) once or twice per week. Seeds such as pumpkin, sunflower, and flax make excellent training treats in small quantities.

Campbell's Dwarf Hamsters and Diabetes

Campbell's dwarf hamsters are genetically prone to diabetes. Limit all sugary foods including fruit, corn, and peas. Avoid honey-coated treats entirely. Monitor for excessive drinking and urination, which may indicate the onset of diabetes.

Ferret Nutrition

Ferrets are obligate carnivores with short digestive tracts designed to process meat rapidly. They require high-protein, high-fat, low-fiber, and low-carbohydrate diets.

  • Primary diet: High-quality ferret kibble or premium kitten food with at least 36% protein and 20% fat from animal sources. The first three ingredients should be named animal proteins (chicken, turkey, lamb). Avoid fish-based foods.
  • Avoid carbohydrates: Ferrets cannot efficiently digest carbohydrates, fruits, or vegetables. High-carb diets contribute to insulinoma, the most common ferret cancer. Never feed grains, corn, peas, or sugary treats.
  • Raw diet option: Some experienced ferret owners feed a balanced raw prey-model diet of raw meaty bones, organ meat, and muscle meat. This requires careful research and veterinary guidance to ensure nutritional completeness.
  • Treats: Small pieces of cooked meat, freeze-dried raw meat treats, or a small amount of salmon oil. Commercial ferret treats often contain sugar and grains and should be avoided.
  • Feeding frequency: Ferrets have fast metabolisms and should have food available at all times. They eat small, frequent meals throughout the day.

Chinchilla Nutrition

  • Unlimited timothy hay: The cornerstone of chinchilla nutrition, just as with rabbits and guinea pigs. Essential for dental wear and digestive function.
  • Timothy pellets: 1-2 tablespoons per day of a high-quality chinchilla pellet such as Oxbow Essentials Chinchilla Food. Avoid mixes with dried fruit, seeds, or nuts.
  • No fresh fruits or vegetables: Unlike rabbits and guinea pigs, chinchillas should not receive fresh produce. Their digestive systems are extremely sensitive, and fresh foods cause dangerous bloat and diarrhea.
  • Treats (very limited): A single dried rosehip, a plain rolled oat or two, or a very small piece of dried herb. One raisin per week maximum. Chinchillas are prone to dental disease and diabetes, so sugar must be minimized.

Rat and Mouse Nutrition

  • Rats: A high-quality lab block (Oxbow Essentials Regal Rat, Mazuri Rat and Mouse) as the staple diet, supplemented with small portions of fresh vegetables, cooked grains, and occasional protein. Rats are prone to obesity and tumors, so caloric intake should be monitored.
  • Mice: A quality mouse pellet or lab block, supplemented with small seeds, grains, and occasional fresh vegetables. Mice have even faster metabolisms than rats and need food available at all times.
  • Fresh foods for rats: Broccoli, peas, banana, berries, cooked sweet potato, cooked pasta, and scrambled egg are all excellent options offered in small quantities.

Hedgehog Nutrition

  • Primary diet: High-quality cat food (not dog food) with at least 30% protein and less than 15% fat, or a dedicated hedgehog food. Choose a formula with named meat as the first ingredient.
  • Insects: Mealworms, crickets, and waxworms provide essential protein and enrichment. Offer 3-5 insects several times per week. Gut-load insects before feeding for maximum nutrition.
  • Vegetables and fruits: Small amounts of cooked sweet potato, green beans, peas, bell pepper, and occasional berries or melon. Avoid grapes, raisins, and citrus fruits.
  • Portion control: Hedgehogs are highly prone to obesity. Offer 1-2 tablespoons of food per night and adjust based on weight. Regular weighing helps monitor body condition.

Foods Dangerous to Small Animals

Toxic and Dangerous Foods — Never Feed These

  • Chocolate: Toxic to all small animals. Contains theobromine and caffeine.
  • Onions, garlic, and leeks: Cause hemolytic anemia in rabbits, guinea pigs, and other species.
  • Avocado: Contains persin, toxic to rabbits, guinea pigs, and birds.
  • Rhubarb: Contains oxalic acid, toxic to all small animals.
  • Raw beans: Contain lectins that are toxic when uncooked.
  • Iceberg lettuce: Contains lactucarium, which can be harmful in large amounts, and has virtually no nutritional value.
  • Seeds from fruits: Apple seeds, cherry pits, and similar contain cyanide compounds.
  • Sugary human foods: Candy, cookies, bread, and processed snacks cause dental disease, obesity, and diabetes.

Water and Hydration

Fresh, clean water must be available at all times for all small animal species. Both water bottles and heavy ceramic bowls work well, though bowls are more natural for most species and encourage better hydration. Change water daily and clean the container regularly to prevent algae and bacterial growth. In hot weather, monitor water consumption closely, as dehydration can develop rapidly in small animals.
