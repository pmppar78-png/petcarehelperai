Cat Obesity Prevention

Managing overweight cats including health risks, ideal weight ranges, feeding strategies, portion control, and exercise ideas for indoor cats.

A heavy cat is a medical patient

Obesity in cats is extra body fat, scored on the same nine-point body-condition idea the World Small Animal Veterinary Association publishes, where five is ideal and eight or nine is heavy. Ribs that you cannot feel under a fat pad, a back that is broad and flat, a waist that has disappeared when you look from above, and a sagging abdominal apron are the usual findings. A number on the scale matters only next to that score and next to last year's weight. A cat can be "only a pound up" and already too fat for that frame. Indoor, neutered cats are the typical patients, because the hunting budget disappeared and the food bowl did not. Some breeds look broad even when lean. The score of the ribs and the waist still wins over a breed reputation.

The diseases that follow show up in ordinary exams. Diabetes mellitus is more likely in overweight cats, and weight loss is part of diabetic remission conversations the veterinarian leads. Osteoarthritis hurts more when every jump carries extra mass. Grooming fails, so mats and skin infections appear on the back the tongue cannot reach. Anesthesia and surgery become harder. The sharpest risk of a careless diet is hepatic lipidosis: a fat cat who stops eating mobilizes fat faster than the liver can handle and becomes jaundiced and weak. That is why "just feed less until the bowl is empty" is a bad plan, and why any diet that makes the cat refuse food gets stopped the same day.

Confirm that the weight is fat

A belly that enlarged over a weekend may be fluid, a tumor, or a bladder problem, and it needs an exam before anyone congratulates a diet. The veterinarian palpates, may tape a weight and a body-condition score into the record, and runs bloodwork when drinking and urinating have increased. Diabetes, kidney disease, and hyperthyroidism change appetite in different directions. Hypothyroidism, the classic weight-gain hormone problem of dogs, is rare in cats. An overweight diabetic cat who keeps gaining despite insulin needs a conversation about acromegaly and about whether the insulin plan is working. Neither of those is solved by a smaller scoop alone. If the cat is simply fat, with a normal exam and normal screening labs, you can start a planned loss.

Stop the diet if eating stops

A cat on a weight-loss plan who skips meals, vomits, or looks jaundiced should be seen promptly. Hepatic lipidosis is the emergency hiding inside a well-meant cut in calories. Do not withhold food to "reset" a cat, and do not offer a dog weight-loss drug, a human diet pill, or a stimulant. Those products can kill a cat. Call the clinic, restart the previous amount if they tell you to, and bring the food log.

A slow plan with a scale

The veterinarian sets a target and a weekly rate, often discussed around one percent of body weight a week, slower when the cat is older or already has liver concerns. Faster loss is how lipidosis starts. The math begins with a measured cup or, better, a gram scale in the kitchen. Food bags change calorie density. "Half a cup" of two different foods can be hundreds of calories apart. Treats, pill pastes, and the other cat's leftovers count. A food toy that makes the cat work for the measured portion replaces a bored hour. Play that mimics a hunt, a short chase and then the meal, uses the muscles diabetes and arthritis will otherwise waste. Two or three short games beat a single exhausted sprint.

Therapeutic diets exist for weight loss and for diabetes. The veterinarian picks one when the cat's labs and preferences support it. A grocery food can also work if the calories are truly counted. There is no brand ranking that replaces the scale. In a multi-cat home, feed the heavy cat in a separate place, use a microchip feeder if you have one, or put the slim cat's food up where only that cat jumps. Free-choice dry food all day is the habit that usually has to end. Water stays available. A cat who cries at 5 a.m. can get a measured portion from an automatic feeder overnight so the total for the day still matches the plan. Recheck weight every two to four weeks at the clinic or on the same home scale. If the weight does not move for a month, the portion is still too large or someone else is feeding. If the weight drops faster than the veterinarian approved, add calories back. Muscle loss is a failure, even if the number looks flattering.

  • Feel the ribs weekly. You want them padded, and you want to find them without pressing hard.
  • Write down every edible thing, including dental treats and the cream on a pill.
  • Weigh the cat on the same scale, morning, before breakfast.
  • Book a recheck if drinking increases, because diabetes can appear during a weight change.
  • Keep the cat's jumps possible with steps or a ramp if arthritis is already in the record.

After the goal weight

Maintenance calories are higher than weight-loss calories and still lower than the free-feed habit that created the problem. The veterinarian recalculates when the body-condition score is where you agreed. Keep the kitchen scale. A holiday week of extras is how a cat regains the apron. Dental disease, pain, and nausea will also change intake, so a cat who suddenly loses more weight after months of stability needs an exam. Unplanned loss is a different disease from the plan you just finished.

What you will pay

The valuable purchases are an exam, screening bloodwork, a kitchen gram scale, and the food you will actually measure. Rechecks are smaller visits. A hepatic lipidosis hospitalization, if a crash diet goes wrong, costs more than a year of planned food. Diabetic monitoring, if it becomes necessary, is its own budget and is helped by the same weight loss. Ask the clinic to write the daily calorie number and the gram amount of the specific bag you are buying, so a new bag with a new calorie density does not silently undo the math.

Questions about cat weight

How fast can my cat lose weight?

Slowly, on a rate your veterinarian sets, often near one percent of body weight each week. Faster loss raises the chance of hepatic lipidosis, especially if the cat stops eating entirely. Use a gram scale, count treats, and recheck. If the cat skips meals, call the clinic and pause the cut. A dog diet pill or a human stimulant has no place in this plan.

Is a big belly always fat?

A belly that sags gradually, with ribs that are hard to feel, is usually fat. A belly that blows up over a day or two can be fluid, a mass, or another abdominal crisis and needs an exam before a diet starts. Increased thirst in a heavy cat is a reason to test for diabetes. Hypothyroidism, common as a weight-gain story in dogs, is rare in cats, so do not wait on a thyroid pill to do this work.

What if I have two cats and only one is heavy?

Feed them apart. Microchip feeders, separate rooms, or a high perch for the slim cat's bowl can protect the measured portion. Free-choice kibble in a shared bowl defeats the plan. Write down which human feeds which meal so a second breakfast does not appear out of kindness. Both cats still need an exam, because the slim one may be losing weight from disease while the heavy one is simply overfed.
