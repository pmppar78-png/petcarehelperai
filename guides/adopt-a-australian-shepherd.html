Australian Shepherd

Adopting an Australian Shepherd: breed-specific rescues, what to expect, adoption costs, and preparing your home for a rescued Australian Shepherd.

Finding an Australian Shepherd to Adopt

Australian Shepherds are surrendered to rescue when their energy and intelligence overwhelm owners who expected a calmer dog. The breed's popularity on social media means people buy Aussie puppies for their looks without understanding that this is a working dog that needs a job. By age one, an under-stimulated Aussie has usually remodeled the house.

Aussie Rescue and Placement Helpline (ARPH) is the largest breed rescue network, coordinating placements across the country. Regional groups affiliated with the Australian Shepherd Club of America also place dogs year-round.

Health Awareness: Australian Shepherds carry genetic predispositions to hip dysplasia, epilepsy, cataracts. Not every individual will be affected, but knowing these risks lets you work with your vet to establish an appropriate screening schedule. Early detection changes outcomes significantly for most of these conditions.

Breed-Specific Rescues

Breed rescues foster Aussies in homes where their herding behaviors, noise sensitivity, and energy levels can be honestly evaluated. Ask the foster family specifically about the dog's herding instinct -- many Aussies nip at children's heels, chase bikes, and try to herd other pets, which needs to be managed from day one.

  • Size: medium (40-65 lbs)
  • Energy Level: High
  • Shedding: Heavy
  • Common Health Issues: Hip Dysplasia, Epilepsy, Cataracts
  • Lifespan: 12-15 yrs

Shelter Adoption

Shelters often have Aussie mixes, and many carry the same herding drive as purebreds. Ask to observe the dog's behavior on a walk -- an Aussie that fixates on moving objects (cars, joggers, other dogs) is showing herding instinct that will need training and management at home.

Shelter adoption fees typically run $150-$400. Breed rescues charge $300-$600 and usually include MDR1 gene testing (which identifies dangerous drug sensitivities common in the breed), basic vetting, and a behavioral assessment.

What to Expect

Aussies are working dogs that happen to live in pet homes. They have an almost compulsive need to be doing something, and they notice everything in their environment. They form intense bonds with their owner, often choosing one person as "their human." They shed heavily, vocalize when excited or frustrated, and will stare at you with those striking eyes until you give them something to do.

  • Aim for 1-2 hours of activity daily, mixing walks with play and training to keep things engaging
  • Feed a high-quality diet formulated for medium breed dogs (800–1,200 calories/day)
  • Maintain a daily brushing grooming routine
  • Schedule breed-appropriate health screenings for hip dysplasia
  • Securing pet insurance before any health issues arise gives you the broadest coverage at the most reasonable cost

Preparing Your Home

A securely fenced yard is strongly recommended. Aussies are agile enough to clear four-foot fences and smart enough to find weak spots. Six-foot fencing is the standard recommendation. If you do not have a yard, you need a solid plan for daily off-leash exercise in a safe, enclosed area.

Remove or protect anything at tail height. Aussies have long, enthusiastic tails that sweep coffee tables clean. Move breakables up and invest in spill-proof water bowls.

Stock up on grooming supplies before your Aussie arrives. Their double coat needs brushing several times a week, and during the twice-yearly coat blow, you will fill a grocery bag with fur in a single brushing session. A high-velocity dryer is a worthwhile investment if you plan to groom at home.

First Days Home

Aussies need a decompression period, but they also need structure from the start. Do not skip exercise during the first week thinking the dog needs rest -- an Aussie with pent-up energy in an unfamiliar house will find destructive outlets. Establish a walking and play routine immediately.

Watch for herding behavior directed at family members, especially children who run and squeal. An Aussie nipping at a toddler's ankles is not being aggressive -- it is herding. Redirect this behavior to appropriate outlets like fetch, tug, and structured games.

Book a vet visit within the first week. Ask about MDR1 gene testing if the rescue did not provide it (certain common medications including ivermectin are dangerous for MDR1-affected dogs), a hip evaluation, and an eye exam. These three screens cover the most significant Australian Shepherd health risks and should be part of any new-owner baseline.

Cost of Australian Shepherd Ownership

  • Annual food costs: $400–$800 for high-quality dog food
  • Veterinary care: $300–$700 annually for routine visits, plus potential emergency costs
  • Grooming: $45–70 per professional session (daily brushing home grooming recommended)
  • Pet insurance: $35–55/month for comprehensive coverage
  • Supplies and toys: $200–$500 annually for bedding, toys, leashes, and other essentials

Hip and Joint Health Management

Hip dysplasia — a polygenic condition where the femoral head fails to fit properly within the acetabulum — is a documented concern in the Australian Shepherd. The Orthopedic Foundation for Animals (OFA) maintains a breed-specific database showing dysplasia prevalence rates, and the PennHIP evaluation method provides a distraction index that can predict hip laxity as early as 16 weeks of age. Even in smaller-framed Australian Shepherds, the biomechanical stress of daily activity accumulates over the breed's 12-15 yrs lifespan. Joint supplements containing glucosamine hydrochloride, chondroitin sulfate, and omega-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA) have demonstrated clinical benefit in peer-reviewed veterinary orthopedic literature when started before symptomatic onset.
