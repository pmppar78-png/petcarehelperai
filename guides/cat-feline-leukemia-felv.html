Feline Leukemia Virus (FeLV)

Practical guide to FeLV in cats covering transmission, testing, symptoms, treatment options, prevention, and managing FeLV-positive cats.

FeLV moves through close, friendly contact

Feline leukemia virus is a retrovirus that cats pass in saliva, and often in milk, urine, and feces as well. Mutual grooming, shared food and water dishes, nursing, and birth are enough. A bite can transmit it too, yet the everyday pattern families need to picture is friendly contact, the opposite social picture from the deep-bite story of feline immunodeficiency virus. A combo test at adoption reports the two viruses with different methods: FeLV screening looks for viral antigen, while FIV screening looks for antibody. A positive line on either one deserves its own confirmation plan. Mixing the explanations up is how a cat gets isolated for the wrong reason or, worse, left in a group where saliva is still being shared.

Age and immune response decide what the virus does next. Some cats clear an infection after exposure. Some contain it so well that antigen disappears from the blood even though virus remains hidden in the body, a regressive pattern a PCR may still detect. Some stay progressively infected, antigen-positive, and those are the cats who become anemic, pick up repeated infections, and face a higher risk of lymphoma and leukemia. Kittens and cats in crowded housing are the most vulnerable to the progressive path. A single indoor cat with no new housemates has a much smaller chance of ever meeting the virus.

Testing that matches the biology

Point-of-care ELISA antigen tests are the usual screen, and the American Association of Feline Practitioners recommends testing new cats, sick cats, and cats about to be vaccinated for FeLV or mixed into a group. A positive ELISA in a healthy cat should be confirmed with a second method, such as immunofluorescence or PCR, because rare false positives happen and the consequences of the label are large. A negative test on a cat who was just exposed does not finish the question. Antibody has nothing to do with this screen, and antigen can take weeks to appear, so the veterinarian times a repeat. Kittens can be infected late in the sequence of tests. Retest them on the schedule the clinic writes down. FeLV vaccination does not flip an antigen test to positive, which is a useful difference from the old FIV vaccine story. If two tests disagree, pause before you rehome the cat or before you introduce a housemate. Discordant results are a lab conversation, sometimes with a second blood draw.

Illness in a progressively infected cat

  • Pale gums, weakness, and an anemia that may be severe.
  • Fever, poor healing, and abscesses or respiratory infections that keep returning.
  • Weight loss, chronic diarrhea, or a mouth that stays inflamed.
  • Lumps, chest fluid, or a decline that makes the veterinarian look for lymphoma.
  • Reproductive failure in a breeding cat, including sick or fading kittens.

Many of these signs have other causes. Anemia in a cat is also a story about fleas, blood parasites, kidney disease, and immune disease. The FeLV result changes the odds and the contagion plan. It does not spare the cat a real workup. Progressive infection has ended within months for some cats and has stayed quiet for years in others. The veterinarian will talk about the form of infection, the current blood counts, and what quality of life looks like in this house. There is no product you can order that reliably clears progressive FeLV. Care is monitoring, prompt treatment of the infections that appear, and a decision about any chemotherapy if lymphoma is actually diagnosed. Those drugs and doses stay with the clinician.

Same-week signs in a known positive cat

White or yellow gums, breathing effort, collapse, or a cat who stops eating should be seen promptly. Anemia and chest lymphoma both present as a cat who suddenly cannot cope. Do not start a steroid left over from another pet while you wait, because steroids can worsen an infection and can also change what the veterinarian sees on the next blood sample. Bring the previous FeLV tests with you so the emergency team does not repeat the wrong assay.

Households, vaccines, and separation

A progressively infected cat should live indoors and apart from FeLV-negative cats. Friendly grooming is the risk, so "they get along" is the reason to separate dishes and sleeping spots. A regressive or abortive infection is interpreted by the veterinarian before anyone is asked to leave the group. In a peaceful home, human family members are safe. The virus does not infect people. Ordinary handwashing after the litter box is the hygiene that matters.

A vaccine exists, and it is a risk decision. Cats who go outdoors, cats who live with a positive housemate despite your best layout, and cats entering a shelter or foster group are the usual candidates. A strictly indoor cat in a tested, closed home may not benefit enough to justify the injection, and the veterinarian can say so for that patient. Vaccination does not replace testing. A cat should be tested before the vaccine so you are protecting a negative animal. Boosters follow the product the clinic stocks and the lifestyle you actually provide. No puppy series belongs on this calendar. If a positive cat develops a mass at an injection site, tell the veterinarian. Any lump that persists should be measured, whatever vaccine or other injection preceded it.

Foster and shelter introductions need a written test. Combining an untested adult with kittens is how a litter becomes antigen-positive. If you already share a home with a positive cat and a negative cat, ask the veterinarian to map separate rooms, litter boxes, and a vaccine plan for the negative cat while you decide whether the arrangement can be made truly separate. Hope is a weak barrier against saliva.

Money and planning

Screening is inexpensive next to the cost of a wrong introduction. Confirmation, a CBC, and a chemistry panel are the reasonable spend when a line turns positive. Later costs follow the disease that appears: a transfusion or a lymphoma workup is a different order of magnitude from the snap test. Ask for the confirmatory plan in writing before you make a permanent decision about a newly adopted cat. Some clinics include FeLV antigen testing in a new-kitten visit. Use that visit.

Questions about feline leukemia virus

How is FeLV transmission different from FIV?

FeLV spreads through saliva during ordinary closeness: grooming, shared bowls, nursing, and birth, as well as through bites. FIV is mainly a deep-bite virus, so peaceful housemates are a smaller risk for FIV than for FeLV. The screening tests differ too. FeLV tests look for antigen, and FIV tests look for antibody. A veterinarian should confirm each positive and tell you whether the cats can keep sharing a room.

Can a vaccinated cat still get FeLV?

Vaccination lowers risk and does not make exposure impossible. Test before you vaccinate, keep boosters matched to the life the cat really lives, and still separate a progressively infected cat from negative cats. The vaccine does not make the antigen screening test read positive. A negative cat who was just groomed by an unknown new housemate should be retested on the veterinarian's timeline.

Does a positive test mean the cat will be sick immediately?

Some cats clear the virus, some contain it, and some stay progressively infected and become anemic or develop cancer months or years later. Confirmation and a conversation about progressive versus regressive infection matter more than the first line on a screen. There is no mail-order cure for progressive infection. Indoor care, prompt visits for new illness, and a plan to protect negative housemates are the parts you control.
