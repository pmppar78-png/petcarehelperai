Cat Pregnancy and Queening Guide

Guide to cat pregnancy covering signs, nutrition, preparing for kittens, labor stages, and neonatal kitten care for the first weeks of life.

Length of a cat pregnancy

Queens usually give birth about 63 to 67 days after a successful mating, and a few healthy litters land a couple of days on either side of that span. Cats are induced ovulators, so the mating itself helps set the clock, yet a queen can be bred across several days and you may not know which pairing took. A veterinarian confirms pregnancy by gently palpating a cooperative queen around the third to fourth week, or by ultrasound. Radiographs late in pregnancy, once the kittens' bones have mineralized, can suggest how many kittens to expect. An early film will undercount because the skeletons are still invisible.

Nipples pink up early. The belly rounds in the second half, and many queens become affectionate or, the opposite, newly private. Appetite climbs. Feed a growth or all-life-stages food the clinic agrees with, in several smaller meals, because a crowded abdomen leaves less room for one large plate. Keep fresh water in more than one spot. Skip supplements, essential oils, and any flea product that was not prescribed for a pregnant cat. Many permethrin dog products are dangerous to cats at any time, and pregnancy is a poor moment to experiment.

A queen who was gaining and then stops eating, who strains without producing a kitten, or who passes dark green or bloody fluid before labor is underway needs a same-day call. Fever, hiding with a tense belly, or a sudden collapse can mean an infected uterus or a kitten in trouble. Indoor-only housing during the last weeks prevents a surprise litter in a neighbor's shed.

Preparing the queening space

A few days before you expect birth, offer a box in a closet or a quiet bathroom. A clean laundry basket or a carrier with the door open, lined with an old towel, is enough. She may ignore your choice and pick the back of a closet. If the spot is safe, let her have it and move hazards away. If she chooses a laundry pile that will be washed, relocate the towels and sit with her until she accepts the box. Gather a digital thermometer you already know how to use, clean towels, unwaxed floss if a cord bleeds, and the emergency number. Scales that measure in grams or ounces help you see whether kittens gain.

Ask the clinic in advance what they want you to do if labor stalls, and whether this queen has a pelvis or a breed shape that makes a cesarean more likely. Most domestic cats deliver without surgery. A queen who had a previous cesarean, a kitten already known to be oversized, or a fracture history in the pelvis should have that plan written down before the first contraction. Children and other pets stay out of the room. Dogs are curious and can stress her into moving the kittens.

What labor looks like in a queen

Restlessness, nesting, loud purring, and a refusal of food often open the day. She may pace and lick herself. Contractions can look like quiet squatting. The first kitten may arrive inside a membrane. A confident queen tears it, licks the face, and severs the cord. If the membrane stays over the nose, you peel it off and rub the kitten dry. Place the kitten at a nipple once it is breathing. Many litters arrive with short gaps, sometimes only minutes, and the whole birth may finish in a few hours. A long pause with the queen sleeping calmly and nursing can be normal between kittens. A long pause with hard straining is different.

Call the veterinarian if she has strong contractions for 30 to 60 minutes and no kitten appears, if a kitten is visible at the vulva and does not emerge with the next pushes, or if she seems exhausted and more kittens are still expected based on imaging. Foul discharge, open-mouth breathing, or gums that look white are emergency signs. After the last kitten she should relax and let them nurse. Continued crying, digging, or a remaining hard belly needs a recheck so a stuck kitten is found before morning.

Kittens should feel warm and should gain a little weight every day. A kitten who slides off the nipple, feels cool, or cries without settling needs warmth first and then a chance to nurse. Ask the clinic before syringe-feeding or bottle-feeding, and use a commercial milk replacer made for kittens if they tell you to supplement. Cow's milk and homemade mixtures cause diarrhea. The queen eats a great deal while nursing and needs the litter box cleaned constantly, because a dirty box will push her to hold urine.

The days after the kittens arrive

Bloody or dark discharge can continue and should trend lighter. A rotten smell, a fever, or a queen who ignores the kittens needs a visit. Mastitis feels like a hot, painful mammary gland, and the milk may look abnormal. Eclampsia is less commonly discussed in cats than in small dogs, yet tremors, stiffness, or seizures in a nursing queen are still an emergency trip. Do not offer calcium tablets while you drive. Let the clinic treat it.

Keep the room warm and draft-free. Handle kittens briefly once the queen trusts you, so they learn human scent. Weigh them at the same time each day and write the numbers down. A kitten who fails to gain for a day gets a closer look at latch and at the queen's milk, and a call if the next weight is also flat. Plan vaccines, deworming, and weaning with the clinic as the kittens approach four weeks and start showing interest in the queen's food. Weaning is a gradual offer of gruel, and the queen's calories come back down only after nursing fades. Spay the queen after she has recovered, on the clinic's timeline, if a breeding program is outside your plan. An accidental litter is a medical event, and the next one is preventable.

Early pregnancy clues worth a phone call

A clear or pinkish discharge can show up late and still be ordinary, while green, brown, or foul fluid at any point before active labor is a call. Repeated vomiting, a queen who hides and will not rise for food, or a belly that seems painful when she is picked up should be seen. Roundworms and other parasites are common, and the clinic chooses a dewormer that fits pregnancy. Flea control is the same story: bring the product you already own and ask before you apply it. A queen who goes outside can pick up a new mating and a disease in the same week, so indoor housing is part of prenatal care.

Write the breeding dates you know, the tom's name if you have it, and any prior difficult birth on a card for the clinic. If you did not plan this litter, say that plainly. The veterinarian can still outline nutrition, a due window, and what an emergency looks like. An unplanned litter deserves the same whelping box and the same after-hours plan as a deliberate one.

  • Expect birth about nine weeks after mating, with ultrasound or a late radiograph to confirm kittens.
  • Feed a clinic-approved growth diet in small meals and skip unprescribed flea products.
  • Stage a quiet box, towels, and the after-hours number before the due window.
  • Clear a membrane from a kitten's face if the queen does not.
  • Call when hard straining runs 30 to 60 minutes without a kitten, or a kitten stays visible and stuck.
  • Weigh kittens daily and ask before any bottle supplement.
  • Treat seizures in a nursing queen as an emergency.
  • Plan spay, vaccines, and weaning with the clinic once the litter is stable.

Questions about cat pregnancy and queening

How long are cats pregnant?

Most queens deliver about 63 to 67 days after mating. A veterinarian can confirm kittens by palpation or ultrasound and can use a late radiograph to estimate the count once bones are visible.

What does normal queening look like?

Nesting, pacing, and purring often come first. Kittens may arrive minutes apart inside a membrane the queen usually removes. Calm nursing pauses can separate kittens. Hard straining without progress is a different pattern and should be reported.

When does cat labor need a veterinarian?

Call for 30 to 60 minutes of strong contractions with no kitten, a kitten lodged at the vulva, foul discharge, white gums, or a queen who still looks heavily pregnant and exhausted. Tremors or seizures while she is nursing are an emergency.
