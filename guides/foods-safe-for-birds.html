Safe and Toxic

This page is educational reference material for preparing questions. A licensed veterinarian examines the animal, makes the diagnosis, and sets the treatment.

Owner-focused guide to safe fruits, vegetables, seeds, and grains for pet birds plus foods that are toxic including avocado, chocolate, and caffeine.

A pellet base and a produce list you can defend

Companion parrots, cockatiels, and budgies do best when a formulated pellet is the main food and fresh produce is a daily addition. Seed can stay a training reward in a measured pinch. The produce list below supplements that kind of diet. Produce alone fails as the whole menu. Wash everything, chop it so a foot can hold it, and pull uneaten fresh food the same day so bacteria do not bloom in a warm cage. Offer new items in the morning when the bird is hungry. A bird who only sorts out the millet will need the millet reduced gradually while you watch weight on a gram scale.

Vegetables that most psittacines can have include carrot, bell pepper of any color, broccoli, kale, dandelion greens, squash, sweet potato cooked soft, green beans, and cucumber. Peppers and cooked sweet potato support vitamin A, which seed-only birds often lack. Fruits that can be occasional, because they are sugary, include apple with seeds removed, blueberries, mango with the pit removed, papaya, melon, and berries. Apple seeds and all stone-fruit pits go in the trash. They contain compounds that release cyanide. A thin slice is a training reward. A bowl of fruit is a sugar load for a bird who barely flies.

Foods that injure birds

Avocado is unsafe. Persin in the flesh, skin, and pit has been tied to heart failure and respiratory collapse in parrots and other companion birds. Guacamole, avocado toast, and a smoothie on the desk are all exposures. Chocolate brings theobromine and caffeine. Coffee, tea, and soda add more caffeine. Allium vegetables such as onion and garlic harm avian red cells. Alcohol can kill a small bird in a sip. Xylitol-sweetened yogurt, gum, and baked goods do not belong on a play stand. Salted chips, fried food, and heavily buttered popcorn add sodium and fat a caged bird cannot spend.

Fruit pits from cherries, peaches, plums, nectarines, and apricots are both a cyanide concern and a size that can be swallowed. Tomato leaves and potato eyes are poor offerings even when the ripe fruit or a plain cooked potato piece might be acceptable in a tiny amount. Unidentified yard mushrooms stay out of every bird chop. Houseplants and mystery leaves stay outside the cage. If you cannot name the plant, it does not go on the skewer. Raw dried beans can be toxic. Cooked plain beans, if your avian veterinarian agrees, are a different food. When you are unsure, skip the item and offer pepper or carrot.

If a forbidden food was already eaten

Move the food, place the bird in a carrier in a quiet warm room, and call an avian veterinarian or an emergency clinic that sees birds. A poison hotline can help identify the food: ASPCA Animal Poison Control at 888-426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline at 855-764-7661. Bring the label, the plant, or a photo. . Birds aspirate easily, and there is no safe home method. Emergency signs include fluffed feathers, tail bobbing, vomiting, seizures, and a bird who has fallen to the cage floor. Go even if the amount looked like a crumb of avocado or chocolate. Small bodies leave little margin.

While you travel, keep the carrier covered enough for calm and open enough for air. Do not offer more food or water if the bird is vomiting or barely responsive, unless the clinic tells you to. Tell them the species, the approximate weight, and the time. If several birds shared a chop that contained avocado, say so. One cutting board can expose every bird in the room. Afterward, ask what to watch for overnight and whether droppings should be photographed. Black, red, or very sparse droppings that the food color cannot explain need a recheck.

A daily pattern that stays boring on purpose

Morning: pellets in a clean dish, plus a small chop of two or three vegetables from the safe list. Training uses a few pellets or a tiny piece of pepper. A millet spray that stays up all week is a meal, so retire that habit. Evening: remove soft food, wash the dish, and refresh water. Weigh the bird a few mornings a week before breakfast. A downward trend means you pause any diet experiment and call an avian veterinarian. Lories need a nectar-style diet made for them. A parrot pellet and a carrot do not meet that species. Chickens and ducks in a backyard have different lists again. This page is about companion psittacines. Ask before you export it to poultry.

People food from the table teaches begging and hides onion in sauces. If you want the bird involved in family meals, offer a separate dish of the vegetables you already washed for the chop. Write the forbidden list on the cage: avocado, chocolate, caffeine, onion, garlic, alcohol, pits, and xylitol. Guests can follow a list faster than a lecture. The safe list is long enough to rotate a week of color. Rotation is how you avoid a bird who will eat only corn and seed, and how vitamin A intake stays more than a theory.

  • Use pellets as the base and add washed vegetables such as pepper, carrot, broccoli, kale, and cooked sweet potato.
  • Treat fruit as an occasional slice, with apple seeds and all pits removed.
  • Keep avocado away because persin can cause heart and breathing failure.
  • Also forbid chocolate, caffeine, onion, garlic, alcohol, xylitol, and fruit pits.
  • Call an avian veterinarian after any of those exposures. .
  • Remove fresh food the same day and weigh the bird regularly.
  • Feed lories a diet made for lories. A parrot chop by itself misses how they eat.
  • Post the forbidden list where guests can see it.

Chop day and guest rules

Prepare the bird's vegetables on a clean board before anyone starts onion or avocado on the same surface. Cross-contamination is a real exposure. Wash the board, the knife, and your hands before you skewer pepper and carrot. Apple seeds go into a lidded trash the bird cannot reach, along with every pit. If avocado was already on the board and you are unsure the board was washed, throw out the bird's chop and start over. A crumb of persin is enough to justify a call to an avian veterinarian. Chocolate tasting at the same counter has the same rule: the bird leaves the room until the counter is clean.

Guests follow a card on the cage. Pellets and the listed vegetables are allowed. Avocado, chocolate, coffee, onion, garlic, alcohol, and xylitol are forbidden. Millet stays a brief reward. Leaving a spray up all day turns it into the meal. Weigh the bird if you change the menu, and call if the weight drops. Lories stay on their nectar diet. A helpful guest who offers a parrot mix to a lory should be redirected to the card. Boring consistency is the safety plan.

Questions about foods for pet birds

What vegetables can pet birds eat?

Carrot, bell pepper, broccoli, kale, squash, cooked sweet potato, green beans, and other washed vegetables fit most parrots, cockatiels, and budgies as a supplement to pellets. Remove uneaten fresh food the same day. Seed-only diets miss vitamin A, so the vegetables are doing real work.

Which foods are toxic to birds?

Avocado, because of persin, plus chocolate, caffeine, onion, garlic, alcohol, xylitol, and fruit pits or apple seeds. If one is eaten, call an avian veterinarian or a poison hotline and bring the label. Do not try to make the bird vomit.

Can a bird live on fruit and seed?

Fruit and seed are high in sugar and fat and poor in several nutrients. A formulated pellet should be the majority of the diet for most companion parrots, with vegetables daily and fruit as a small extra. Weigh the bird during any change and call an avian veterinarian if weight falls.
