New Kitten Checklist and First Month Guide

Complete checklist for bringing home a new kitten. Covers supplies, kitten-proofing, first vet visit, feeding schedule, and litter training basics.

Arrival day for a kitten

Carry the kitten into the house in a closed carrier and set that carrier in a quiet room with the door shut. Open the door and let the kitten walk out on a personal timetable. A full-house tour on the first afternoon sends many kittens under a bed for the rest of the night. Put a shallow litter box, water, food, a bed, and a scratcher in that same room. The box goes a few feet from the food, because cats prefer those jobs separated. Show the box by setting the kitten inside after the carrier opens, then again after the first meal.

Keep the food the kitten already knows. A sudden swap is a frequent reason for soft stool in the first week. If you plan a different brand, mix it in over several days once eating is steady. Offer meals on a plate the kitten can reach without climbing into a deep bowl. Water stays fresh in a separate dish. Some kittens prefer a wide cup. Watch whether this kitten drinks, because a kitten who ignores the bowl may have learned to drink from a faucet or a fountain.

Children meet the kitten sitting on the floor with a toy, and they stop when the kitten walks away. Picking up a new kitten for photos can wait until the kitten seeks hands. A carrier left open in the room, with a treat tossed inside once a day, keeps travel from becoming a trap. The clinic phone number goes into every adult's phone on day one.

The first clinic visit and kitten vaccines by name

Schedule the examination within a few days. Bring any shelter or breeder papers and a stool sample. The veterinarian checks weight, eyes, ears, mouth, heart, lungs, belly, and skin, and looks for fleas and ear mites, both common in kittens. Ask for a fecal test and a deworming plan tied to that result. Ask which flea product is labeled for this age and this weight. Many dog products and many hardware-store sprays injure cats, so the label has to name cats and the kitten's age.

The core kitten combination is FVRCP: feline viral rhinotracheitis (herpesvirus), calicivirus, and panleukopenia. The series is repeated through the early months because protection from the mother fades at different ages. Rabies follows local law, often once the kitten is old enough for the product the clinic stocks. FeLV, feline leukemia virus, is discussed when the kitten will go outside, live with cats of unknown status, or join a household that already had a leukemia-positive cat. The clinic may suggest a leukemia test before that vaccine. This page lists names and reasons. It leaves the calendar and any product amount to the person who can examine the kitten.

Ask about a microchip, a spay or neuter window, and whether this kitten needs a fecal recheck after deworming. Keep the kitten indoors, away from unknown cats, until the veterinarian says the vaccine series and the parasite plan have caught up. Shoes that walked through a shelter parking lot can carry panleukopenia into the room, so leave those shoes at the door.

Feeding rhythm by age

A newly weaned kitten, often around eight weeks, eats several small meals spread through the day. Four meals is a common rhythm at that age. By about four to six months many kittens do well on three meals, and by six months a lot of cats move toward breakfast and dinner. Growth is fast, so the amount on the bag is a starting guess. Weigh the kitten every week on a kitchen scale. A kitten who stays flat on the scale needs a clinic conversation, because parasites, disease, or too little food can look the same from across the room.

Kitten-labeled food has the calorie density and the nutrients a growing cat uses. Adult maintenance food can wait until the veterinarian says growth has slowed, often around a year for many cats. Cow's milk gives a lot of kittens diarrhea. Tuna from a can can be a rare taste, and it makes a poor daily diet because of mercury and an unbalanced nutrient pattern, including thiamine concerns when fish is the only food. Cooked egg, plain cooked meat, and a commercial kitten diet cover ordinary treats. Onion, garlic, chocolate, alcohol, grapes, raisins, and xylitol stay out of the dish.

Wet food adds water, which helps some kittens drink enough. Pull uneaten wet food after a short sitting so it does not sour in a warm room. Dry food can stay a bit longer if the kitten is grazing, though scheduled meals make appetite changes easier to notice. A skipped meal in a kitten who is also quiet, vomiting, or hiding is urgent. Young cats develop low blood sugar and dehydration faster than adults.

Litter, scratchers, and the first social month

Unscented clumping litter in a box with low sides is the usual start. Place the kitten in the box after naps and after meals for the first week. Digging with a finger in the litter sometimes starts the idea. Covering an accident with a scolding teaches the kitten to hide the next one behind the couch. Clean the spot with an enzymatic product and add a box closer to where the accident happened. One box on each floor matters once the kitten earns the rest of the house.

A vertical scratcher and a horizontal one give a choice. Put them where the kitten already stretches, often beside a nap spot. If the sofa becomes the target, a scratcher goes in front of that arm and the arm gets a temporary cover. Trim nail tips if the kitten allows a paw hold, and stop at the first struggle. Play is two or three short sessions with a wand toy. Hands are for petting. A kitten who learns that fingers are prey will keep that game into adulthood.

Social exposure for a kitten is gentler than a puppy class. Invite calm visitors to sit in the kitten's room. Let the kitten approach. Carrier trips to the clinic for happy weigh-ins, when the clinic can offer them, keep the car from meaning only shots. Other household pets meet the kitten through a closed door first, then a gate, with the resident pet's routine protected. Food, litter, and lap time stay available to the older animal so the newcomer does not look like a thief.

What the first month should leave you with

By four weeks in the house you want a clinic file, a named vaccine plan, a litter habit, a scratcher the kitten actually uses, a feeding clock, and a kitten who will enter a carrier. Play has a pattern. Night zoomies get a wand session before your bedtime so the burst happens while you are still awake. Windows have secure screens. Blind cords are tied up. Lilies, if anyone brings them home, leave the building, because every part of a true lily can cause kidney failure in a cat.

Call the clinic the same day for repeated vomiting, diarrhea that lasts more than a day, straining in the box with little urine, labored breathing, a limp, or a kitten who will not get up to eat. Bring a note of the last meal and a photo of vomit or stool. The first month is when small problems are still small. A written list of questions beats a memory you try to rebuild in the parking lot.

  • Start in one room with a shallow litter box, water, food, a bed, and a scratcher.
  • Book the veterinary exam within a few days and bring a stool sample.
  • Ask about FVRCP, rabies, and whether FeLV testing and vaccination fit this kitten.
  • Feed several small meals a day at first, then fewer meals as growth steadies.
  • Use a flea product the veterinarian names for this age. Skip dog flea products.
  • Set the kitten in the litter box after meals and naps during week one.
  • Keep play on wand toys and let the kitten leave when the game is over.
  • Store lilies, strings, rubber bands, and human medicines out of reach.

Kitten questions for week one and month one

How soon does a new kitten need a veterinary exam?

Within a few days of arrival. The visit covers a physical exam, a fecal check, and a plan for FVRCP, rabies, and FeLV if the kitten's future includes outdoor access or unknown cats. Bring any papers you were given.

How often should a young kitten be fed?

Newly weaned kittens often eat four small meals across the day. Many shift toward three meals by four to six months and toward two meals around six months. Weigh weekly. The veterinarian adjusts the amount if the kitten is too thin or gaining too fast.

What litter setup works in the first week?

A shallow box with unscented litter, placed away from the food, in the room where the kitten lives. Set the kitten in the box after meals and after naps. Clean accidents without punishment and add a box near any repeat spot.
