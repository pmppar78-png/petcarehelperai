Intestinal Parasites in Dogs

Common intestinal worms in dogs including roundworms, hookworms, whipworms, and tapeworms. Covers symptoms, deworming, and prevention protocols.

Causes and Risk Factors

Multiple factors can contribute to the development of this condition.

  • Genetic predisposition: Certain breeds are at higher risk due to inherited traits
  • Age: Some conditions are more common in puppies/kittens or senior pets
  • Environmental factors: Diet, toxin exposure, stress, and living conditions play a role
  • Underlying health issues: Other conditions may increase susceptibility
  • Immune system status: Compromised immune function can predispose to disease

Symptoms to Watch For

Early detection significantly improves treatment outcomes. Watch for these signs.

  • Changes in appetite, water consumption, or weight
  • Behavioral changes including lethargy, restlessness, or hiding
  • Physical symptoms specific to the affected body system
  • Changes in bathroom habits (frequency, appearance, straining)
  • Visible discomfort, pain response to touch, or changes in posture
  • Respiratory changes, coughing, or labored breathing

Diagnosis

Intestinal parasites in dogs are identified on feces, not on a blood panel. CAPC recommends a fecal flotation, and a separate Giardia test because flotation misses a lot of Giardia. A negative fecal from one stool does not end the search when the dog still has diarrhea. Some hookworms, including multi-anthelmintic-resistant Ancylostoma caninum, survive a routine dewormer, so a repeat fecal after treatment is part of the diagnosis of whether the drug worked.

Treatment Options

The dewormer has to match the parasite the fecal found. Roundworms, hookworms, whipworms, tapeworms, coccidia, and Giardia are not one product. The veterinarian chooses the drug and the dose, and the follow-up fecal says whether it cleared. A dog with resistant hookworms may need a different plan than the first dewormer. Heartworm prevention is a separate drug and does not replace the fecal.

Prevention and Management

  • Regular veterinary checkups (as recommended by the AVMA for all companion animals) for early detection (annually, or semi-annually for seniors)
  • Maintain a healthy weight through proper nutrition and exercise
  • Keep vaccinations and preventive medications current
  • Breed-specific health screenings recommended by your veterinarian
  • Provide a low-stress, enriched environment
  • Follow your vet's recommendations for diet and lifestyle modifications

Is this condition curable?

Common intestinal worms in dogs are clearable when the drug matches the parasite and a later fecal test is negative. CAPC recommends centrifugal flotation, antigen testing, or PCR, because whipworm eggs are dense and hookworms can be present before any eggs appear. Puppies keep acquiring roundworms and hookworms from the dam and the yard, so one dose is not the end of a series. CAPC has confirmed multi-anthelmintic resistant Ancylostoma caninum in several breeds across the United States, not only greyhounds, and those infections stay positive on drugs that used to clear hookworms. A resistant hookworm is the case that does not end by repeating the first tablet.

How can I afford treatment?

Pay for a fecal flotation or antigen test before you pay for a dewormer. CAPC drug tables are parasite-specific: a roundworm product misses giardia, and whipworms need a drug that actually covers them. The wasted money is a pet-store tablet repeated while the wrong organism remains. Puppies often need a series as larvae arrive in the gut, so ask for that series price. Heartworm disease is a separate, much larger invoice than intestinal worms.
