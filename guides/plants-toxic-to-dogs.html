Plants Toxic to

Common household and garden plants toxic to dogs. Covers lilies, sago palm, azaleas, oleander, tulips, and 40+ dangerous plants with symptoms.

Sago palm is a liver emergency

Sago palms and other cycads, including cardboard palm, are outdoor and houseplant hazards with a grim record in dogs. Every part is toxic, and the seeds are especially concentrated. The toxin of concern is cycasin, which the liver converts into compounds that kill liver cells. A dog who chews a seed can vomit within hours, then seem briefly better, then slide into liver failure with jaundice, clotting problems, seizures, and collapse. Survival often depends on how fast decontamination and liver support start. There is no home antidote. If a sago seed, a frond, or a root ball was chewed, call a veterinarian or a poison hotline and go in.

People plant sagos by the front door because they look tidy. Dogs dig the base and pick up fallen seeds. A puppy in a new yard should be walked on a leash until you have scanned for cycads, and the plant should be removed if a dog lives there. Bring a photo of the plant and any chewed pieces. Do not induce vomiting unless the clinic or hotline tells you to.

Tulip bulbs and other spring bulbs

Tulip bulbs contain tulipalin and related irritants. Dogs dig them up in fall and spring because the bulbs smell interesting and sit at nose level in soft soil. Daffodil, narcissus, hyacinth, and amaryllis bulbs cause intense drooling, vomiting, and diarrhea, and some of them affect the heart or the nervous system when the amount is large. The bulb is more concentrated than the leaf. A dog covered in potting soil with bulb fragments in the teeth needs a call even if you only found one hole in the bed.

Autumn crocus is a different plant from spring crocus and contains colchicine, which causes severe gastrointestinal injury and bone-marrow suppression. It is easy to confuse with a harmless bulb. If you are unsure which bulb was planted, bring a piece in a bag and a photo of the package from the garden center. Do not wait for bloody diarrhea to "confirm" the exposure. Identification can happen while you drive.

Castor bean and a few other high-stakes plants

Castor bean plants grow from large mottled seeds. The seeds contain ricin, a toxin that blocks protein synthesis. Chewing a seed releases more ricin than swallowing it whole, and either event is an emergency. Signs include vomiting, bloody diarrhea, weakness, and collapse. The plant is sometimes grown as an ornamental. If you see the spiky seed pods or the mottled beans in a dog's mouth, call immediately and bring the plant material.

Oleander, even a few leaves, disturbs the heart. Yew disturbs the heart. Lily of the valley also contains cardiac glycosides and is a dog problem, distinct from the kidney failure true lilies cause in cats. Rhododendron and azalea cause drooling, vomiting, and sometimes heart effects. Foxglove is another cardiac plant. You do not need to memorize every glycoside. You need a rule: ornamental shrubs with a dog who is vomiting and has a leaf in the teeth get a same-day clinic visit and a plant photo. Mushrooms in the lawn are a separate emergency and should be photographed too, then removed with a glove.

What to do in the yard and on the walk

Walk the property with your phone and identify the shrubs before a puppy arrives. Remove sago, castor bean, oleander, and yew if a dog will have any access. Fence bulb beds or plant bulbs under wire the dog cannot dig through. Indoor bouquets go on a tall shelf, and fallen petals are swept up. A vase of tulips on a coffee table is a bulb-and-water combination a dog will knock over. Teach a solid leave-it on walks, and use a basket muzzle for determined scavengers while training continues, fitted so the dog can pant.

If chewing already happened, collect a sample of the plant, note the time, and call. Rinse visible sap off the fur with mild soap only if the hotline agrees, and keep the dog from licking the rinse water. Do not offer milk, oil, or salt. Watch for vomiting, drooling, wobbliness, a slow or very fast heart, pale gums, and seizures. Cardiac plants and sago palms are "go now" events. Bulb irritation may still need fluids and monitoring. The package label from the nursery is as useful as a medical record, so keep plant tags in a kitchen drawer for the life of the plant.

  • Treat sago palm, especially seeds, as a liver-failure emergency.
  • Call after tulip, daffodil, hyacinth, or amaryllis bulb digging.
  • Treat castor bean seeds as a ricin emergency.
  • Treat oleander, yew, foxglove, and lily of the valley as heart-risk plants for dogs.
  • Bring a photo or a bagged sample. Hold vomiting unless the plant consult instructs it.
  • Remove these plants from dog yards and store nursery tags.
  • Sweep petals and lift bouquets off tables.
  • Use a poison hotline if the clinic line is busy, and travel if they say travel.

Tags in a drawer

Keep nursery tags for every shrub and bulb. A photo of a chewed stem is more useful when you can also show the label. Sago seeds should be bagged and the plant removed if a dog lives there. Bulb beds can sit under wire until the leaves are up and the digging season passes. On the walk home from the clinic, use a leash even in a familiar yard so a second chew does not happen while the first one is still being treated.

After the plant is identified

Once you know the plant, the monitoring changes. Sago palm means liver tests and a conversation about how early support started. Tulip or daffodil bulbs often mean gut protection and fluids. Oleander, yew, or lily of the valley means heart monitoring. If the clinic keeps the dog, ask what a worsening night would look like and who calls you. If they send you home, write the recheck time on the fridge before you sleep. A dog who vomits again, collapses, or has a sudden change in gum color should return even if the appointment is tomorrow.

Remove the rest of the plant the same day if it is in your yard. Fallen sago seeds keep poisoning the next dog, including a neighbor's. Bag them. Nursery tags belong in a drawer so the next bouquet or shrub has a name. On walks, a short leash and a leave-it cue prevent a second chew while you are still dealing with the first. Puppies explore with their mouths. A new yard should be scanned for cycads and bulb beds before the leash comes off.

Questions about plants that poison dogs

Why is sago palm so dangerous?

All parts contain cycasin, and the seeds are the most concentrated. The liver is the target. Vomiting can be followed by a quiet period and then liver failure. Call a veterinarian or poison hotline immediately and bring a photo of the plant.

What if my dog dug up tulip bulbs?

Tulip bulbs irritate the mouth and gut and can cause heavy vomiting. Daffodil, hyacinth, and amaryllis bulbs are related worries, and autumn crocus is more dangerous still. Bag a bulb fragment, call a veterinarian, and skip home vomiting unless they direct it.

Which yard plants affect a dog's heart?

Oleander, yew, foxglove, and lily of the valley are examples. A dog who chews them and then vomits, collapses, or has an odd heart rate needs an emergency clinic. Bring a leaf for identification. This is separate from true lilies, which are a kidney emergency in cats.
