About Pet Care Helper AI

Founded by Paul Paradis — Boston, MA

My family always had a dog when I was growing up — shelter dogs, mostly — and we fostered cats for a while too. Animals were just part of the household.

This site started because of a bad night. My dog would not stop throwing up around 2 a.m., and I could not figure out if she needed an emergency vet or just had an upset stomach. I searched everywhere online and got either incomprehensible medical papers or ancient forum threads with conflicting advice. I drove to the emergency clinic (she was fine — ate something in the yard), but that helpless feeling stayed with me.

I work in tech, and the thought kept nagging at me: why is it so hard to find clear, trustworthy pet health info when you actually need it? Not a replacement for a vet — nothing replaces a vet — but something that helps you figure out if this is a "keep an eye on it" situation or a "get in the car" situation. That is what I set out to build.

I am not a veterinarian. I do not have a medical background. What I do have is a careful process: every article is checked against published veterinary sources like the AVMA guidelines, the Merck Veterinary Manual, and peer-reviewed journals. If I cannot back something up, it does not go on the site. Our full process is described on our Editorial Standards page, and the limits of what we can offer are laid out in our Medical Disclaimer.

What This Site Does

We help pet owners find useful health information, especially when they are worried and their vet's office is closed. The site covers dogs, cats, birds, reptiles, amphibians, and fish — because people who keep a bearded dragon or a betta deserve reliable info just as much as someone with a Labrador.

Three things we care about most:

  • Be available when it matters. Pet scares do not wait for business hours. The site works around the clock and covers a wide range of species.
  • Get the facts right. Health content is checked against published veterinary sources. When we are not sure about something, we say so.
  • Know our limits. We are not veterinarians. We say that clearly and often. If something looks serious, we tell you to call your vet.

The People Behind It

I (Paul) handle editorial direction and platform decisions. I started this as a side project — just trying to solve a problem I kept running into — and it grew into something that helps thousands of pet owners every month.

Our contributors include people who have kept birds, maintained aquariums, fostered rescue dogs, and worked with exotic species. That hands-on experience matters because textbook accuracy alone does not always capture what a pet owner needs to hear at 2 a.m. We also try to include different perspectives: someone who has rushed a cat to the ER brings something different than someone who knows the veterinary literature by heart. Both are valuable.

How We Check Our Work

Every health article goes through what we call a veterinary alignment review: we check each claim against current guidelines from the AVMA and the Merck Veterinary Manual, along with peer-reviewed veterinary journals. If we cannot back something up with a reputable source, it gets cut.

We also review older content periodically to catch anything that has gone stale. Veterinary guidelines change, and we need to keep up. We do not always catch everything as fast as we would like, but it is an ongoing process.

If you are a licensed veterinarian and you want to help us improve, we would genuinely welcome that. Reach out at advertise@petcarehelperai.com.

How the AI Works

You describe what is going on with your pet in plain English, and the AI helps you sort out whether it is likely something minor, something worth a vet call in the morning, or something that needs attention right now.

It cannot examine your pet or run tests. It is not a diagnosis tool. But it can help you organize what you are seeing, recognize warning signs you might miss, and figure out the right next step — which, more often than not, is calling your vet.

Beyond the AI chat, we maintain articles on practical topics like pet insurance, nutrition, training, preventive care, and emergency first aid. We also have a directory of veterinary resources: local clinics, emergency hospitals, exotic pet specialists, and tele-vet services.

How Content Gets Made

Here is our process, plainly stated:

  1. Research first. We start with veterinary literature — AVMA guidelines, the Merck Veterinary Manual, peer-reviewed journals. If a claim cannot be sourced, it gets cut.
  2. Draft with AI, edit by hand. AI helps with research and first drafts. A human then checks the facts, rewrites anything unclear or inaccurate, and decides what gets published.
  3. Safety check. Could anything in this article be misunderstood in a way that hurts an animal? If so, it gets rewritten. Emergency warning signs are called out prominently.
  4. Publish with sources. We include references and disclaimers. No burying the fine print.
  5. Review on a schedule. Health-critical content is reviewed roughly every 90 days. General care content roughly every six months. When major veterinary guidelines change, we update affected articles as quickly as we can.

For the full details, visit our Editorial Standards page.

Standards We Hold Ourselves To

  • Source health claims. Every health recommendation traces back to published veterinary literature. When we cannot find a solid source, we leave it out.
  • Default to caution. If sources disagree or evidence is thin, we recommend the safer option and tell you to check with your vet.
  • Be honest about AI. We use it. We say so. Our Editorial Standards page explains exactly how.
  • Keep money out of health advice. We have affiliate partnerships (disclosed on our Partners page), but they never affect health recommendations.
  • Review regularly. Health articles are reviewed roughly every 90 days. General content roughly every six months. When we get something wrong, we fix it.
  • Stay in our lane. We do not diagnose. We do not prescribe. We do not give dosage advice. That is your vet's job.

When This Site Helps Most

Your cat is acting strange and the vet's office is closed. Your dog ate something she should not have and you do not know how worried to be. Your parrot is sitting on the bottom of the cage and you have no idea if that is normal. Those are the moments this site was built for.

Most pet owners do not need a diagnosis from a website — they need to know whether this is a "wait and see" situation, a "call the vet tomorrow" situation, or a "go now" situation. That is what the AI helps sort out.

It also helps with vet visit prep. When you walk in knowing which symptoms to describe and what details matter, you get more out of a short consultation. Several users have told us that going in organized helped their vet reach a faster diagnosis.

If you keep exotic pets — a ball python, a betta, a cockatiel — finding good species-specific health info online can be especially hard. Specialized vets are not always nearby, and reliable guidance is scattered across niche forums. We have tried to pull it together in one place.

Who Uses This

New puppy owners who want to know what is normal. Cat people dealing with a health scare for the first time. Reptile keepers looking for species-specific care info. Families deciding whether they are ready for a pet. We hear from all of them.

We pay attention to what people ask and what they tell us. If someone points out an error or suggests a topic we have missed, we look into it. Our Contact page is always open.

A Note on Trust

There is a lot of mediocre pet health content online, and some people are understandably skeptical of AI-powered tools. We get that.

Everything here is educational — that is stated on every page, and we mean it. The AI defaults to caution: if there is any real doubt about a pet's condition, the response will tell you to see a vet. For potential poisoning, it directs you to poison control or emergency care immediately.

We have genuine respect for veterinarians and for the work they do. This site does not pretend to replace them. What it tries to do is help pet owners take the right next step, and most of the time, that step is picking up the phone and calling a vet.
