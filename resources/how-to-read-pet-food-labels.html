How to Read Pet Food Labels: A Veterinary Nutritionist’s Guide

Walking down the pet food aisle can feel overwhelming. Dozens of brands compete for your attention with claims like “all-natural,” “holistic,” “human-grade,” and “veterinarian-recommended.” But behind the glossy packaging and enticing marketing language, the information that actually matters is governed by federal and state regulations — and knowing how to read it can help you make genuinely informed decisions about what you feed your pet.

Pet food labeling in the United States is regulated by both the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO). The FDA establishes baseline requirements for proper labeling, ingredient safety, and manufacturing practices, while AAFCO develops model regulations and nutrient profiles that most states adopt into law. Understanding how these regulations work is the key to decoding any pet food label.

The Guaranteed Analysis Panel

Every commercial pet food sold in the United States is required to display a guaranteed analysis on its label. This panel provides minimum or maximum percentages of specific nutrients in the food. At a minimum, it must include four values: crude protein (minimum), crude fat (minimum), crude fiber (maximum), and moisture (maximum).

The word “crude” does not mean the nutrients are low quality. It refers to the specific laboratory methods used to measure them. Crude protein, for example, is determined by measuring nitrogen content using the Kjeldahl method, then multiplying by a factor of 6.25 to estimate total protein. This method measures all nitrogen-containing compounds, not just digestible amino acids, which is why the guaranteed analysis alone cannot tell you about protein digestibility or quality.

Crude protein represents the minimum percentage of protein in the food. For adult dogs, AAFCO requires a minimum of 18% crude protein on a dry matter basis; for adult cats, the minimum is 26%. Puppies and kittens require higher minimums — 22.5% for puppies and 30% for kittens. However, more protein is not necessarily better. Excess protein beyond what the body can utilize is simply metabolized for energy or excreted.

Crude fat is the minimum percentage of fat, determined by ether extraction. AAFCO minimums are 5.5% for adult dogs and 9% for adult cats on a dry matter basis. Fat is the most energy-dense macronutrient, providing about 8.5 kilocalories per gram compared to 3.5 kcal/g for protein and carbohydrates. Foods higher in fat will be more calorie-dense.

Crude fiber is the maximum percentage of indigestible plant material. This measurement actually underestimates total dietary fiber because the crude fiber method dissolves and loses a significant portion of soluble fiber during testing. A food listing 4% crude fiber may actually contain 8–12% total dietary fiber.

Moisture is the maximum percentage of water in the food. This number is critical for comparing foods across different formats. Dry kibble typically contains 6–10% moisture, while canned food ranges from 75–85%. This dramatic difference means you cannot directly compare the guaranteed analysis numbers between wet and dry food without converting to a dry matter basis.

Converting to Dry Matter Basis

To fairly compare a canned food and a kibble, you need to remove the water from the equation. Here is how: subtract the moisture percentage from 100 to find the dry matter percentage, then divide the nutrient percentage by the dry matter percentage and multiply by 100. For example, a canned food with 10% protein and 78% moisture has a dry matter protein content of 10 ÷ (100 − 78) × 100 = 45.5%. A kibble with 26% protein and 10% moisture has a dry matter protein of 26 ÷ 90 × 100 = 28.9%. In this case, the canned food is actually significantly higher in protein than the kibble, despite appearing lower on the label.

The Ingredient List

Federal regulations require that ingredients be listed in descending order by weight. The first ingredient weighs the most in the formulation, and the last ingredient weighs the least. However, this weight is measured before processing, which means ingredients with high moisture content — like fresh chicken — will appear higher on the list even though much of that weight is water that evaporates during cooking.

This is why a food listing “chicken” as its first ingredient may actually contain more corn or rice by dry weight. Fresh chicken is approximately 70% water, so 10 pounds of chicken yields only about 3 pounds of dry matter. Meanwhile, “chicken meal” (which has already been rendered and dried) is only about 5% moisture, so 8 pounds of chicken meal contributes nearly 7.6 pounds of dry matter. A food with chicken meal as its first ingredient may actually contain more animal-derived protein than one listing fresh chicken first.

Watch for a practice called ingredient splitting. This is when a manufacturer lists different forms of the same ingredient separately to push each one further down the list. For example, a food might list chicken as the first ingredient, followed by ground corn, corn gluten meal, and corn bran. Each corn ingredient appears individually, but if you added them all together, corn-based ingredients might actually outweigh the chicken. This practice is not illegal, but it can create a misleading impression of the food’s composition.

Understanding By-Products

Few pet food ingredients generate as much controversy — or misunderstanding — as by-products. Chicken by-product meal, for example, consists of ground, rendered, clean parts of the carcass including necks, feet, undeveloped eggs, and intestines. It does not include feathers, beaks, or feces, which are explicitly excluded by AAFCO definitions.

From a nutritional standpoint, by-products are not inherently inferior. Organ meats like liver and kidney are among the most nutrient-dense foods available, rich in vitamins A, B12, iron, and essential amino acids. In the wild, predators preferentially consume organ meats before skeletal muscle. The negative perception of by-products is largely a marketing phenomenon driven by companies positioning their products as “premium.” Board-certified veterinary nutritionists consistently note that by-products can be excellent protein sources when properly processed.

AAFCO Nutritional Adequacy Statements

Perhaps the most important piece of information on any pet food label is the AAFCO nutritional adequacy statement, sometimes called the “complete and balanced” statement. This tells you whether the food meets established nutrient profiles and, crucially, how that determination was made.

There are two primary methods for establishing nutritional adequacy:

Formulation method: The manufacturer calculates that the recipe meets AAFCO nutrient profiles based on the known nutrient content of each ingredient. The statement reads something like: “[Product name] is formulated to meet the nutritional levels established by the AAFCO Dog Food Nutrient Profiles for [life stage].” This method is less rigorous because it relies on theoretical calculations. Nutrient interactions, bioavailability, and processing losses are not directly measured.

Feeding trial method: The food is actually fed to dogs or cats following AAFCO protocols for a specified period (typically six months for adult maintenance), and the animals are monitored for health parameters including body weight, blood work, and overall condition. The statement reads: “Animal feeding tests using AAFCO procedures substantiate that [product name] provides complete and balanced nutrition for [life stage].” This method provides real-world evidence that animals can thrive on the food.

Foods labeled “for intermittent or supplemental feeding only” do not meet AAFCO profiles for any life stage and should not be used as a sole diet. Treats, toppers, and some specialty diets carry this designation.

Pay close attention to the life stage designation: “all life stages,” “adult maintenance,” “growth,” or “growth and reproduction.” An all-life-stages food meets the most demanding nutrient profiles (growth/reproduction) and is therefore suitable for any age, but it may provide more calories and certain nutrients than a sedentary adult pet actually needs.

Product Naming Rules

AAFCO has established strict rules about how animal-derived ingredients can be referenced in product names, and understanding these rules reveals how much actual meat is in the food:

The 95% Rule: If a product is named “Chicken for Dogs” or “Salmon Cat Food,” the named ingredient must constitute at least 95% of the total weight of all ingredients (excluding water used for processing) and at least 70% of the total product weight. When two ingredients are named (e.g., “Chicken and Rice Dog Food”), together they must reach 95%, with the first-named ingredient predominating.

The 25% Rule (Dinner/Entrée/Platter): If the product name includes a descriptor like “dinner,” “entrée,” “platter,” “recipe,” or “formula,” the named ingredient must constitute at least 25% of the total weight (excluding water for processing) and at least 10% of the total product. So “Chicken Dinner for Dogs” needs only 25% chicken — dramatically less than “Chicken for Dogs.”

The 3% Rule (“With”): The word “with” signals that the named ingredient constitutes at least 3% of the product by weight. “Dog Food with Chicken” requires only 3% chicken. The difference between “Chicken Dog Food” and “Dog Food with Chicken” is enormous — 95% versus 3%.

The “Flavor” Rule: A product labeled with a flavor (e.g., “Beef Flavor Dog Food”) has no minimum percentage requirement for that ingredient. It simply must contain a sufficient amount of the named flavor to be detectable. This could be achieved through small quantities of beef digest or other flavoring agents.

Decoding Marketing Claims

Pet food marketing uses many terms that sound impressive but have varying degrees of regulatory meaning:

“Natural” has an AAFCO definition: a feed or ingredient derived solely from plant, animal, or mined sources, not produced by or subject to a chemically synthetic process, and not containing any additives or processing aids that are chemically synthetic — except for vitamins, minerals, and trace nutrients that can be synthetic if no natural alternative exists. Most commercial pet foods can meet this definition.

“Organic” is regulated by the USDA National Organic Program and requires certification. Pet foods labeled “organic” must follow the same USDA organic standards as human food. This is a meaningful, verified claim.

“Holistic” has no legal or regulatory definition in pet food. It is purely a marketing term with no enforceable standard. A food labeled “holistic” is not required to meet any criteria beyond standard pet food regulations.

“Human-grade” is a meaningful claim under AAFCO guidelines, but it requires that every ingredient and the finished product itself be stored, handled, processed, and transported in compliance with federal regulations for human-edible food. Very few pet foods can legitimately make this claim, and those that do are typically significantly more expensive.

“Grain-free” simply means the food does not contain cereal grains like wheat, corn, rice, barley, or oats. It does not mean carbohydrate-free — grain-free foods typically substitute legumes (peas, lentils, chickpeas), potatoes, or tapioca as carbohydrate sources. Importantly, the FDA has been investigating a potential link between certain grain-free diets (particularly those high in legumes) and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in dogs since 2018. While a definitive causal relationship has not been established, many veterinary cardiologists and nutritionists advise caution with grain-free diets unless there is a specific medical reason to avoid grains.

“Veterinarian-recommended” or “veterinarian-formulated” are not regulated terms. Any company can claim their food was recommended or formulated by a veterinarian. What matters more is whether the company employs a full-time, board-certified veterinary nutritionist (DACVN) who oversees formulation.

Choosing Food Based on Life Stage

Nutritional requirements vary significantly across a pet’s life. Puppies and kittens need higher protein, fat, calcium, and phosphorus to support rapid growth. Large-breed puppies, in particular, need controlled calcium levels (typically 0.7–1.2% on a dry matter basis) to reduce the risk of developmental orthopedic diseases like osteochondrosis. Feeding an “all life stages” food to a large-breed puppy may provide excessive calcium unless the product specifically states it meets the AAFCO nutrient profile for growth of large-size dogs (70 lbs or more as an adult).

Adult maintenance diets are designed for fully grown animals with moderate activity levels. Senior diets are not defined by AAFCO, which means there is no official nutrient profile for senior pets. Senior formulas vary widely between brands — some are lower in calories and fat to address obesity, while others are higher in protein to combat age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia). The best approach is to choose a senior diet based on your individual pet’s health needs, ideally with veterinary guidance.

What to Look for in a Trustworthy Brand

The World Small Animal Veterinary Association (WSAVA) has published guidelines for selecting pet foods. Key questions to ask about any brand include: Does the company employ at least one full-time board-certified veterinary nutritionist? Does the company own its manufacturing facilities or at least have rigorous quality control over its contract manufacturers? Does the company conduct AAFCO feeding trials rather than relying solely on formulation? Can the company provide complete nutrient analyses (not just the guaranteed analysis minimums and maximums) upon request? Does the company invest in published, peer-reviewed research?

Companies that can answer “yes” to most or all of these questions have demonstrated a commitment to evidence-based nutrition that goes beyond marketing. While smaller or boutique brands can also produce excellent products, they are less likely to have the resources for feeding trials and in-house nutritional expertise.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Do not select a food solely based on the ingredient list. Two foods with identical ingredient lists can have vastly different nutrient profiles depending on the proportions and quality of those ingredients. The ingredient list tells you what is in the food, not how much or how digestible it is.

Do not assume that more expensive foods are automatically better. Price reflects many factors including marketing budgets, packaging, distribution costs, and brand positioning. Some moderately priced foods from established companies with veterinary nutritionists on staff are nutritionally superior to expensive boutique options.

Do not change your pet’s diet frequently without reason. While dietary rotation is popular in some pet owner communities, frequent food changes can cause gastrointestinal upset. If you do switch foods, transition gradually over 7–10 days by mixing increasing proportions of the new food with decreasing amounts of the old food.

Finally, remember that the best food for your pet is one that meets their specific nutritional needs, agrees with their digestive system, is made by a reputable company, and fits your budget. If your pet has a healthy coat, good energy levels, firm stool, and maintains an ideal body condition score, their current food is likely meeting their needs — regardless of what the marketing says about any other product.
