How to Choose a Veterinarian: A Pet Owner's Complete Guide

Introduction: Your Most Important Pet Care Partner

Of all the decisions you make as a pet owner, choosing the right veterinarian may be the single most consequential. Your veterinarian is not just someone you see when your pet is sick—they are your primary partner in keeping your animal healthy throughout its entire life. From puppy vaccinations to senior wellness screenings, from routine dental cleanings to emergency triage at midnight, the quality of veterinary care your pet receives directly shapes its health outcomes, longevity, and quality of life.

Yet many pet owners choose a veterinarian based solely on proximity or a quick internet search, without considering the factors that actually determine whether a practice is the right fit for their pet and their family. The American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) emphasizes that the veterinarian-client-patient relationship (VCPR) is the foundation of effective veterinary medicine. A strong VCPR means your veterinarian knows your pet's history, understands your values and financial situation, and can provide care that is both medically appropriate and personally tailored.

This guide walks you through every aspect of selecting, evaluating, and building a relationship with a veterinarian—whether you have a Labrador Retriever, a cockatiel, a bearded dragon, or a community fish tank. We cover the different types of veterinary practices, the specific qualities to look for, questions to ask during your first visit, red flags that should prompt you to look elsewhere, and special considerations for exotic pet owners, multi-pet households, senior pets, and pet owners on a budget.

Types of Veterinary Practices

Understanding the different types of veterinary practices available helps you determine which is the best starting point for your pet's care. Each type serves a different purpose, and many pet owners end up using more than one over the course of their pet's life.

General Practice (Primary Care) Veterinary Clinics

General practice clinics are the backbone of veterinary medicine and serve as the primary care provider for most pets. These clinics handle wellness examinations, vaccinations, parasite prevention, dental care, routine surgeries (spay/neuter, mass removals), basic diagnostics (blood work, X-rays, urinalysis), and treatment of common illnesses and injuries. A good general practice veterinarian is your first point of contact for virtually all health concerns and will refer you to a specialist when a condition requires advanced expertise or equipment. Most general practice clinics operate during standard business hours, typically Monday through Saturday, though hours vary.

Emergency and Specialty Veterinary Hospitals

Emergency veterinary hospitals provide urgent and critical care outside of normal business hours—evenings, weekends, and holidays. Many emergency facilities are part of larger specialty hospitals that also house board-certified veterinary specialists in fields such as internal medicine, surgery, oncology, cardiology, neurology, ophthalmology, and dermatology. These hospitals are equipped with advanced diagnostic tools including MRI, CT scanning, ultrasound, and endoscopy. You will typically visit an emergency or specialty hospital on referral from your general practice veterinarian or when your regular clinic is closed and your pet needs immediate attention. Costs at emergency and specialty hospitals are generally higher than at general practice clinics, reflecting the advanced equipment, 24-hour staffing, and specialized training involved.

Mobile Veterinary Services

Mobile veterinarians provide care in your home or at your location. This option is particularly valuable for pets that experience extreme stress during car rides or in clinic waiting rooms, for owners with mobility limitations, for households with multiple pets that would be difficult to transport simultaneously, and for large animals. Mobile veterinary services typically offer wellness exams, vaccinations, blood work, and sometimes basic treatments. They generally cannot provide surgery, advanced imaging, or emergency care. Home visits often cost more than clinic visits due to the veterinarian's travel time and the limitations of a mobile setup.

Low-Cost and Community Veterinary Clinics

Low-cost clinics provide basic veterinary services—vaccinations, spay/neuter surgery, microchipping, and sometimes basic wellness exams—at reduced prices. These clinics may be operated by nonprofit organizations, humane societies, or municipal animal services. They play an essential role in making veterinary care accessible to pet owners who might otherwise be unable to afford it. However, low-cost clinics typically do not provide comprehensive medical care, advanced diagnostics, or ongoing treatment for chronic conditions. They are best used as a supplement to, rather than a replacement for, a primary care veterinary relationship, particularly for pets that need more than basic preventive services.

Veterinary Teaching Hospitals

Veterinary schools at universities operate teaching hospitals that provide a full range of veterinary services, including highly specialized care. Appointments at teaching hospitals are often supervised by board-certified specialists, and the diagnostic capabilities rival or exceed those of private specialty hospitals. The potential trade-off is that appointments may take longer because veterinary students are involved in the examination process under faculty supervision. Costs at teaching hospitals are sometimes lower than at private specialty practices, though this varies by institution. Veterinary teaching hospitals can be an excellent option for complex or rare conditions, particularly if you live near a veterinary school.

What to Look for in a Veterinarian

Once you understand the types of practices available, the next step is evaluating specific qualities that distinguish an excellent veterinary practice from a mediocre one. The following criteria should guide your evaluation.

Credentials and Accreditation

Every practicing veterinarian must hold a Doctor of Veterinary Medicine (DVM) or Veterinary Medical Doctor (VMD) degree from an accredited veterinary school and must be licensed by the state in which they practice. You can verify a veterinarian's license through your state's veterinary medical board. Beyond basic licensure, look for additional credentials that indicate a commitment to excellence:

  • AAHA accreditation: The American Animal Hospital Association (AAHA) is the only organization that accredits veterinary practices in the United States and Canada. AAHA-accredited hospitals are evaluated against approximately 900 standards covering patient care, pain management, surgery, pharmacy, laboratory, diagnostic imaging, exam facilities, medical records, cleanliness, continuing education, and emergency preparedness. Accreditation is voluntary, and only about 12 to 15 percent of veterinary practices achieve it. While a non-accredited practice can certainly provide excellent care, AAHA accreditation provides independent, third-party verification that the practice meets rigorous standards.
  • Board certifications: Veterinarians who complete additional residency training (typically 3 to 4 years after veterinary school) and pass rigorous examinations can become board-certified specialists through the American Board of Veterinary Practitioners (ABVP) or various specialty colleges (such as the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine or the American College of Veterinary Surgeons). Board certification is particularly relevant when seeking care for complex conditions.
  • Continuing education: All states require veterinarians to complete continuing education hours to maintain their license, but the best practitioners go well beyond the minimum requirements. Ask whether the veterinary team participates in additional training, conferences, or certification programs.

Communication Style

The way a veterinarian communicates with you is one of the most important factors in the overall quality of care your pet receives. Veterinary medicine involves complex decisions, and you need a veterinarian who can help you understand your options. Look for a veterinarian who:

  • Explains diagnoses, treatment options, and prognoses in clear, understandable language without excessive jargon
  • Listens to your concerns and questions without dismissing them or making you feel rushed
  • Presents multiple treatment options when they exist, including the option to monitor and wait when appropriate
  • Discusses costs openly and proactively, rather than presenting a bill as an afterthought
  • Acknowledges when they do not know something and is willing to research or refer to a specialist
  • Respects your role as the decision-maker for your pet's care while providing their professional recommendation

A veterinarian who rushes through appointments, speaks in impenetrable medical terminology, or becomes defensive when you ask questions is not providing the standard of communication that effective veterinary care requires. Trust your instincts on this: if you do not feel heard and respected during appointments, the relationship will not serve your pet well over time.

Facility Quality

The physical quality of a veterinary facility provides tangible clues about the practice's standards. During your first visit, take note of:

  • Cleanliness: The waiting area, exam rooms, and any visible treatment areas should be clean and well-maintained. A veterinary clinic handles animals that may be infectious, injured, or stressed, so rigorous cleaning protocols are essential for preventing disease transmission.
  • Equipment: A well-equipped general practice should have in-house diagnostic capabilities including X-ray (radiography), a laboratory for running basic blood work and urinalysis, and dental equipment for cleanings and extractions. Ask whether the clinic has an in-house lab or sends samples to an outside laboratory—in-house testing typically provides faster results, which matters when your pet is acutely ill.
  • Separate waiting areas or protocols: Some practices provide separate waiting areas for dogs and cats, or for sick and well patients. This reduces stress and minimizes the risk of disease transmission in the waiting room.
  • Comfortable environment: Pay attention to whether the clinic feels welcoming and calm. Excessive noise, overcrowding, or a chaotic atmosphere can indicate staffing or management issues that may affect the quality of care.

Staff Demeanor and Animal Handling

How the entire veterinary team—not just the veterinarian—handles your pet is a critical indicator of the practice's philosophy and culture. Observe how technicians and assistants interact with animals in the waiting area, during weigh-ins, and during the examination:

  • Do they approach animals calmly and allow them to acclimate before handling?
  • Do they use gentle restraint techniques rather than forceful holds?
  • Do they use treats, positive reinforcement, and calming techniques to reduce stress?
  • Are they patient with anxious or difficult animals, or do they appear frustrated?

Many progressive practices now follow Fear Free or Low Stress Handling protocols, which are evidence-based approaches to reducing fear, anxiety, and stress in veterinary patients. Clinics that are Fear Free Certified have invested in training their entire team—from front desk staff to veterinarians—in techniques that make veterinary visits less traumatic for animals. This is not merely a comfort issue: stressed animals can mask or exaggerate symptoms, making diagnosis more difficult, and the stress of veterinary visits can cause some owners to avoid bringing their pets in for necessary care.

Emergency Protocols

Understanding what happens when your pet has a medical emergency outside of normal business hours is essential. Ask any prospective veterinary practice:

  • What are your after-hours emergency procedures?
  • Do you have a veterinarian on call after hours, or do you refer to a specific emergency hospital?
  • Which emergency hospital do you work with, and do they have access to your patient records?
  • Is there a nurse or technician advice line available for after-hours questions?

Some practices operate their own emergency services or share an after-hours facility with other practices. Others refer all emergencies to a separate emergency hospital. Neither approach is inherently better, but you should know the protocol before an emergency occurs. Knowing where to go and what to do when your pet is in crisis can save critical minutes and reduce your own panic in an already stressful situation.

Location and Hours

While location should not be the primary factor in choosing a veterinarian, convenience matters for maintaining consistent care. A clinic that is prohibitively far from your home or workplace makes it harder to keep regular appointments, respond quickly in an emergency, and follow through on recommended treatment plans. Consider:

  • How far is the clinic from your home or workplace?
  • What are the clinic's hours? Do they offer early morning, evening, or weekend appointments?
  • How easy is it to get an appointment? What is the typical wait time for non-urgent visits?
  • Can the clinic accommodate same-day sick visits, or is there a significant wait?

A clinic 10 minutes away that you visit regularly will serve your pet far better than a highly rated practice an hour away that you visit reluctantly. That said, if you have an exotic pet that requires specialized care, traveling a greater distance to see a qualified exotic animal veterinarian is often necessary and worthwhile.

Pricing Transparency

Veterinary care can be expensive, and you deserve to know what you are committing to before treatment begins. A practice that values pricing transparency will:

  • Provide written estimates before performing procedures, diagnostics, or treatments
  • Explain what each line item on the estimate covers and why it is recommended
  • Discuss the range of possible costs if the diagnosis is uncertain
  • Be upfront about payment expectations and policies
  • Offer or accept payment plans, pet insurance, or third-party financing options (such as CareCredit or Scratchpay)
  • Not pressure you into declining care because of cost, but instead work with you to find an appropriate level of care within your budget

Veterinary costs vary significantly between practices and regions. There is nothing wrong with asking for an estimate or requesting a comparison of treatment options at different price points. A veterinarian who is offended by cost discussions is not communicating in your pet's best interest.

Questions to Ask During Your First Visit

Your first visit to a new veterinary practice is essentially an interview—you are evaluating whether this practice is the right fit for your pet and your family. In addition to observing the facility and staff, consider asking the following questions:

  1. How many veterinarians are in the practice, and will I typically see the same one?
  2. What are your protocols for common preventive care (vaccinations, parasite prevention, dental care)?
  3. How do you handle after-hours emergencies?
  4. Can you provide written estimates before procedures?
  5. What payment options do you accept? Do you work with pet insurance or offer payment plans?
  6. Do you have experience with my specific pet species or breed?
  7. What is your approach to pain management?
  8. How do you communicate test results and follow-up care?
  9. Do you offer telemedicine or phone consultations for non-emergency questions?
  10. What continuing education or specialty training has your team completed recently?

Pay attention not only to the answers but to how comfortably and thoroughly the practice responds. A practice that welcomes these questions is one that values informed, engaged pet owners.

Red Flags to Watch For

Not every veterinary practice will be a good fit, and some may exhibit warning signs that should prompt you to seek care elsewhere. Be cautious if you observe any of the following:

  • Reluctance to explain or answer questions: A veterinarian who brushes off your questions, becomes impatient when you ask for clarification, or refuses to explain the reasoning behind a diagnosis or treatment recommendation is not providing the standard of communication your pet deserves.
  • No written estimates or surprise billing: If a practice routinely performs procedures without providing a cost estimate beforehand, or if you are consistently surprised by bills that are significantly higher than discussed, this represents a failure of transparency.
  • Pushing unnecessary services: While preventive care and recommended diagnostics are important, a practice that aggressively upsells optional add-ons, premium products, or unnecessary procedures without clear medical justification may be prioritizing revenue over your pet's needs.
  • Rough handling of animals: Any staff member who handles your pet roughly, uses excessive force during restraint, or appears indifferent to your pet's fear or pain is a serious red flag. Veterinary care should be administered as gently as possible.
  • Unsanitary conditions: Dirty exam rooms, unpleasant odors beyond what is expected in a veterinary setting, visibly soiled equipment, or disorganized treatment areas suggest inadequate infection control and facility management.
  • High staff turnover: If you notice that the staff changes frequently—different technicians and front desk personnel every time you visit—this can indicate management problems, a toxic work environment, or understaffing, all of which can affect care quality.
  • Discouraging second opinions: A confident, competent veterinarian should welcome second opinions. If a veterinarian becomes defensive or hostile when you mention seeking another perspective, consider this a red flag.
  • No emergency protocols: A practice that cannot clearly explain what you should do in an after-hours emergency is not adequately prepared to support you in a crisis.

Special Considerations

Exotic Pet Owners: Reptiles, Birds, Amphibians, and Fish

If you own an exotic pet, finding an appropriate veterinarian requires additional effort and research. Most veterinary school curricula focus primarily on dogs and cats, and many general practitioners have limited experience with exotic species. The physiological differences between a ball python, a macaw, and a golden retriever are enormous, and the diagnostic and treatment approaches are fundamentally different.

For exotic pets, look for veterinarians who:

  • Are members of species-specific professional organizations such as the Association of Reptilian and Amphibian Veterinarians (ARAV), the Association of Avian Veterinarians (AAV), or the Association of Exotic Mammal Veterinarians (AEMV)
  • Hold board certification from the ABVP in avian practice, reptile and amphibian practice, or exotic companion mammal practice
  • Have demonstrable experience treating your specific type of pet and can discuss species-specific husbandry, nutrition, and common health conditions with fluency
  • Maintain appropriate equipment for exotic species, such as gram scales for accurate weight monitoring of small animals, and species-appropriate anesthesia and surgical capabilities

The ARAV and AAV both maintain online directories of member veterinarians, which can help you locate an exotic animal practitioner in your area. You may need to travel farther for an exotic vet, but the difference in care quality makes it worthwhile. A veterinarian unfamiliar with your species may miss critical signs, misdiagnose conditions, or prescribe inappropriate treatments.

Multi-Pet Households

If you have multiple pets—especially a combination of species—finding a practice that can competently serve all of your animals simplifies logistics and keeps all medical records in one place. Some general practices treat dogs, cats, and certain small mammals. However, if your household includes exotic species such as reptiles or birds alongside dogs or cats, you may need relationships with two different veterinary practices: a general practice for your dogs and cats, and an exotic animal specialist for your other pets.

When you have multiple pets, ask about multi-pet discounts or combined appointment scheduling. Some practices offer these accommodations, which can reduce both cost and the number of separate trips you need to make. Also discuss each pet's individual needs during wellness visits—what is routine for one species may not apply to another.

Senior Pets with Chronic Conditions

Senior pets with ongoing health conditions such as arthritis, kidney disease, diabetes, heart disease, or cancer require more frequent veterinary attention and often benefit from a veterinarian who takes a proactive, comprehensive approach to geriatric care. When evaluating a veterinarian for a senior pet, consider:

  • Does the practice have experience managing your pet's specific condition?
  • Are they equipped to perform the regular monitoring your pet needs (blood work, urinalysis, blood pressure, imaging)?
  • Do they offer or discuss pain management protocols, including both pharmaceutical and non-pharmaceutical approaches?
  • Will they work with you to develop a long-term care plan that balances quality of life with treatment goals?
  • Are they experienced in discussing end-of-life care, palliative care, and euthanasia with compassion and honesty?

Our Senior Pet Care Guide provides additional information on caring for aging pets.

Pet Owners on a Budget

Financial constraints should never prevent a pet from receiving necessary care, and a good veterinarian will work with you to find solutions rather than judging you for your budget. If cost is a significant concern:

  • Be upfront with your veterinarian about your financial situation—they cannot help you find affordable options if they do not know about your constraints
  • Ask about payment plans or financing options such as CareCredit, Scratchpay, or similar services
  • Inquire about which services are most critical versus which are optional or can be deferred
  • Look into low-cost clinics for basic preventive services such as vaccinations and spay/neuter
  • Consider pet insurance if you have not yet enrolled—it can significantly reduce the financial burden of unexpected illnesses and injuries (see our Pet Insurance Guide)
  • Research breed-specific rescue organizations and nonprofit funds that provide financial assistance for veterinary care

Prioritize preventive care, even on a tight budget. Annual wellness exams, vaccinations, and parasite prevention are far less expensive than treating the conditions they prevent.

When to Seek a Specialist vs. a General Practitioner

Your general practice veterinarian is your first line of care, and most health issues can be diagnosed and treated at the general practice level. However, certain situations warrant a referral to a board-certified veterinary specialist:

  • Complex or rare conditions: If your pet has been diagnosed with a condition that your general practitioner does not see regularly, a specialist with focused expertise can offer more informed treatment options and better outcomes.
  • Conditions not responding to initial treatment: If a prescribed treatment is not producing the expected improvement after a reasonable timeframe, a specialist's evaluation may reveal a different diagnosis or a more effective treatment approach.
  • Advanced surgical procedures: Orthopedic surgery (cruciate ligament repair, fracture repair), soft tissue surgery (complex tumor removal, portosystemic shunt correction), and neurological surgery (spinal disc surgery) often benefit from a board-certified surgeon's training and experience.
  • Cancer diagnosis: A veterinary oncologist can provide the most current and comprehensive treatment options, including chemotherapy, radiation therapy, immunotherapy, and clinical trial access.
  • Chronic conditions requiring ongoing management: Conditions such as uncontrolled diabetes, refractory epilepsy, or chronic kidney disease that are difficult to stabilize may benefit from an internist's evaluation.
  • Second opinions: If you have questions about a recommended treatment plan or want to explore alternatives, seeking a specialist's second opinion is entirely appropriate and should be welcomed by your general practitioner.

A referral to a specialist does not replace your general practitioner. Specialists typically work in consultation with your primary veterinarian, sending reports and recommendations back to them for ongoing coordinated care.

Building a Relationship with Your Veterinarian

The best veterinary care happens within the context of a long-term relationship built on mutual trust, open communication, and shared commitment to your pet's wellbeing. Here are practical ways to build and maintain a strong relationship with your veterinary team:

  • Keep regular appointments: Consistent visits establish baseline health data and allow your veterinarian to track changes over time. Skipping annual wellness exams means potential problems go undetected.
  • Be honest and thorough: Tell your veterinarian about your pet's diet (including treats and table food), exercise habits, behavioral changes, and any supplements or medications you are giving. Do not withhold information because you are embarrassed or afraid of being judged. Veterinarians need complete information to provide accurate care.
  • Follow recommendations or discuss alternatives: If your veterinarian recommends a treatment or diagnostic test that you cannot afford or are uncomfortable with, say so. A good veterinarian will work with you to find an alternative rather than insisting on the most expensive option.
  • Bring organized notes: Write down your questions and observations before each visit. Note any changes in appetite, water intake, energy level, bowel habits, urinary habits, or behavior. This information is clinically valuable and saves time during the appointment.
  • Provide feedback: If something about a visit bothered you—the wait time, the communication style, how your pet was handled—let the practice know. Most practices want to improve and appreciate constructive feedback from clients.
  • Express appreciation: Veterinary medicine is a demanding profession with high rates of burnout and compassion fatigue. Letting your veterinary team know that you appreciate their care makes a meaningful difference in their day and strengthens the relationship.

How to Switch Veterinarians If Needed

There are many legitimate reasons to switch veterinarians: you have moved, your current practice's hours or location no longer work for you, you feel your concerns are not being taken seriously, or you simply want a different communication style or approach. Switching veterinarians is your right as a pet owner, and you do not need to justify the decision to your current practice.

To make the transition smooth:

  1. Request a complete copy of your pet's medical records. Call or email your current veterinary clinic and ask for a copy of your pet's full medical history, including vaccination records, lab results, surgical reports, medication history, and any diagnostic imaging. Most clinics will provide records at no charge or for a small administrative fee. Under AVMA ethical guidelines and most state regulations, veterinary practices are required to provide copies of records upon request.
  2. Provide records to your new veterinarian. Bring or send the records to your new practice before or at your first appointment. A complete medical history allows your new veterinarian to understand your pet's baseline health, avoid repeating unnecessary tests, maintain continuity of ongoing treatments, and identify trends in lab values over time.
  3. Schedule a comprehensive first visit. Your initial appointment with a new veterinarian should include a thorough physical examination and a review of your pet's history. This is the ideal time to discuss your pet's current medications, dietary needs, behavioral concerns, and any ongoing health conditions.
  4. Update vaccinations and preventive care records. Ensure your new veterinarian has accurate vaccination dates to avoid unnecessary revaccination or gaps in protection. Also update your pet's microchip registration with your new veterinarian's contact information.

You do not need to "fire" your old veterinarian or provide an explanation for leaving. Simply request your records and begin care with your new provider. The transition does not need to be adversarial.

Telemedicine and Virtual Veterinary Visits

Telemedicine has become an increasingly available option in veterinary care, accelerated by technology adoption in recent years. Virtual veterinary visits can be conducted via video call, phone call, or sometimes through messaging platforms, and they offer genuine convenience in certain situations. However, it is important to understand both the capabilities and the limitations of virtual care.

When Telemedicine Works Well

  • Follow-up consultations: After an in-person visit, a virtual follow-up to discuss lab results, adjust medications, or assess recovery progress can be highly effective and saves a trip to the clinic.
  • Behavioral questions: Discussing behavioral concerns, training challenges, or environmental adjustments is well-suited to telemedicine because it often involves conversation rather than physical examination.
  • Triage and advice: When you are unsure whether a symptom warrants an in-person visit, a telemedicine consultation can help you determine the appropriate level of urgency.
  • Nutrition and husbandry guidance: Dietary recommendations, supplement discussions, and habitat or environmental advice for exotic pets can be effectively delivered remotely.
  • Chronic condition check-ins: For pets with well-managed chronic conditions, periodic telemedicine check-ins between in-person visits can help maintain continuity of care.

When Telemedicine Falls Short

  • New symptoms or acute illness: A veterinarian cannot perform a physical examination through a screen. They cannot palpate the abdomen, listen to the heart and lungs, check lymph nodes, assess hydration, or evaluate pain responses virtually. New or sudden symptoms generally require an in-person examination.
  • Diagnosis of new conditions: In most states, veterinarians cannot legally establish a new diagnosis or prescribe medications without a valid veterinarian-client-patient relationship (VCPR), which typically requires an in-person physical examination. Telemedicine regulations vary significantly by state, so check your state's rules.
  • Emergencies: Telemedicine should never replace emergency veterinary care. If your pet is experiencing difficulty breathing, uncontrolled bleeding, seizures, collapse, or other emergency symptoms, go to an emergency veterinary hospital immediately.
  • Procedures and treatments: Any condition requiring physical intervention—surgery, wound care, fluid administration, injections, or diagnostic sampling—obviously requires an in-person visit.

Telemedicine is best understood as a valuable supplement to traditional veterinary care rather than a replacement for it. The ideal approach is to establish a strong in-person relationship with a veterinarian and use telemedicine for the situations where it genuinely adds convenience without compromising care quality.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I take my pet to the veterinarian?

Most healthy adult dogs and cats should visit the veterinarian at least once per year for a comprehensive wellness examination. Puppies and kittens need more frequent visits during their first year for vaccinations and developmental checkups, typically every 3 to 4 weeks until 16 weeks of age. Senior pets (generally age 7 and older) benefit from twice-yearly examinations to catch age-related conditions early. Exotic pets such as birds and reptiles should also see an appropriate veterinarian at least annually. See our Preventive Veterinary Care Guide for detailed schedules by species and life stage.

What is AAHA accreditation and does it matter?

AAHA stands for the American Animal Hospital Association. AAHA accreditation is a voluntary process in which veterinary practices are evaluated against approximately 900 standards covering patient care, surgery, pharmacy, laboratory, exam facilities, medical records, cleanliness, and emergency preparedness. Only about 12 to 15 percent of veterinary practices in the United States and Canada are AAHA accredited. While a non-accredited practice can still provide excellent care, AAHA accreditation provides an independent verification that the practice meets high standards consistently.

Can I use a regular veterinarian for my exotic pet?

It depends on the veterinarian's training and experience. Most general practice veterinarians are trained primarily in dog and cat medicine. Exotic pets such as reptiles, birds, amphibians, and fish have unique physiologies and require specialized knowledge. Look for a veterinarian who is a member of the Association of Reptilian and Amphibian Veterinarians (ARAV) or the Association of Avian Veterinarians (AAV), or who holds board certification from the American Board of Veterinary Practitioners (ABVP) in avian, reptile and amphibian, or exotic companion mammal practice.

How do I transfer my pet's medical records to a new veterinarian?

Contact your current veterinary clinic and request a copy of your pet's complete medical records. Most clinics will provide records at no charge or for a small administrative fee. You can typically request records by phone, email, or in person. Some clinics will send records directly to your new veterinarian if you provide the new clinic's contact information and sign a release form. It is a good idea to keep your own copy of your pet's records as well. Medical records include vaccination history, lab results, surgical reports, medication history, and any diagnostic imaging.

Are virtual or telemedicine vet visits a good option?

Telemedicine veterinary visits can be useful for follow-up consultations, behavioral questions, minor concerns, medication refill discussions, and triage to determine whether an in-person visit is needed. However, virtual visits cannot replace physical examinations, and veterinarians cannot legally diagnose or prescribe medications in most states without an established veterinarian-client-patient relationship (VCPR) that includes a physical exam. Telemedicine works best as a supplement to, rather than a replacement for, regular in-person veterinary care.
