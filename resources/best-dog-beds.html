Best Dog Beds: How to Choose the Right Bed for Your Dog

Dogs sleep a lot -- somewhere between 12 and 14 hours a day on average, and even more for puppies and senior dogs. That is a huge chunk of their life spent lying down, which is exactly why the surface they sleep on matters more than most people think. A flat blanket tossed on the floor or a bargain-bin bed that went flat after a month is not giving your dog the support it needs, especially as it ages.

Here we walk through the main types of dog beds on the market, what to look for depending on your dog's size, age, and health, how to measure properly so the bed actually fits, and when it is time to replace the one you have. A well-chosen bed can genuinely improve quality of life, particularly for older dogs with joint stiffness or anxious dogs that need a secure spot to retreat to.

Why a Good Dog Bed Matters

A decent bed does more than keep your dog off the cold floor. Here is what it actually affects, and why a twenty-dollar bargain bed and a hundred-dollar orthopedic bed are not the same thing.

Joint Health and Orthopedic Support

Dogs that sleep on hard floors or flat, unsupportive surfaces place sustained pressure on their joints, particularly the hips, elbows, shoulders, and spine. Over time, this pressure can contribute to the development of calluses, pressure sores, and exacerbate conditions like arthritis, hip dysplasia, and elbow dysplasia. Large and giant breeds are especially vulnerable because their greater body weight concentrates more force on contact points. A properly supportive dog bed distributes your dog's weight evenly, reduces pressure on joints, and can slow the progression of degenerative joint conditions. For dogs already diagnosed with orthopedic issues, the right bed can significantly improve their comfort and mobility.

Sleep Quality

Just like humans, dogs need quality sleep to maintain physical health, cognitive function, and emotional regulation. Dogs cycle through stages of sleep including REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, during which dreaming occurs and important memory consolidation takes place. A comfortable, supportive bed allows dogs to enter deeper sleep stages more easily and stay asleep longer without being disturbed by discomfort. Dogs that sleep poorly may exhibit increased irritability, reduced training responsiveness, a weakened immune system, and slower recovery from illness or injury.

Temperature Regulation

Dogs regulate their body temperature differently than humans, and their sleeping surface plays a significant role. Hard tile or concrete floors can leach body heat during colder months, causing discomfort and potentially worsening joint stiffness. In warmer weather, thick, poorly ventilated beds can trap heat and cause overheating, which is particularly dangerous for brachycephalic breeds (like Bulldogs, Pugs, and Boston Terriers) that already struggle with temperature regulation. The right bed material and design—whether elevated for airflow, gel-infused for cooling, or insulated for warmth—helps your dog maintain a comfortable body temperature throughout the night and across seasons.

Anxiety Reduction and Security

A dedicated bed provides dogs with a personal space that serves as a den-like retreat. This sense of having their own territory is deeply rooted in canine psychology. Dogs that are anxious, nervous, or easily stressed often benefit significantly from having a consistent, comfortable space they can retreat to when they feel overwhelmed. Bolster beds and cave-style beds are particularly effective for anxious dogs because the raised edges or enclosed design create a sense of security and containment. Establishing a bed as a safe space can complement behavioral training and help manage separation anxiety, noise phobias, and generalized anxiety in dogs.

Types of Dog Beds

There are more styles of dog beds than you might expect. Knowing what each type is designed to do narrows the field fast.

Orthopedic and Memory Foam Beds

Orthopedic dog beds feature high-density memory foam, egg-crate foam, or medical-grade supportive foam that conforms to your dog's body shape and distributes weight evenly. These beds are specifically engineered to relieve pressure on joints and provide therapeutic support. The best orthopedic beds use a combination of foam layers—often a firm base layer for structural support topped with a softer comfort layer that molds to the dog's body. Memory foam responds to body heat, becoming softer where it contacts the dog and providing customized support. These beds are ideal for senior dogs, dogs with arthritis or hip dysplasia, post-surgical recovery, and large breeds predisposed to joint issues. Expect to pay more for genuine orthopedic beds, but the investment in your dog's joint health is typically well worth it.

Bolster and Nest Beds

Bolster beds feature raised edges or walls around the perimeter of the sleeping surface, creating a nest-like structure. Dogs can rest their heads on the raised edges like a pillow or curl up against them for a sense of enclosure. These beds are popular because they appeal to dogs' natural nesting instincts and provide a comfortable head rest. Bolster beds come in various shapes including round, oval, and rectangular, and the bolster height can range from a few inches to nearly a foot. They are well suited for dogs that like to curl up to sleep, lean against something while resting, or those that benefit from feeling enclosed. The bolster also helps contain dogs that move around during sleep, preventing them from rolling off the bed.

Flat and Mat Style Beds

Flat beds and mats are the simplest dog bed design: a padded surface without raised edges. They range from thin travel mats to thick, cushioned pads. Their low-profile design makes them easy to place in crates, under desks, in cars, and in other spaces where a bulkier bed would not fit. Flat beds are also a good choice for dogs that like to stretch out fully while sleeping, as there are no bolster walls restricting their movement. However, because they lack raised edges, they do not provide the same sense of security as bolster or cave beds, and dogs that sleep near the edge may partially slide off during the night. Quality varies significantly in this category, so look for models with adequate fill thickness and durable covers.

Elevated and Cot Style Beds

Elevated beds (also called raised beds or cot-style beds) feature a fabric sleeping surface stretched over a rigid frame, suspending the dog several inches off the ground. This design provides excellent airflow beneath the dog, making elevated beds particularly effective for temperature regulation in warm climates. The raised surface also keeps dogs off cold, damp, or hard floors. The fabric surface provides gentle, hammock-like support that can be comfortable for joints. Elevated beds are extremely durable—many use heavy-duty, rip-resistant fabric—and they are easy to clean since the fabric can be wiped down or hosed off. They are popular for outdoor use, kennels, and for dogs that tend to overheat. The main drawback is that they do not provide the same cushioned support as foam-based beds, and some dogs may find the slightly bouncy surface unfamiliar at first.

Cave and Hooded Beds

Cave beds (also called hooded or covered beds) feature an enclosed or semi-enclosed design that allows the dog to burrow inside, similar to sleeping under a blanket. These beds have a top cover, hood, or tent-like structure that creates a dark, cozy interior. Cave beds are excellent for dogs that naturally burrow—breeds like Dachshunds, Chihuahuas, and Terriers often love them—as well as anxious dogs that feel calmer in enclosed spaces. The covered design also helps retain body warmth, making cave beds a good choice for dogs that get cold easily or live in cooler climates. Smaller dogs tend to prefer cave beds, although larger versions are available. One consideration is that cave beds can trap heat, so they may not be appropriate for dogs in warm environments or breeds prone to overheating.

Heated Beds

Heated dog beds contain built-in heating elements, either electric or self-warming. Electric heated beds plug into a wall outlet and allow temperature control, while self-warming beds use reflective materials (like Mylar linings) to reflect the dog's own body heat back to them without electricity. Heated beds are particularly beneficial for senior dogs with arthritis (warmth helps relax muscles and ease joint stiffness), dogs with poor circulation, small breeds and short-coated breeds that chill easily, and dogs living in cold climates or poorly insulated spaces. When choosing an electric heated bed, prioritize models with chew-resistant cords, automatic shut-off timers, and adjustable temperature settings. Self-warming beds are safer for unsupervised use and do not increase electricity costs, though they provide less intense heat than electric models.

Cooling Beds

Cooling dog beds help dissipate body heat and are designed for warm climates, summer months, or breeds that are prone to overheating. There are several cooling technologies used in dog beds. Gel-infused foam absorbs and disperses heat away from the dog's body. Pressure-activated cooling pads contain a gel that activates when the dog lies on it, providing a cooling sensation for several hours. Elevated beds provide cooling through increased airflow. Breathable mesh and open-weave fabrics allow air to circulate around the dog. Cooling beds are especially important for brachycephalic breeds (Bulldogs, Pugs, Boston Terriers), thick-coated breeds (Huskies, Malamutes, Bernese Mountain Dogs), senior dogs, overweight dogs, and any dog in a hot climate. These beds can help prevent heat-related illness and improve comfort during warm weather.

Choosing the Right Bed for Your Dog's Needs

Not every dog sleeps the same way, and a bed that works great for one dog might be completely wrong for another. Match the bed to your dog's actual situation -- not just their size, but their age, health, and temperament.

Puppies

Puppies are enthusiastic chewers and are likely to view a new bed as a toy, at least initially. Durability and washability should be your top priorities when choosing a puppy bed. Look for beds made with chew-resistant fabrics like ballistic nylon or ripstop material, and avoid beds with zippers, buttons, or decorative elements that a puppy could chew off and swallow. The bed should have a removable, machine-washable cover since puppies are prone to accidents during house training. Consider starting with a moderately priced flat or crate mat rather than an expensive orthopedic bed—you can upgrade once your puppy outgrows the chewing stage. If using a crate for training, choose a bed that fits the crate dimensions snugly so your puppy cannot bunch it up or get underneath it.

Senior Dogs

Senior dogs benefit more from a high-quality bed than any other age group. As dogs age, they develop joint stiffness, muscle weakness, and often arthritis or other degenerative conditions. An orthopedic bed with high-density memory foam or multi-layered supportive foam is the best choice for senior dogs. Look for beds with a low entry point or step-in design so your dog does not have to climb over a tall bolster to get in and out. This is particularly important for dogs with mobility limitations. A non-slip bottom is essential to prevent the bed from sliding on hard floors when your senior dog steps on or off it. Heated options can provide additional comfort by soothing stiff joints and improving circulation. If your senior dog is incontinent, choose a bed with a waterproof liner beneath the removable cover to protect the foam insert from moisture damage.

Large and Giant Breeds

Large and giant breeds like Great Danes, Mastiffs, Saint Bernards, and Newfoundlands present unique challenges when it comes to dog beds. Their substantial weight means that standard beds compress and flatten quickly, losing their supportive properties. Invest in a bed specifically rated for your dog's weight, with high-density foam (at least 1.8 pounds per cubic foot density for dogs over 80 pounds) that resists compression over time. The bed should be large enough for your dog to stretch out fully—many large breed dogs sleep on their sides with legs extended, and a bed that is too small forces them into uncomfortable positions. Heavy-duty, reinforced stitching and durable fabric are important because the wear and tear from a large dog is significantly greater. Elevated cot-style beds rated for heavy weight are another excellent option for large breeds, as they provide consistent support without the issue of foam compression.

Small Breeds

Small breeds like Chihuahuas, Toy Poodles, Yorkshire Terriers, and Maltese often prefer cozy, enclosed sleeping spaces that make them feel secure. Bolster beds with proportionally sized raised edges, cave beds, and round nest beds are popular choices for small dogs. These breeds tend to get cold more easily due to their small body mass and higher surface-area-to-volume ratio, so beds that retain warmth—self-warming beds, hooded beds, or beds with plush faux-fur interiors—are particularly appreciated. Avoid beds that are oversized for your small dog, as they may not provide the snug, secure feeling that small breeds prefer. Look for beds with soft, plush surfaces since small dogs are lighter and benefit less from firm orthopedic foam designed to support heavier bodies.

Dogs with Arthritis

For dogs diagnosed with arthritis, the bed is not just a comfort item—it is a therapeutic tool. A high-quality orthopedic bed with memory foam at least 4 inches thick is the minimum recommendation. Memory foam conforms to the dog's body, evenly distributing weight and relieving pressure on inflamed joints. Some orthopedic beds designed for arthritic dogs incorporate dual-layer foam systems with a firm base layer and a softer top layer for optimal support and cushioning. Heated beds or self-warming beds provide additional therapeutic benefit, as warmth increases blood flow to stiff joints and relaxes surrounding muscles. The bed must have a low entry threshold so the dog does not have to step over a high bolster wall, which can cause pain. A waterproof liner is also practical since some medications for arthritis can increase urination frequency. Place the bed in a warm, draft-free location where your dog spends the most time, and consider having multiple beds if your dog frequents different rooms.

Anxious Dogs

Dogs with anxiety—whether separation anxiety, noise phobias, or generalized anxiety—often benefit from beds that provide a sense of enclosure and security. Cave beds, hooded beds, and deep-walled bolster beds create a den-like environment that can help calm nervous dogs. Calming beds specifically designed for anxious dogs often feature ultra-soft, faux-fur interiors, raised rims for head resting, and donut-shaped designs that mimic the feeling of being held. The soft, plush material provides tactile comfort that can reduce stress-related behaviors. Placing the bed in a quiet corner, inside a crate (if the dog is crate-trained), or in a consistent location helps establish the bed as a reliable safe space. Pairing the bed with positive reinforcement—treats and praise when the dog uses it—strengthens the association between the bed and feelings of calm and safety.

What to Look for When Buying a Dog Bed

The type of bed matters, but so do the details. These are the features that separate a bed you will replace in three months from one that lasts years.

Size

The bed must be large enough for your dog to lie comfortably in their preferred sleeping position. Dogs that stretch out on their sides need longer beds than dogs that curl up. Always measure your dog (instructions below) rather than guessing based on breed. When in doubt, choose the larger size—a bed that is slightly too large is far better than one that is too small. For bolster beds, measure the interior sleeping surface, not the overall dimensions that include the bolster walls.

Fill Material

The fill material is the most important factor in determining bed quality and longevity. High-density memory foam (at least 3 to 4 pounds per cubic foot for medium dogs; higher for large breeds) provides the best support and retains its shape longest. Standard polyester fiber fill is softer and cheaper but compresses quickly and needs frequent replacement. Egg-crate foam provides moderate support at a lower cost than solid memory foam. Recycled or shredded foam fill allows some customization of firmness but tends to become lumpy over time. Cedar-chip fill offers a natural scent that may repel insects but provides minimal cushioning on its own.

Cover Material

The cover material affects durability, comfort, ease of cleaning, and resistance to odors. Canvas and ballistic nylon are the most durable options and are best for destructive dogs. Microsuede and microfiber are soft, comfortable, and relatively easy to clean. Cotton and linen are breathable and comfortable but may stain more easily. Faux fur and sherpa are plush and warm, ideal for small breeds and anxious dogs, but they trap more hair and may retain odors. Mesh and open-weave fabrics promote airflow and are best for warm climates.

Washability

A dog bed that cannot be washed properly will accumulate dirt, dander, bacteria, and odors quickly. Prioritize beds with removable, machine-washable covers secured with a durable zipper. The inner foam or fill should be protected by a waterproof or water-resistant liner, and ideally the foam should be spot-cleanable or have its own washable cover. Beds marketed as "fully machine washable" (including the insert) are convenient but often contain lower-quality fill that compresses after repeated washing.

Non-Slip Bottom

A non-slip bottom is essential, especially for senior dogs, large dogs, and any dog on hard flooring. Without a non-slip base, the bed will slide across the floor every time your dog gets on or off it, creating a frustrating and potentially unsafe situation. Dogs with mobility issues may avoid a bed that moves unpredictably. Look for beds with rubberized dots, textured fabric, or a full non-slip coating on the bottom surface.

How to Measure Your Dog for a Bed

Proper measurement ensures you purchase the right size bed on the first try. Follow these steps for accurate measurements:

  • Step 1: Observe your dog's sleeping position. Watch your dog sleep for a few nights and note their preferred position. Side sleepers need longer beds, curlers need round or bolster beds, and sprawlers need the largest option available.
  • Step 2: Measure length. With your dog standing or lying on their side, measure from the tip of the nose to the base of the tail. This gives you the body length. Add 6 to 12 inches (15 to 30 cm) to allow room for stretching and repositioning. If your dog sleeps on their side with legs extended, measure from nose tip to the end of the extended back legs and add 6 inches.
  • Step 3: Measure width. Measure your dog from the widest point (usually shoulder to shoulder or hip to hip) while they are lying down. Add 6 to 12 inches to this measurement for the minimum bed width.
  • Step 4: Consider weight. Your dog's weight determines the fill density needed. Dogs under 30 pounds can use standard-density fills. Dogs between 30 and 70 pounds should have medium-density foam. Dogs over 70 pounds need high-density foam (1.8 pounds per cubic foot or higher) to prevent premature compression.
  • Step 5: Account for growth. If you are buying for a puppy, estimate their adult size based on breed and current growth trajectory, or choose a bed you are comfortable replacing as the puppy grows. Buying an adult-sized bed for a small puppy often results in the puppy ignoring it because it does not feel cozy or secure.

Maintenance and Cleaning Tips

A little weekly upkeep goes a long way. Clean beds last longer, smell better, and stay supportive. Here is a practical routine.

  • Vacuum weekly: Use a handheld vacuum or upholstery attachment to remove hair, dander, dirt, and debris from the bed surface. This prevents buildup that can contribute to odors and attract pests. For beds in high-shedding households, vacuuming two to three times per week is advisable.
  • Wash the cover every 1 to 2 weeks: Remove the cover and wash it in cold or warm water on a gentle cycle with a mild, pet-safe detergent. Avoid fabric softeners, which can irritate some dogs' skin and reduce the fabric's moisture-wicking properties. Tumble dry on low heat or air dry to prevent shrinking.
  • Spot clean accidents immediately: Blot up any urine, vomit, or other accidents as quickly as possible with paper towels. Then clean the area with an enzymatic cleaner specifically designed for pet stains. Enzymatic cleaners break down the proteins in biological fluids, eliminating odors at the molecular level rather than simply masking them. This also prevents your dog from being attracted back to the same spot.
  • Air out the foam insert monthly: On a dry, sunny day, remove the foam insert and place it outside in indirect sunlight for a few hours. Sunlight has natural antibacterial properties, and fresh air helps dissipate trapped odors and moisture. Do not leave foam in direct sunlight for extended periods, as UV exposure can degrade the foam material over time.
  • Inspect for damage regularly: Check seams, zippers, and the cover fabric for signs of wear or damage every time you wash the cover. Small tears can quickly become large ones under the stress of a dog stepping on and off the bed. Repair minor damage promptly to prevent further deterioration.
  • Rotate and flip the bed: If the bed design allows it, rotate it 180 degrees or flip the foam insert periodically. This distributes wear more evenly and prevents a single area from becoming permanently compressed. This is similar to rotating a mattress and can significantly extend the useful life of the fill material.
  • Use a waterproof liner: If your bed does not come with a waterproof liner between the cover and the foam, consider adding one. These liners protect the foam from moisture, urine, and sweat, which can break down foam over time and create a breeding ground for mold and bacteria.

When to Replace a Dog Bed

No bed lasts forever. When the foam has gone flat or your dog starts avoiding it, that is your cue. Here are the specific signs to watch for:

  • Visible flattening: The fill material has compressed to the point where it no longer springs back, and you can feel the floor through the bed when pressing down where your dog typically lies. This means the bed is no longer providing meaningful cushioning or support.
  • Lumps and uneven surfaces: The fill has shifted, clumped, or deteriorated, creating an uneven sleeping surface. This is particularly common with fiber-fill and shredded-foam beds and can cause uncomfortable pressure points.
  • Persistent odors: If the bed retains noticeable odors even after thorough washing of the cover and airing out the fill, bacteria have likely established themselves deep within the fill material. No amount of surface cleaning will resolve this.
  • Structural damage: Significant tears, broken zippers, fraying seams, or a non-slip bottom that has worn smooth all indicate that the bed's useful life is ending. While minor repairs are worthwhile, extensive damage suggests it is time for a new bed.
  • Your dog avoids the bed: If your dog used to sleep on their bed regularly but has started choosing the floor, couch, or other surfaces, the bed may have become uncomfortable. Dogs are practical—they choose the most comfortable option available to them.
  • Changes in your dog's health or size: If your dog has been diagnosed with a new joint condition, has gained or lost significant weight, or has entered their senior years, their bed needs may have changed even if the current bed is still in decent condition. Upgrading to an orthopedic bed when arthritis develops is a common and important transition.

As a general guideline, plan on replacing standard-quality beds every 1 to 2 years, moderate-quality beds every 2 to 3 years, and high-quality orthopedic beds every 3 to 5 years. These timelines assume regular cleaning and proper maintenance. Heavy-use situations, destructive dogs, and multiple-dog households may require more frequent replacement.

DIY vs. Store-Bought Dog Beds

Making a dog bed yourself is tempting, especially if you sew or work with wood. It can save money in some cases, but there are real trade-offs worth thinking through first.

Advantages of DIY Dog Beds

  • Customization: You can build a bed to the exact dimensions, shape, and material specifications your dog needs. This is particularly useful for unusually sized dogs or specific health requirements.
  • Cost savings (sometimes): If you already own a sewing machine and have basic skills, you may be able to produce a comparable bed for less than a premium store-bought option. Repurposing materials like old pillows, blankets, or foam pads can reduce costs further.
  • Material control: You choose every material that goes into the bed, which is beneficial if your dog has specific allergies or sensitivities to certain fabrics or chemicals.

Advantages of Store-Bought Dog Beds

  • Engineered support: Quality commercial beds, particularly orthopedic models, are designed with specific foam densities, layer configurations, and support profiles that are difficult to replicate at home. The foam used in commercial orthopedic beds is often medical-grade and purpose-manufactured for weight distribution.
  • Safety testing: Reputable manufacturers test their beds for safety, including non-toxic materials, flame resistance, and structural integrity. DIY beds do not undergo this testing.
  • Warranty and return policies: Most quality commercial beds come with warranties covering defects and premature wear. If the bed does not work for your dog, many retailers offer return or exchange options.
  • Convenience: A store-bought bed arrives ready to use. Factoring in the time spent sourcing materials, cutting fabric, sewing, and assembling a DIY bed, the time investment can be substantial.
  • Consistent quality: Commercial beds are manufactured to consistent specifications, so replacement beds will match the original. DIY beds are one-of-a-kind, and recreating an exact duplicate can be challenging.

For most people, buying from a reputable manufacturer is the better bet -- especially for dogs with joint problems, where the foam engineering really matters. DIY beds work well as travel beds, backup beds, or projects for experienced crafters who know how to build something sturdy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Experienced dog owners consistently report that paying attention to this detail early on prevents larger problems down the road. Start with the fundamentals and refine your approach as you learn your dog's individual preferences and needs.

How often should I replace my dog's bed?

Most dog beds should be replaced every 1 to 3 years, depending on quality, usage, and your dog's size. Signs that it is time for a replacement include visible flattening of the fill material where your dog typically lies, lumps or uneven surfaces that no longer provide consistent support, persistent odors that remain after washing, torn or fraying fabric that cannot be repaired, and your dog choosing to sleep elsewhere instead of on the bed. Orthopedic beds with high-density memory foam tend to last longer than polyester-fill beds.

What size dog bed should I get for my dog?

To determine the right bed size, measure your dog from the tip of the nose to the base of the tail while they are lying in their preferred sleeping position. Add 6 to 12 inches to that measurement for the minimum bed length. For width, measure your dog from shoulder to shoulder and add 6 to 12 inches. If your dog likes to stretch out fully, choose a bed that accommodates their full length with legs extended. If your dog curls up to sleep, a round or bolster bed slightly larger than their curled diameter works well. When in doubt, size up rather than down.

Are orthopedic dog beds worth the extra cost?

Orthopedic dog beds are worth the investment for senior dogs, large and giant breeds, dogs with arthritis or joint conditions, dogs recovering from surgery, and breeds predisposed to hip dysplasia or elbow dysplasia. The high-density memory foam or supportive foam layers distribute your dog's weight evenly, reducing pressure on joints and providing relief from pain. For healthy young dogs of small to medium size, a standard quality bed may be sufficient, but an orthopedic bed can still provide preventive joint support. Think of it as an investment in your dog's long-term comfort and mobility.

Can I wash my dog's bed in a regular washing machine?

Many dog beds are designed with removable, machine-washable covers that can be washed in a standard household washing machine. Check the care label for specific instructions. Use a gentle or delicate cycle with cold or warm water and a mild, pet-safe detergent free of heavy fragrances and dyes. Avoid bleach unless the manufacturer specifically permits it. For the inner foam or filling, most cannot go in the washing machine. Instead, spot clean with a mild detergent solution and allow to air dry completely before reassembling. Some smaller beds with polyester fill can be washed whole, but always verify with the manufacturer's care instructions first.

Should I let my dog sleep in my bed instead of buying a dog bed?

Whether your dog sleeps in your bed or their own is a personal choice with trade-offs. A dedicated dog bed offers several advantages: it provides support specifically designed for canine joints, establishes healthy boundaries and a consistent sleep routine, reduces allergen exposure for sensitive owners, and gives your dog a designated safe space of their own. Co-sleeping can strengthen the bond between dog and owner but may disrupt sleep quality for light sleepers, contribute to separation anxiety, and is not ideal for dogs with incontinence or mobility issues that make getting on and off the bed difficult. Many owners find a compromise works best, with the dog bed placed in the bedroom near the owner's bed.
