Best Cat Trees and Scratching Posts: Expert Guide for Every Budget (2026)

Scratching posts and cat trees tend to get lumped in with toys and accessories, but they are closer to necessities. Cats scratch because they have to -- it keeps their claws healthy, marks territory, and gives them a full-body stretch they cannot get any other way. And vertical space? That is a basic environmental need for a species that instinctively wants to climb and perch. Skipping these items does not just deprive your cat; it usually means your furniture pays the price instead.

This page covers the major styles of cat trees and scratching posts, which materials actually last, and how to pick something your cat will use instead of ignoring. We also get into placement, introducing new furniture, and DIY options for people who prefer building their own.

Why Cats Need Scratching Surfaces and Vertical Space

Scratching and climbing look like recreation, but they are closer to biological necessities. Cats that cannot do either tend to develop problems -- wrecked furniture, aggression, anxiety, or all three.

The Biology of Scratching

Scratching serves several essential functions for cats. First, it is the primary mechanism for maintaining claw health. The scratching motion removes the dead outer sheaths of the claws, exposing sharp new growth underneath. Without regular scratching, these sheaths can build up and cause discomfort or even ingrown claws. Second, scratching is a full-body stretch that engages the muscles of the shoulders, legs, paws, and spine. Cats typically scratch after waking from a nap, extending their bodies fully to maintain flexibility and muscle tone. Third, scratching deposits scent from the interdigital glands (located between the toes) onto surfaces, creating territorial markers that are both visual (scratch marks) and olfactory (pheromones). This scent communication is critical for cats' sense of security in their environment. A cat that cannot scratch is a cat whose fundamental needs are being unmet, which often manifests as stress, anxiety, or destructive behavior directed at furniture, carpets, and doorframes.

Why Vertical Space Matters

Cats are semi-arboreal animals. In the wild, they climb trees and elevated surfaces to survey their territory for threats and prey, to escape ground-level dangers, and to rest in secure locations where they are less vulnerable. Domestic cats retain these instincts completely. Providing vertical space through cat trees, shelves, and perches effectively expands your cat's perceived territory without adding square footage to your home. This is particularly valuable in smaller living spaces and in multi-cat households where competition for territory can lead to aggression, bullying, and stress-related health problems. A cat with access to vertical space is typically calmer, more confident, and less likely to develop behavioral issues. Elevated resting spots also give cats a sense of control over their environment, which is a core psychological need for this species.

The Cost of Not Providing Cat Furniture

Owners who skip cat trees and scratching posts often end up paying far more in damaged furniture, carpet repairs, and veterinary bills for stress-related conditions. Cats deprived of appropriate scratching and climbing outlets will find their own—your couch, your curtains, your bookshelves. Providing purpose-built cat furniture from the start is both the humane choice and the economically smart one.

Types of Cat Trees

Cat trees range from simple two-tier platforms to full floor-to-ceiling structures. Here is what each major type offers and where it fits best.

Floor-to-Ceiling Cat Trees

Floor-to-ceiling cat trees (also called ceiling-height or tension-mounted trees) extend from the floor to the ceiling, typically adjustable for ceiling heights of 7.5 to 9.5 feet. They are secured at the top by a tension rod or ceiling bracket, providing exceptional stability even for large, active cats. These trees maximize vertical space and usually include multiple platforms, condos, hammocks, and scratching posts along their height. Floor-to-ceiling trees are the best option for multi-cat households, highly active cats, and large breeds that need robust structures. The main drawback is their footprint—they require dedicated floor space and are not easily moved. They also tend to be the most expensive category of cat tree. When choosing a floor-to-ceiling model, verify the ceiling height range, weight capacity, and whether it uses tension mounting (adjustable spring rod) or bracket mounting (screwed into the ceiling) for stability.

Compact and Mid-Size Cat Trees

Compact cat trees stand between 2 and 5 feet tall and occupy a relatively small footprint, making them the most popular choice for average households. They typically feature two to four platforms, one or two scratching posts, and possibly a condo or hammock. Freestanding and stable without ceiling attachment, these trees work well in apartments, bedrooms, and living rooms. Mid-size trees (4 to 6 feet) offer more platforms and features while still fitting in most rooms. This category offers the widest variety of designs, from minimalist modern aesthetics to traditional carpet-covered towers. Quality varies enormously in this price range, so pay close attention to the base dimensions (wider is more stable), post diameter (thicker is sturdier), and overall weight capacity.

Wall-Mounted Cat Furniture

Wall-mounted cat shelves, steps, and perches attach directly to the wall, creating climbing paths and resting spots without using any floor space at all. This makes wall-mounted systems ideal for small apartments, minimalist interiors, and creative cat owners who want to design custom climbing routes. Individual shelves, bridges, and hammocks can be arranged in virtually any configuration, and the system can be expanded over time. Installation requires drilling into wall studs or using appropriate anchors, so they are better suited for homeowners than renters (though removable options with minimal wall damage do exist). The modular nature of wall-mounted systems means they can be adapted as your cat's needs change. Some cats take to wall-mounted furniture immediately; others need encouragement with treats and play to learn the new routes.

Window Perches

Window perches are platform-style beds that mount to a window frame or attach with suction cups, giving cats a comfortable spot to observe the outdoors. They are sometimes called cat window hammocks or cat window seats. Window perches satisfy cats' desire to watch birds, squirrels, and activity outside—a form of environmental enrichment sometimes called "cat television." They occupy zero floor space and are usually easy to install and remove. Suction cup models work on most windows but have weight limits (typically 25 to 40 pounds) and should be checked regularly for secure adhesion. Frame-mounted models are more secure and can support heavier cats. Window perches work well as a supplement to a cat tree rather than a replacement, since they provide a resting spot but not scratching surfaces or climbing opportunities.

What to Look for When Choosing Cat Furniture

A cheap cat tree and a good one can look similar in photos. The difference usually shows up in stability, materials, and how long it lasts before falling apart. Here is what to check.

Stability

Stability is the single most important feature of any cat tree. If a cat tree wobbles, sways, or tips when your cat jumps onto it, your cat will stop using it—and rightfully so. Cats are acutely aware of the stability of surfaces they stand on, and an unstable perch triggers their survival instincts. Look for a wide, heavy base relative to the tree's height. A general rule: the base should be at least as wide as the tallest point is high divided by two. Heavier trees are more stable. Posts with larger diameters (3.5 inches or thicker) resist swaying better than narrow posts. For tall trees, wall or ceiling anchoring provides additional security. If you have large cats (15 pounds or more), stability becomes even more critical, as their jumping force places significant lateral stress on the structure.

Materials and Construction

The materials used in a cat tree determine its durability, your cat's interest in using it, and how it integrates with your home decor. The structural core should be solid wood, heavy-duty particleboard, or engineered wood—avoid trees that rely on hollow cardboard tubes for primary support. The covering material varies: plush carpet, faux fur, fleece, or sherpa for platforms and condos (comfort-focused), and sisal rope, sisal fabric, or other scratchable materials for posts. Look for trees where the scratching surfaces are replaceable or repairable, since the scratching areas wear out long before the structural components. Quality hardware (bolts rather than only screws, metal brackets at stress points) adds longevity and safety.

Sisal Rope vs. Carpet vs. Cardboard Scratching Surfaces

The scratching material is one of the most debated aspects of cat furniture. Each material has distinct advantages. Sisal rope (natural fiber rope wrapped around posts) provides a satisfying, shreddable texture that most cats love. It wears visibly over time, which is actually appealing to cats since they can see and smell their scent marks. Sisal fabric or sisal mat (woven flat material) lasts significantly longer than rope and provides a consistent scratching surface. Many behaviorists consider it the superior scratching material. Carpet (low-pile or commercial grade) is the most common covering on budget cat trees. While some cats will scratch carpet-covered posts, it can confuse cats about which carpets are acceptable to scratch. Cardboard scratchers are inexpensive and many cats enjoy the texture, but they wear out quickly, create debris, and are typically supplemental rather than primary scratching surfaces. Bare wood or bark-covered logs appeal to some cats' instinct to scratch natural materials and last virtually forever, though they are less common in commercial products.

Size and Your Cat's Needs

Match the cat tree to your cat's size, age, and activity level. Kittens need shorter trees with closely spaced platforms they can safely reach. Active adult cats benefit from taller structures with varied levels and features. Senior cats need lower platforms, wider steps, and ramps or gradual inclines instead of large gaps between levels. Large breeds like Maine Coons, Ragdolls, and Norwegian Forest Cats need trees with oversized platforms (at least 18 inches in diameter), thick posts (4 inches or more), reinforced joints, and weight ratings of 40 pounds or more per platform. A tree designed for a 10-pound cat will not adequately serve a 20-pound Maine Coon.

Multi-Cat Considerations

In households with multiple cats, cat furniture takes on additional importance. Each cat needs access to resting spots and scratching surfaces without being forced into close proximity with other cats if they do not want it. Look for trees with multiple platforms at different heights, multiple entry and exit points (so one cat cannot trap another), and separate condos or enclosed spaces. A general guideline is to provide at least one dedicated resting spot per cat, plus one extra. In multi-cat homes, it is often better to have two or three moderate cat trees placed in different rooms than one large tree in a single location, as this distributes resources and reduces territorial conflict.

Best Cat Trees by Category

Rather than recommending specific brands (which change constantly), here is what to prioritize in each common situation so you know what features matter most for your household.

Best for Budget-Conscious Buyers

If you are spending under $60 to $80, focus on the essentials: a stable base, at least one sisal-wrapped scratching post of adequate height (30 inches minimum for adult cats), and two or three platforms. Skip elaborate condos and hammocks at this price point—the money is better spent on structural quality. A simple two-to-three-tier tree with thick sisal posts will serve your cat far better than an elaborate six-tier tree made with flimsy materials that wobbles at the slightest touch. Check the base dimensions and overall weight. A budget tree that weighs less than 15 pounds is likely too light to be stable. At this price range, expect to replace the tree every 2 to 3 years as the sisal wears and the structure loosens.

Best for Large Cats

Large breeds (Maine Coons, Ragdolls, British Shorthairs, Norwegian Forest Cats, Savannahs) and overweight cats need cat trees specifically engineered for their size and weight. Prioritize trees with reinforced bases at least 24 inches wide, solid wood or heavy-duty engineered wood construction, posts at least 4 inches in diameter, platforms at least 18 by 18 inches (larger is better), and an overall weight capacity clearly stated at 50 pounds or more. Perches and hammocks should be attached with bolts rather than screws. Floor-to-ceiling models with ceiling tension mounts offer the best stability for large cats. Expect to invest $150 to $350 for a tree that will safely support a large cat long-term. This is not an area to cut corners, as a structural failure under a heavy cat can cause injury.

Best for Small Apartments

In apartments where floor space is at a premium, look for cat trees with a small footprint but significant vertical height—essentially a tall, narrow design. Trees with a base of 18 by 18 inches or less can fit in corners and against walls without consuming living space. Wall-mounted cat shelves are another excellent apartment solution since they use zero floor space. A combination of a compact floor tree and a window perch can provide comprehensive enrichment in a small space. Corner-specific cat trees are designed to nestle into room corners, making efficient use of otherwise wasted space. Some modern designs incorporate cat furniture into human furniture—side tables with built-in cat condos, bookshelves with cat-accessible platforms—which serves double duty in tight quarters.

Best for Multi-Cat Households

Multi-cat homes should prioritize trees with the most platforms and levels for the budget, multiple access points at each level, and spacious platforms that allow cats to pass each other without confrontation. Look for at least one enclosed condo per two cats, as condos provide important hiding spots for less dominant cats. Avoid trees where the only way to reach the top is a single vertical path, as this creates bottleneck points where dominant cats can block access. Trees with branching structures—where different paths lead to different platforms—are ideal. As mentioned, distributing multiple trees across your home is often more effective than relying on one large structure.

Best for Senior Cats

Senior cats still benefit from vertical space and scratching surfaces, but their reduced mobility means you need to adapt. Choose shorter trees (2 to 4 feet) with platforms spaced no more than 10 to 12 inches apart vertically, so your senior cat can step from one level to the next rather than jumping. Ramps and gradual inclines are excellent additions. Platforms should be larger and padded for joint comfort—orthopedic or memory foam platform cushions are a worthwhile upgrade. Non-slip surfaces on platforms prevent slipping. Low-entry condos (openings at platform level rather than requiring a step up) make enclosed spaces accessible. Scratching posts do not need to be as tall for seniors; a 20-to-24-inch post at a slight angle can be easier for cats with arthritis to use than a tall vertical post.

Placement Tips for Cat Trees and Scratching Posts

Where you put the tree matters almost as much as which tree you buy. A great cat tree in a back room nobody uses will collect dust. A decent tree next to a sunny window will get used daily.

  • Place near windows: Cats are drawn to windows for bird watching, sunbathing, and monitoring outdoor activity. A cat tree positioned near a window becomes an immediately desirable perch. This single tip alone accounts for more successful cat tree placements than any other factor.
  • Choose social areas, not back rooms: Cats are more social than many people assume, and they want to be near the household activity. Place cat trees in living rooms, family rooms, or home offices where people spend time. Hiding a cat tree in a guest bedroom or basement dramatically reduces its use because cats want to observe and be part of the household.
  • Put scratching posts near sleeping areas: Cats frequently scratch immediately after waking from a nap. Placing a scratching post or tree near your cat's favorite sleeping spot capitalizes on this natural behavior pattern. If your cat sleeps on the couch, put the scratcher right next to the couch.
  • Position near problem areas: If your cat is scratching a specific piece of furniture, place the scratching post directly in front of or beside that furniture. Once your cat is consistently using the post, you can gradually (a few inches per day) move it to a more convenient location.
  • Provide one scratching surface per floor: In multi-story homes, provide at least one scratching option on each floor your cat has access to. Cats will not go to a different floor to scratch when the urge strikes—they will use whatever is nearby.
  • Avoid unstable surfaces: Place cat trees on hard, flat flooring whenever possible. Trees placed on thick carpet or uneven surfaces wobble more, which discourages use. If your floors are all carpeted, consider placing a thin plywood board under the base for added stability.
  • Consider multi-cat dynamics: In multi-cat homes, avoid placing all cat furniture in one room. Distribute trees and scratching posts throughout the house so less dominant cats have access to resources without having to pass through another cat's territory.

How to Get Cats to Use New Furniture

Do not expect love at first sight. Cats are territorial creatures, and a large new object in their space can provoke anything from curiosity to outright suspicion. A few tricks speed things along.

Let Curiosity Work Naturally

When you first set up the cat tree, resist the urge to pick up your cat and place them on it. Forcing interaction creates negative associations. Instead, position the tree and let your cat investigate on their own schedule. Most cats will approach, sniff, and begin rubbing their face on the new furniture within hours. Full climbing and scratching may take a few days. Patience is essential.

Use Scent Transfer

Rub a soft cloth on your cat's cheeks (where the facial pheromone glands are located) and then rub that cloth on the platforms and posts of the new tree. This transfers your cat's scent onto the furniture, making it smell familiar rather than foreign. You can also place a worn t-shirt or blanket that carries your scent on one of the platforms to further increase comfort.

Strategic Catnip and Treats

Sprinkle dried catnip on the platforms and scratching surfaces. Approximately 50 to 70 percent of cats respond to catnip with increased interest and playful behavior. For cats that do not respond to catnip, try silvervine (which affects a higher percentage of cats) or valerian root. Place treats on different levels of the tree, starting low and working up, to encourage climbing exploration. A lickable treat spread on a platform creates a positive association with being on the tree.

Interactive Play Sessions

Use a wand toy or feather toy to lure your cat onto and around the cat tree during play sessions. Drag the toy along the scratching posts to encourage scratching behavior. Play sessions near and on the tree build positive associations and help your cat discover all the features. This is particularly effective for younger, play-motivated cats.

Positive Reinforcement

When your cat uses the tree or scratching post voluntarily, reward them immediately with a treat, verbal praise, or a chin scratch—whichever your cat values most. Never punish your cat for scratching furniture and then expect the cat tree to be a welcome alternative. Punishment creates stress, which actually increases unwanted scratching behavior. Redirection and positive reinforcement are far more effective.

DIY Cat Tree and Scratching Post Options

If you like building things, a DIY cat tree or scratching post can save money and give you exactly the setup your cat needs. Just be serious about safety -- cats put real force on these structures when they jump.

Simple DIY Scratching Post

The most straightforward DIY project is a basic scratching post. You need a wooden base (at least 16 by 16 inches and 1 inch thick for stability), a wooden post (a 4-by-4-inch lumber cut to at least 30 inches tall), sisal rope (3/8 inch diameter, approximately 100 to 200 feet depending on post height), wood glue, and screws. Secure the post to the base with screws driven up from the bottom, apply wood glue to the post, and wrap the sisal rope tightly from bottom to top, securing the start and end with staples or tacks. The key to a good DIY post is making the base heavy enough that the post does not tip. Adding a heavy-duty rubber mat under the base improves grip on hard floors.

DIY Cat Shelves

Wall-mounted cat shelves can be built from solid wood planks (at least 10 inches wide and 18 inches long), heavy-duty L-brackets rated for the weight (use brackets rated for at least twice your cat's weight), and carpet remnants or sisal fabric glued to the top surface for grip. Space shelves 12 to 16 inches apart vertically to create a staircase pattern cats can climb. Every shelf must be anchored into wall studs, not just drywall—a shelf that pulls out of the wall under a jumping cat's weight is a serious safety hazard.

DIY Considerations and Safety

  • Stability testing: Before letting your cat use any DIY structure, push on it firmly from multiple angles. It should not wobble, tilt, or shift. If it moves at all, reinforce the base or add wall anchors.
  • Avoid toxic materials: Use only pet-safe wood stains, finishes, and adhesives. Many standard wood stains, paints, and polyurethane coatings contain volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that are harmful to cats. Water-based, non-toxic options are available at most hardware stores. Untreated, natural wood is the safest option.
  • No exposed hardware: Ensure all screws, staples, and nails are countersunk or covered. Exposed hardware can catch on fur, scratch skin, or injure paws.
  • Material quality: Use real sisal rope, not polypropylene or nylon rope that looks similar. Synthetic ropes do not provide the right texture, can cause static, and cats generally reject them.
  • Weight-test everything: Put weight on every platform and shelf that exceeds your heaviest cat's weight by at least a factor of three. This accounts for the dynamic force of a cat jumping onto the surface, which is substantially greater than their static weight.

Simple scratching posts and wall shelves are doable for most handy cat owners. Full multi-level trees are a serious woodworking project -- if the engineering is beyond your skill level, a commercial tree from a good manufacturer is the safer route.

Frequently Asked Questions

How tall should a cat tree be?

A cat tree should be at least 3 feet tall to provide meaningful vertical space, but most cats prefer taller structures in the range of 5 to 6 feet that allow them to survey their territory from above. Floor-to-ceiling models (typically 7 to 9 feet) are ideal for active, climbing-oriented cats and multi-cat households where vertical territory reduces conflict. For senior cats or kittens, a shorter tree of 2 to 3 feet with wider, more accessible platforms is safer and more practical. Consider your ceiling height, the room's layout, and your cat's age and mobility when deciding on height.

Why does my cat scratch furniture instead of the scratching post?

Cats may ignore a scratching post for several reasons. The post may be too short or unstable, causing it to wobble when the cat tries to use it. Cats need to fully stretch their bodies while scratching, so the post should be at least as tall as your cat when stretched out on hind legs (typically 30 to 32 inches minimum for adult cats). The material may not appeal to your cat, as some cats prefer sisal rope, others prefer sisal fabric, and some prefer cardboard or bare wood. Location also matters: place the post near where your cat already scratches, near sleeping areas, or in high-traffic social zones. Avoid hiding posts in back corners, as cats scratch partly to mark territory in visible areas.

Is sisal rope or sisal fabric better for cat scratching posts?

Both sisal rope and sisal fabric are excellent scratching surfaces, but they offer different experiences. Sisal rope is the traditional option, wrapped tightly around a post. It provides a satisfying texture and visible shred marks that cats enjoy. However, sisal rope can unravel over time and may need rewrapping. Sisal fabric (also called sisal mat or sisal carpet) is a woven flat material that is more durable, lasts longer than rope, and does not unravel. Many cats actually prefer sisal fabric because it provides consistent resistance across the entire surface. If you are unsure which your cat prefers, look for a cat tree that includes both materials on different posts so your cat can choose.

Can a cat tree help with behavioral problems?

Yes, a cat tree can help address several common behavioral problems. Destructive scratching on furniture often resolves when cats have appropriate scratching surfaces. Aggression between cats in multi-cat households frequently stems from insufficient territory, and vertical space from cat trees effectively expands the perceived territory. Anxiety and stress-related behaviors like hiding, overgrooming, or inappropriate elimination can improve when cats have elevated safe spaces where they feel secure. Boredom-driven behaviors such as excessive vocalization, knocking items off counters, or nighttime hyperactivity may decrease when cats have climbing and perching opportunities that provide mental and physical stimulation throughout the day.

How do I get my cat to use a new cat tree?

Introduce a new cat tree gradually rather than expecting immediate use. Place it in a room your cat already frequents, ideally near a window or in a social area. Rub a soft cloth on your cat's cheeks and then rub it on the tree to transfer familiar scent. Sprinkle dried catnip on the platforms or use a catnip spray. Place treats on different levels to encourage exploration. Use an interactive wand toy to lure your cat onto the tree during play sessions. Never force your cat onto the tree or place them on it against their will, as this creates negative associations. Most cats begin using a new tree within a few days to two weeks. If your cat shows no interest after two weeks, try moving the tree to a different location, as placement is often the issue.
