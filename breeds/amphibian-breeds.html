Amphibian Species Guides

Browse our complete collection of amphibian care guides covering frogs, toads, salamanders, and newts. Each guide provides detailed information on habitat setup, diet, health, temperament, and costs.

Amphibians make fascinating pets that bridge the gap between aquatic and terrestrial worlds. From the vibrant colors of poison dart frogs (safely captive-bred) to the endearing smile of axolotls, these creatures offer an unique window into one of nature's most diverse animal groups. Whether you're drawn to the low-maintenance care of tree frogs or the interactive nature of fire-bellied toads, our breed directory covers everything you need to know.

Choosing the Right Amphibian

Amphibian care looks simple from a distance — small tank, live bugs, mist the glass — but the margins are tight. Temperature, humidity, and water chemistry matter far more than they do with a mammal, and most losses come from getting one of those three wrong. Use the checklist below before buying:

  • Habitat type: Some amphibians are fully aquatic (like axolotls), some are terrestrial (like toads), and others need both land and water (like fire-bellied newts)
  • Humidity requirements: Most amphibians need 60-80% humidity, which means investing in misting systems or manual spraying schedules
  • Temperature sensitivity: Amphibians are ectothermic and highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations — consistency is critical
  • Handling tolerance: Most amphibians should be handled minimally, as oils and chemicals on human skin can be harmful to their permeable skin
  • Diet: Most pet amphibians eat live insects (crickets, fruit flies, worms) dusted with calcium and vitamin supplements
  • Lifespan: Pet amphibians can live 5-20+ years depending on species, so plan for a long-term commitment

Real-World Owner Insight

Talk to longtime caretakers of Amphibian Breeds and a more textured picture emerges, one shaped by routines rather than averages. Body language and appetite shifts are often hours ahead of obvious behavioral changes. Expect narrow preferences about drinking water, food texture, and resting surfaces — and accommodate them where reasonable. A reader described a stretch of rainy days where the usual morning routine collapsed, and it took almost two weeks to rebuild a rhythm that had felt automatic before. If something that used to work fails, inspect environment and schedule before concluding the pet has a behavior problem.

  • A budget reality check: the second year of ownership tends to cost less than the first, but only if the foundational gear choices held up.
  • Hold two or three clinics in your contacts, not a single primary, so that holiday closures do not leave you scrambling.

Local Vet & Care Considerations

Finding a herp-experienced exotic veterinarian is the single biggest predictor of whether a sick amphibian gets treated or written off. ARAV (Association of Reptilian and Amphibian Veterinarians) maintains a searchable directory; call candidates before you need one and ask specifically whether they treat amphibians, not just reptiles. Urban and coastal regions tend to have deeper rosters — inland or rural keepers often rely on telehealth follow-ups with a distant specialist plus a local general-practice DVM for injections and supportive care. Budget $75–$220 for an initial exotic consult, and factor in a second-opinion option for anything beyond routine husbandry advice.

Day-to-day care decisions usually come down to water quality and temperature rather than veterinary intervention. A TDS meter, a reliable thermometer at both basking and cool ends, and a humidity gauge that you trust will catch 80% of problems before they become vet visits. Log ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate weekly for aquatic species; log substrate moisture and ambient temp daily for terrestrial ones. Seasonal temperature swings are the most common cause of mid-year appetite loss — a cheap room thermometer placed where the enclosure actually sits usually explains it.

Amphibian Husbandry Primer

Amphibians are not miniature reptiles. Their skin is a permeable organ that absorbs water, dissolved gases, and — unfortunately — soaps, lotions, and residual cleaning products. Rinse your hands with dechlorinated water before any handling, and keep handling to genuine care needs rather than interaction time. If you must move an animal, damp powder-free nitrile gloves are safer for both of you than bare hands, which transfer oils and warmth that stress sensitive species.

Water is the single most important variable you can control. Tap water in most municipalities contains chlorine, chloramine, or both; a standard dechlorinator neutralizes chlorine but not chloramine, so check your utility's water quality report and choose a conditioner that handles your actual supply. For aquatic species (axolotls, African clawed frogs, newts) cycle the tank for four to six weeks before adding animals, and target nitrates below 20 ppm. For terrestrial species, mist with distilled or reverse-osmosis water to avoid mineral deposits on the glass and on shed skin.

Lighting is often over-specified and under-calibrated. Most amphibians are crepuscular or nocturnal and do not need UVB at the intensities reptiles require; a low-output 2.0 or 5.0 UVB tube over a screen top is sufficient for species that benefit at all, and many (axolotls most notably) are actively stressed by bright light. Provide a genuine photoperiod — 12 hours on, 12 hours off, shifted seasonally — and use a timer rather than relying on memory.

Feeding is where first-year owners most often overshoot. Adult frogs and toads eat two to three times per week; salamanders and newts often eat less. Variety matters more than volume: rotate crickets, dubia roaches, black soldier fly larvae, and occasional earthworms, and dust with a calcium-plus-D3 supplement at most feedings and a multivitamin at roughly one in four. Overfeeding is a far more common cause of early mortality than underfeeding, and a slightly lean amphibian almost always outlives an obese one.
