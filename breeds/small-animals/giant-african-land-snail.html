Giant African Land Snail Overview

In the United States, the giant African land snail is federally regulated. The animal on this page is Lissachatina fulica, often sold under the older name Achatina fulica. Import, interstate movement, and possession are illegal without a USDA permit because this snail is a major crop pest. A shop label, a care card, or a low price does not replace that permit. This page is not a guide to obtaining one, hiding one, or shipping one. The legal status comes before any husbandry.

The card lists an invertebrate of 6-8 inches, a lifespan of 5-10 years, a docile and low-maintenance temperament, beginner difficulty, and an origin in East Africa. Those lines describe the animal where keeping it is legal. They do not authorize possession in the United States. Beginner and low-maintenance are husbandry words. They are not a reading of federal pest law. A snail that can reach 6-8 inches and live 5-10 years is a long-lived crop pest, which is why the permit exists.

The cost table on the card is initial setup $100-$500+, animal purchase $10-$500+, annual food and bedding $150-$500, and annual vet care $50-$200. Print those figures as the card's budget, not as a shopping list. A purchase price in the $10-$500+ range does not make an unpermitted snail legal to buy, to move across a state line, or to bring into the country. Spending the setup money on a secure enclosure is irrelevant if possession itself is prohibited where you live.

Where these snails are legal to keep, the husbandry that actually matters is calcium, humidity, and a secure lid. The shell is built from calcium, and a snail kept short of it grows a thin, pitted shell even if the vegetable bowl looks full. They need the air in the enclosure damp, not a dry box with a water dish in one corner. They are strong for an animal that looks slow, and a loose lid or a gap at the rim is an escape. A 6-8 inch snail on the floor of a house is already a containment failure.

They reproduce quickly. A legal keeper who starts with one or two can end up with a clutch, and then with far more snails than the original enclosure was built for, inside that same 5-10 year lifespan. Eggs should not be released outdoors. Do not tip substrate, egg clutches, or unwanted snails into a garden, a compost pile, or a ditch. That is how a crop pest leaves a terrarium. Surplus animals are a containment problem, not a gift to the yard.

Housing and environment design for Giant African Land Snail has evolved considerably as our understanding of small animal welfare has improved. The size and complexity of the enclosure or hutch directly correlates with your Giant African Land Snail's physical health, behavioral normality, and overall lifespan. Experienced Giant African Land Snail keepers consistently advocate for the largest enclosure or hutch that space and budget allow, supplemented with regular supervised free-roaming time outside the enclosure.

Temperament & Behavior

Humidity without a tight lid just makes an easier escape into a damp room. Calcium belongs in the diet the whole life, not as a one-time supplement when the shell already looks rough. A snail that seals itself to the glass for a long stretch may be resting, or it may be dry. Check the lid and the moisture before you decide the animal is fine because the card calls the temperament docile. Docile does not mean the animal stays put or that the shell maintains itself.

Read the law that applies where you are before you read the beginner rating again. In the United States the controlling facts are the USDA permit and the ban on import, interstate movement, and possession without it. Elsewhere, the controlling facts are calcium, humidity, a secure lid, fast reproduction, and eggs that stay out of the wild. The 6-8 inch size and the 5-10 year lifespan are the animal. They are not permission.

Social needs vary considerably among small animals, and Giant African Land Snail specifically have particular requirements that owners should understand before acquisition. Even naturally social Giant African Land Snail require careful introduction protocols when adding new cage mates—sudden introductions in shared territory can trigger fight-or-flight responses that establish lasting negative associations.

A healthy, well-adjusted Giant African Land Snail will display curiosity about new enrichment items, engage in species-typical play behaviors, and maintain consistent activity patterns within their normal circadian rhythm. Keepers who establish a baseline understanding of their individual Giant African Land Snail's normal behavior patterns are better positioned to identify and address problems early, when intervention is most effective and least costly.

  • A buyer who might release one when it gets large
  • A home that cannot lock the tank. They are escape artists and they live for years

A giant African land snail is often illegal to own, import, or move across state lines in the United States because it is an agricultural pest. Check the law before you buy a shell in a shop. If you are somewhere they are legal, they need a humid tank, cuttlebone for calcium, and a plant diet. A cracked shell is a calcium and humidity failure. This is not a harmless classroom novelty.
