Mongolian Gerbil Overview

The Mongolian Gerbil is a very small (2-4 oz) gerbil that makes an excellent pet for beginners and families. Their high activity level and social - same-sex pairs social nature make them engaging and entertaining companions.

Mongolian Gerbils thrive with companionship and are best kept with compatible cage mates. Their diet of gerbil food, hay, vegetables is specifically formulated for their nutritional needs.

The Mongolian gerbil is Meriones unguiculatus, a small desert rodent, not a dwarf hamster and not a mouse. Size on the card is very small, 2-4 oz. . Activity is high. . The beginner label fits a pair that can dig, take a sand bath, and stay together. It does not fit a single gerbil in a shallow layer of bedding with a hamster wheel as the only plan. The 3-5 year lifespan is long enough to regret a cage that cannot hold a tunnel.

They are social, and they do best as a same-sex pair introduced young. The card's social line says same-sex pairs for that reason. Two males, or two females, that grew up together usually hold. Adult strangers dropped into one tank often fight, and a gerbil that has just lost its partner is a poor candidate for a surprise roommate. Do not split a bonded pair because one person wanted a single pet, and do not add a new gerbil to a grieving one on the day of the death. A lone gerbil can live, and many of them do worse than the pair the card is built around.

A Mongolian gerbil is a desert rodent that needs a deep sand bath and a deep cage for tunnels, and it should live in a same-sex pair that was raised together. They are illegal to keep as pets in California. Scent glands on the belly are normal. A wet tail, a head tilt, or a seizure is not. They chew plastic. Give them wood, cardboard, and a wheel with a solid surface.

Watching a pair of Mongolian Gerbils industriously excavate a new tunnel system — bedding flying, noses twitching — is the kind of behavior that reminds you why you chose this species in the first place. Their social intelligence runs deeper than most people expect: bonded pairs develop coordinated routines, groom each other methodically after any disruption, and will call out and search for a cagemate after even brief separations. Keepers who invest in a deep substrate layer and take time to observe rather than constantly handle find that gerbils become genuinely comfortable with human presence on their own terms.

Training Advice

They dig, and the space line is 10+ gal with deep bedding. A 10-gallon tank is the bottom of that range, and only if the bedding is actually deep. An inch of shavings in a 10+ gal tank wastes the gallons. Gerbils tunnel, and the substrate should let them disappear. A wire hamster cage with a shallow pan is the wrong shape even when the floor area looks similar. The lid has to be secure. They chew plastic and they kick bedding, and a loose screen is how a 2-4 oz animal leaves the tank.

Grooming Essentials

Give them a sand bath. A dish of bathing sand, not water, is how a gerbil keeps the coat from going oily. They should not be bathed in water. A damp gerbil in a deep, warm burrow chills, and the coat picks up bedding while it dries. Change the sand when it clumps. The diet is gerbil food, hay, and vegetables. Hay gives them something to chew besides the cage furnishings. Seeds are a side, not the whole ration. A gerbil that is only eating sunflower seeds gets fat under a coat that still looks fine at 2-4 oz.

Temperament & Personality

Some lines are seizure-prone. A gerbil that suddenly stiffens, rolls, or seems absent may be having a seizure, and swinging, shaking, or startling the animal is the wrong response. Note it, keep the room quiet, and talk to a veterinarian if the episodes repeat or the gerbil does not come back to normal. Do not swing a gerbil by the tail, and do not pick one up by the tail. The skin on a gerbil tail can slip off if it is pulled. That injury is ugly, it is painful, and it often ends with the damaged tail removed. Scoop the body instead. High activity and a 3-5 year life still depend on a same-sex pair, deep bedding in a 10+ gal tank, a sand bath, and a tail you never use as a handle.

Cost of Ownership

Gerbils are among the more affordable small pets once the initial setup is behind you, with low food costs and modest supply needs. The main consideration is that they thrive in pairs, so you're budgeting for two animals from the start, including any vet visits — which can be surprisingly expensive at exotic practices even for tiny patients. Knowing that going in makes the overall cost picture much easier to manage.
