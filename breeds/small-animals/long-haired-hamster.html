Overview

The long-haired hamster is the long-haired Syrian, the animal sold as a teddy bear hamster. The species is Mesocricetus auratus. It is not a dwarf hamster, and it is not a teddy guinea pig. Size on the card is 5-7 inches. . Origin is Syria. Temperament is gentle and friendly, and difficulty is beginner. Those words describe a single Syrian that is awake at night. They do not describe a pair of dwarfs, and they do not describe a cavy with a plush coat.

A full-grown long-haired Syrian at 5-7 inches is a large hamster next to a dwarf species that stays near half that length. The cage has to match the Syrian, not the hamster aisle in general. Beginner care still means a large cage, not a colorful tube set sold as a starter kit. Tubes pinch a 5-7 inch body, hold urine, and are miserable to clean. The 2-3 year lifespan is short enough that a bad cage is a large share of the animal's life, not a temporary crate.

Syrians are solitary. Adult long-haired hamsters fight if they are housed together. A pair that looked fine as juveniles will injure each other once they mature, and the long coat hides bites until the fur is stuck together. Do not add a second Syrian, a dwarf hamster, or a mouse as company because the first one looks lonely in the afternoon. It is asleep. Company of another adult is the thing that gets a Syrian hurt. One hamster to an enclosure is the housing plan.

The cage should be large, and the bedding should be deep. Syrians dig and tunnel. A sprinkle of shavings over a solid pan is not bedding. Give a depth they can disappear under, and a hide that stays put when they burrow against it. A wheel is mandatory for an animal this active at night, and it has to be a wheel without bars that catch hair. Wire and rungs tangle a long coat, pull at the skin, and can break a toe. A solid running surface large enough for a 5-7 inch body is the one that fits this coat.

They are nocturnal. The gentle, friendly temperament shows up in the evening, not at noon when someone wakes the hamster to prove it likes people. A long-haired Syrian that is pulled out of a nest every afternoon bites, and then gets labeled mean. Handle in the waking hours, with the lights low enough that you are not dazzling an animal that just came out of a burrow. Children who can only visit in the daytime are visiting a sleeping animal. That is a scheduling fact, not a training failure.

Temperament & Behavior

The coat is the only reason this Syrian is sold under a different name. Long hair mats with urine and with bedding if the nest is wet or the wheel is the wrong shape. A light comb of the worst tangles is enough. This is not a breed you clip into a haircut, and it is not a guinea pig teddy. Spots of bare skin from barbering or from a fight are medical, not a haircut you missed. Because adults fight, you cannot solve a mat by adding a cagemate to groom it.

The card's costs are initial setup $100-$500+, animal purchase $10-$500+, annual food and bedding $150-$500, and annual vet care $50-$200. Spend the setup range on the large cage, the deep bedding, and the solid wheel, not on a tube kit. Annual bedding costs are real because deep substrate is thrown out, not shaken. The $50-$200 vet line is a short-life animal's allowance. A Syrian that stops eating, drools, or has wetness at the tail still needs a vet inside that 2-3 year window. Cheek pouches packed with food can look like a tumor. Know the difference before you panic, and still have the clinic picked out.

Some thrive in pairs or small groups, while others may display territorial aggression toward cage mates. Even naturally social require careful introduction protocols when adding new cage mates—sudden introductions in shared territory can trigger fight-or-flight responses that establish lasting negative associations.
