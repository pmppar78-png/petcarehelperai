Tarantula Overview

Tarantula on this page is a generic name. The card does not name one species. Size is 3-10 inches of legspan, lifespan is 5-30 years, and origin is worldwide tropical and subtropical. That spread is the point. A spider at 3 inches and a spider at 10 inches, one that lives near 5 years and one that can live toward 30, are not the same pet. Do not collapse 5-30 years into a single age, and do not invent a species name this card does not give. Temperament is calm and docile, with the card itself adding that this is species-dependent. Difficulty is beginner-intermediate. Both labels depend on which spider is in the enclosure.

New World species have urticating hairs and usually milder venom. Those hairs are a defense. The spider kicks them off the abdomen when it is disturbed, and they irritate skin, eyes, and lungs. A calm New World spider is still carrying that defense. Usually milder venom is not a promise that a bite is nothing. It is a comparison with the other half of this hobby. The hairs are the reason a New World tarantula is a poor animal to pass around, even when the species has a reputation for sitting still.

Old World species lack those urticating hairs. They tend to be faster, and they have medically more significant venom. A bite from an Old World tarantula is a medical problem, not a badge and not a moment for a home remedy or a dose you found online. This page does not name a species and does not give a treatment. It says the keeper has to know which spider they bought before they even think about handling. Speed plus a more serious venom is a bad combination in an animal someone assumed was the calm one on a poster.

Most tarantulas should not be handled. The calm, docile line is species-dependent, and it describes an animal left in its enclosure, not a spider that wants to be on a hand. Choosing to handle one starts with knowing whether you have a New World species with urticating hairs or an Old World species without them. If you do not know, you do not handle it. A display animal that never comes out is a complete life for a tarantula. Nothing in the 5-30 year span requires a handling session to be successful.

They need a secure lid. Tarantulas climb, including species that spend their time on the ground, and they push. A lid that relies on the animal's weight, or a top that slides, is how a spider ends up on the floor. The enclosure also needs the right moisture for that species. Arboreal spiders need height, airflow, and a moisture level that does not turn the tank into a swamp. Fossorial spiders need depth they can dig and a moisture level that suits a burrow, which is not the same setting. One average humidity poured over every tarantula will be too wet for one species and too dry for another. The 3-10 inch legspan changes the floor space as well. Match the box to the spider you have.

Housing and environment design for Tarantula has evolved considerably as our understanding of small animal welfare has improved. The size and complexity of the enclosure or hutch directly correlates with your Tarantula's physical health, behavioral normality, and overall lifespan. Experienced Tarantula keepers consistently advocate for the largest enclosure or hutch that space and budget allow, supplemented with regular supervised free-roaming time outside the enclosure.

Temperament & Behavior

A fall onto a hard floor can rupture the abdomen. That is the practical reason the lid stays on and the spider stays in the enclosure. An abdomen that splits is an injury an invertebrate does not walk off. Handling over a table, a tile floor, or a sink is how that fall happens. Even a short drop is enough. The beginner-intermediate rating assumes the animal is contained. It does not assume the keeper can catch a falling spider safely. If a tarantula gets out, guide it with a cup and a piece of card back into the enclosure. Do not grab it.

Social needs vary considerably among small animals, and Tarantula specifically have particular requirements that owners should understand before acquisition. Some Tarantula thrive in pairs or small groups, while others may display territorial aggression toward cage mates. Age, sex, and the introduction method all influence compatibility. Even naturally social Tarantula require careful introduction protocols when adding new cage mates—sudden introductions in shared territory can trigger fight-or-flight responses that establish lasting negative associations.

Activity patterns and play preferences in Tarantula provide important insights into their overall wellbeing. A healthy, well-adjusted Tarantula will display curiosity about new enrichment items, engage in species-typical play behaviors, and maintain consistent activity patterns within their normal circadian rhythm. Keepers who establish a baseline understanding of their individual Tarantula's normal behavior patterns are better positioned to identify and address problems early, when intervention is most effective and least costly.

Tarantulas are one of the most cost-effective exotic pets available — ongoing expenses are minimal once a proper enclosure is set up, since they eat infrequently and require no specialized lighting or heating in most homes. The main upfront investment is the habitat itself: a well-ventilated terrarium with appropriate substrate, hides, and humidity control. Veterinary care is rarely needed, but finding an exotics vet who sees invertebrates before an emergency arises is still a wise precaution.

Budget more aggressively for the first year. Beyond the obvious — food, vet visits, supplies — there are costs that catch people off guard: replacing items your Tarantula destroys during teething, emergency visits for swallowed objects, and higher food costs during rapid growth phases. After that initial period, expenses settle into a more manageable rhythm.

Owners who maintain a regular preventive care schedule for their Tarantula consistently report lower overall vet costs than those who wait for problems to appear. The math favors prevention every time.

Housing follows the species, not the word tarantula. A terrestrial spider in a tall tank climbs and falls, and a ruptured abdomen is usually fatal. Give substrate and a hide, and skip wire mesh that can trap a foot. Do not handle. A fall kills the animal, and a bite is painful. Fasting and a spider on its back often mean a molt. Disturbing a molt can kill it. Write down the scientific name, because humidity for a desert terrestrial will rot an arboreal species, and the reverse dries the arboreal one out.
