A solid cat that follows you

Burmese are compact, people-obsessed cats of about 8 to 12 pounds, often living 15 to 18 years. The coat is short and shiny, sable in the classic color and several others in modern registries. They talk less than a Siamese and sit more than an Abyssinian. Activity is still high. A Burmese left alone all day is a bad plan; a pair is often happier than one cat watching the door. Children and polite dogs usually work.

They like food. The compact body gets heavy without looking "big," and that weight is part of the diabetes story. Measure meals.

Sugar, potassium, skulls

Diabetes shows up often enough that a middle-aged Burmese drinking more should have a blood glucose, not a new water fountain and a shrug. Hypokalemic polymyopathy causes weakness and a ventroflexed neck; potassium and a gene test are the clinical path, and the episodes can look like the cat is being dramatic. The head defect is a breeding disaster you avoid by asking for the DNA result. Dental disease, cherry eye, and corneal sequestrum are the face problems. A cat that paws at its mouth in distress needs a vet the same day.

A routine year is often about $1,080 to $2,060. Kittens commonly cost $600 to $1,500. Merck describes feline diabetes and hypokalemic polymyopathy. Not eating, or drinking and wasting away, uses the not-eating guide and the checker.

Burmese questions

Why are Burmese linked to diabetes?

The breed is overrepresented for diabetes mellitus, especially as they age and gain weight. Excess thirst, a sticky litter box, and weight loss despite a good appetite are the signs. Keeping them lean is the useful prevention.

What is Burmese hypokalemia?

A genetic tendency to low potassium that causes muscle weakness, sometimes a neck that will not hold the head up. It comes and goes. A DNA test exists in many lines. Sudden weakness is a clinic visit, not a nap.

What is the Burmese head defect?

A lethal craniofacial malformation in kittens that inherit two copies. Reputable breeders DNA-test and do not pair carriers. It is a reason to see results, not a disease you manage in a pet that already looks normal.
