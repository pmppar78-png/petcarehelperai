The barn cat with a standard

American Shorthairs are the pedigree version of the North American working shorthair: 8 to 15 pounds, males heavier than females, a lifespan often 15 to 20 years, and a coat that a weekly brush can handle. The famous look is the silver tabby with black swirls. Temperament is the selling point that is usually true: easygoing, moderately active, not a screamer, good with children and other animals. They play, then they sleep on the paperwork.

Do not pay breed prices for a lookalike without papers, and do not assume a papered cat is medically charmed. The body is solid enough to hide fat. A waist should still exist.

Heart, hips, weight

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, a thickened heart muscle, has shown up in American Shorthair lines. A murmur or a breeder who has never scanned a parent is a reason to ask for an echocardiogram plan, not a reason to panic at every nap. Hip dysplasia occurs in some heavier cats and looks like a reluctance to jump. Teeth need the same care as any shorthair. Routine yearly costs are among the lower pedigree totals, about $950 to $1,850, and kittens are often $600 to $1,200.

VCA explains hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in ordinary language. Skipping meals is the not-eating guide. Open-mouth breathing or a sudden collapse uses the checker on the way to a clinic.

American Shorthair questions

Is an American Shorthair just a regular cat?

A registered American Shorthair is a breed with a standard, often a silver classic tabby, selected from working barn cats. A random short-haired cat from a shelter is a domestic shorthair. Both can be wonderful. They are not the same paperwork.

Are they good for beginners?

Usually yes. Activity is moderate, grooming is simple, and they tend to be patient with children and other pets. Obesity and dental disease are the ways an easy cat becomes an expensive one.

What health issue should buyers ask about?

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy has been found in the breed. Ask whether the parents had heart ultrasounds. Hips can be loose in a heavier cat. Neither problem is as defining as the Persian face, and neither is zero.
