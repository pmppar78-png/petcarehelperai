A small boa that stays small

Rosy boas, Lichanura, are the desert boas of the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. Adults are usually 2 to 4 feet. Twenty-five to 30 years or more is easy to skip when the snake looks simple. Care level is beginner: a dry tank, a thermostat, and mice.

Localities differ in color. A coastal animal and a desert animal are still rosy boas. Keep the cool side in the 70s and the warm side up toward 90°F, inside a 72 to 90°F band. Humidity stays low, about 30 to 40 percent. Aspen or a dry soil mix, two hides, and a water bowl are the furniture. Twenty gallons is a starting size and small for a long adult. Go longer rather than taller.

Mice, and a tight lid

Thawed mice match the body. They are not huge feeders. A snake that refuses through winter may be cooling off. A snake that refuses in summer, or that loses weight, needs a look. They climb just enough to test a loose lid. House one snake per cage.

Captive-bred animals are the ones to buy. Some localities are protected. A stuck shed on the tail tip rots if you ignore it. Clinics that see snakes are listed by ARAV.

Rosy boa questions

How long do rosy boas live?

25 to 30 years or more. That is a long life for a snake this small. Plan on an adult snake, a decades-long project in a small body.

Do rosy boas need high humidity?

No. The range is 30 to 40 percent. A wet tank causes bad sheds and blistered bellies. A humid hide during shed is enough extra moisture.

Are rosy boas handleable?

They are slow and usually calm. Support the body. A hungry rosy boa will still mistake a warm hand for food, so wash before you pick one up.

Slow desert heat

Rosy boas are small boas, about 2 to 4 feet, with a lifespan of 25 to 30 years or more. That is a long life for a snake this small. Care is beginner. Temperament is docile and slow-moving. The enclosure is 20 gallons or more for an adult, with a secure lid, a warm hide, a cool hide, and a water bowl that does not soak the whole cage. They do not need height. They need a dry floor and a place to coil.

Temperatures of 72 to 90°F should be a gradient on a thermostat. The warm side can approach the top of the band. The cool side should sit near the low 70s so the snake can leave the heat. A fluorescent over Rosy Boa can mark day and night while the thermostat holds 72-90°F and humidity 30 to 40 percent. A harsh desert basking rock beyond the stated range is a burn risk for a slow boa that will sit still. Humidity of 30 to 40 percent is low. Keep substrate dry. Offer a moist hide when the eyes go blue, then let it dry out again.

Mice and a long calendar

Mice are the diet. Thawed prey, sized to the snake, on a modest schedule. A slow boa can still miss and bite a hand that smells like food. Use tongs. One snake per cage. The adult size stays small, and the years do not. Plan on an adult snake and a multi-decade thermostat, with a vet fund for the whole span.

In a 30 to 40 percent cage, skipped moist hides leave eye caps and tail rings. Check every shed. A belly that stays wet and red means the substrate was damp outside of shed week. Respiratory noise is uncommon in a correctly dry rosy boa and still needs a reptile veterinarian if it appears. Twenty-five to thirty years of a docile 2 to 4 foot boa is a small cage you keep honest: 72 to 90°F, dry, and locked.
