A loud snake from sandy woods

Pine snakes, Pituophis melanoleucus, are the big, often pale snakes of sandy pine country in the eastern and southern United States. Adults commonly run 4 to 8 feet. Lifespan is 15 to 20 years, shorter on paper than a bull snake and still a long time. Care is intermediate. They hiss like their gopher-snake cousins and they dig.

The cage listed is 4 by 2 by 2 feet. A large adult uses more. Temperatures of 75 to 88°F and humidity of 40 to 50 percent fit. Deep substrate lets them burrow the way the species actually lives. A water bowl, two hides, and a locked lid finish the basic box. Thawed mice and rats are the diet. .

Subspecies with legal weight

Black pine snakes, northern pine snakes, and Louisiana pine snakes are conservation priorities. The pet to buy is a captive-bred animal with a clear subspecies, a known animal from a breeder, with the roadside box left closed. Babies bluff. Adults usually calm down if you are steady and you do not feed them by hand.

Respiratory infections show up as open-mouth breathing or bubbles at the nose. That needs a reptile vet, found through ARAV.

Pine snake questions

Are pine snakes the same as bull snakes?

They are close relatives in Pituophis. Bull snakes are P. catenifer sayi. Pine snakes are P. melanoleucus and its subspecies. The hiss is similar. The range and the legal status are not.

Do pine snakes need damp cages?

No. Hold 40 to 50 percent humidity. They burrow in sandy soil. Soak the hide during shed, and keep the rest dry.

Why buy captive-bred only?

Northern, black, and Louisiana pine snakes have serious conservation problems. A wild adult in the trade is the wrong animal.

Room to hiss and then settle

Pine snakes are large colubrids, about 4 to 8 feet, living roughly 15 to 20 years. Care is intermediate. Temperament is hissy and generally calm once the snake knows the routine. The enclosure is about 4 by 2 by 2 feet, longer for the big adults. They are strong and will push a loose lid. The pet to buy is a captive-bred animal with a clear subspecies. Babies bluff. Adults usually calm down if you are steady and you feed with tools.

Temperatures of 75 to 88°F should be a gradient on a thermostat. Warm hide toward 88°F, cool end nearer 75°F. Humidity of 40 to 50 percent is on the dry side, with a water bowl and a moist hide when the eyes are blue. A fluorescent over Pine Snake can mark day and night while the thermostat holds 75-88°F and humidity 40 to 50 percent. A desert basking chart with a dangerous surface temperature does not fit. This is a heavy terrestrial snake using hides.

Rats and a dry-climate shed

Rats and mice, thawed and sized to the snake, are the diet. They can be enthusiastic. Use tongs and a hook at the door so the hiss and the strike stay on the prey. . Give them time to digest before handling. A loud hiss is the species. Pain and a rattle are different problems. Calm handling reduces the bluff. It may never go silent.

One-piece skin, clear eyes, and an intact tail tip are the Pine Snake shed result to expect at 75-88°F at an adult size around 4 to 8 feet. At 40 to 50 percent, a pine snake that has no moist hide during blue eyes often keeps eye caps and tail rings. Inspect every shed. Respiratory noise after a cold, damp episode needs a reptile veterinarian. Fifteen to twenty years of a 4 to 8 foot hissing snake is a 4 by 2 by 2 foot commitment with a thermostat, and a subspecies name on the receipt.
