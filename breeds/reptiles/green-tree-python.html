The green python on a perch

Green tree pythons are Morelia viridis. Adults are about 5 to 7 feet, and the enclosure listed here is 2x2x3 ft (arboreal) at 72-88°F. Care is advanced. They are a display animal, and they can bite. Both are the daily reality. The resting coil looks like an emerald tree boa and the animal is a python from a different ocean.

Locality matters. Aru, Sorong, Biak, and Cyclops animals differ in adult color, neonate color, and sometimes attitude. Babies of many lines are yellow or brick red and turn green. The cage is arboreal: perches, a vertical temperature gradient from the low 70s at the cool end toward the upper 80s, and humidity around 50 to 70 percent as listed, higher while shedding. The 2 by 2 by 3 foot minimum is a tight adult cage. Wider perches and more length help.

Rodents, tongs, and almost no handling

They eat mice and small rats. A strike from the perch is fast. Feed with tongs and give the snake the rest of the week in peace. Captive-bred locality animals are the ethical and the practical buy. Wild-caught imports do poorly. A python that never uses the perch, or that keeps a crust of shed on the tail, needs a setup change and maybe a vet.

Line up a clinic through ARAV before the bite or the respiratory infection, not after.

Green tree python questions

Are green tree pythons the same as emerald tree boas?

No. The posture looks similar. Green tree pythons are Morelia from New Guinea and nearby islands. Emerald tree boas are Corallus from South America. Heat, humidity, and the laws around them are not identical.

Why is my baby yellow or red?

Many localities hatch yellow or red and turn green. Biak, Aru, Sorong, and other lines do this on different schedules. A failed color change can also be illness, so watch weight and sheds.

Can I hold a green tree python?

They are a display animal that can bite. Holding is a rare chore, not the hobby. The teeth are long.

A perch gradient from 72 to 88

Green tree pythons are medium arboreal snakes, about 5 to 7 feet, with a 15 to 20 year lifespan. Care is advanced. Temperament is a display animal that can bite. The listed enclosure is 2 by 2 by 3 feet and arboreal. Many keepers give more length so an adult can stretch along a perch. Horizontal branches of the right thickness, fixed so they cannot fall, are the furniture. A water bowl under the perch will be fouled and needs a cleaning routine.

Let the snake choose by moving higher or lower and toward a warmer end. Every heat source goes on a thermostat. Guard lamps. A coiled python will burn its belly on an exposed bulb. A desert basking chart does not fit this snake. Humidity of 50 to 70 percent should come with ventilation. Stale wet air and dry air are both failures. Read a hygrometer at perch height.

Rodents and the mouth

Mice and rats, thawed and offered with long tongs at the perch, are the diet. Heat pits make a warm hand look like food. Handle for cage maintenance and veterinary trips, with a plan, and leave the display animal on the branch the rest of the week. Babies may be yellow or red and turn green. That shift is normal. Buy captive-bred.

Labored breathing in a Green Tree Python belongs with a reptile veterinarian, along with the hygrometer reading beside 72-88°F across 15 to 20 years. Open-mouth breathing, mucus, or a snake that refuses the perch and sits on the floor needs a reptile veterinarian who knows arboreal pythons. A patchy shed means the 50 to 70 percent humidity failed during the cycle. Advanced care is the perch, the guarded heat, and the decision to watch a 5 to 7 foot snake for 15 to 20 years.
