A pretty neck ring and a hard diet

Ring-neck snakes, Diadophis punctatus, are the small dark snakes with an orange or yellow ring and a bright belly. They commonly reach 6 to 10 years, care is intermediate, and they stay secretive. People pocket them on hikes. Those snakes are the ones that die. This is a poor pet, and in many states a wild one is illegal to take.

If a legal captive-bred animal exists in front of you, the setup is a cool, damp, cluttered cage. Temperatures of 65 to 75°F are cooler than a corn-snake rack. Humidity around 50 to 60 percent, leaf litter, and hiding places match the way they live. Ten gallons is only a minimum. The diet is the wall: slugs, earthworms, and small amphibians. A mouse-pinkie habit is not this species. Feeding wild salamanders is a conservation problem and a parasite delivery system.

Leave wild snakes in the leaves

A ring-neck under a log is already at home. Moving it into a jar so a child can see the belly color is how these pages of advice get ignored. They musk, they hide, and they starve when the food is wrong. Rear fangs and mild saliva are real and still not the reason to keep one.

A captive that refuses food for a long stretch is the case described on the not-eating page. Better to have skipped the purchase. A vet search starts at ARAV.

Ring-neck snake questions

Are ring-neck snakes a good first snake?

No. Care is intermediate, they stay secretive, and the diet is slugs, worms, and salamanders. Most animals in the trade were wild-caught and fade in a tank.

Do they show themselves?

Rarely. A correct setup is dark and cluttered. If you want a snake you can watch, pick a different species.

Is the bite dangerous?

They are small and rear-fanged. A bite is usually a local irritation for people. The saliva can irritate. The bigger problem is the snake dying in a jar while the keeper stays unharmed.

A hard diet and a small shed

The diet is slugs, worms, and salamanders. That menu is the reason care is intermediate. Earthworms are the item most keepers can actually supply. Dusting and gut-loading matter less than offering prey the snake will recognize, and a salamander-based diet is a wildlife and parasite problem. Many ring-necks never thrive on a simplified menu. If you keep one, weigh it. A secretive snake can starve under the leaves while the cage looks occupied.

Look over the Ring-Neck Snake eye caps after a shed. The cage figures to blame or credit are 65-75°F and humidity 50 to 60 percent at an adult size around 10 to 15 inches. A complete tiny shed means the 50 to 60 percent humidity and the moist pocket worked. A dried, stuck shed on a snake this small is an emergency relative to its size. Respiratory decline and a refusal to eat worms also need a reptile veterinarian who will see a miniature colubrid. Six to ten years at 65 to 75°F is only a fair outcome when the diet is solved. For most people, the right choice is to leave the ring-neck outside.
