The small monitor with a large build

Ackies, Varanus acanthurus, are Australian ridge-tailed monitors about 24 to 28 inches long, tail included. Lifespan runs about 15 to 20 years for this Medium (24-28 in) animal at 80-150°F (gradient) in a 4x2x2 ft enclosure. They are the monitor most often suggested to a keeper who wants a lizard that uses its brain and still fits in a house. The cage does not shrink with the reputation. Adults need about 4 by 2 by 2 feet, a foot or more of substrate they can burrow in, stacked cork, and a basking rock hot enough that the surface reads well over 120°F while a burrow stays humid and cool. Air temperature in the 80s on the warm side is not the same number as that rock.

Humidity in the air can sit around 40 to 60 percent if the burrows are damp. A bone-dry cage and a water bowl in the corner is how they dehydrate. UVB over the bask is worth installing.

Insects, and a lot of them

Diet is insects and other small prey: roaches, crickets, and the occasional egg. Rodents as a habit make them fat. Dust calcium. They are inquisitive and will learn the feeding tongs. Toes and tail tips shed stuck if the burrow dried out. A monitor that stops eating, swells at the joints, or breathes with its neck extended needs a vet who has seen varanids, a clinic that already treats varanids, because a general practice will be guessing at a monitor.

Ackie monitor questions

Are ackie monitors beginner lizards?

Care is intermediate. They are the small monitor people recommend first, and the setup is still a 4-foot cage, deep dirt, and a basking surface that can pass 130°F. That build is the real ackie setup, with heat and soil a monitor needs from the first week.

How hot should the basking spot be?

Surface temperature where they bask often needs to reach well above 120°F, sometimes near 140 to 150°F on the rock, with a much cooler retreat. Measure the surface, not the air in the middle of the room. A thermostat is mandatory.

Do they like to be handled?

Many become confident and will climb a familiar person. That takes time and a feeding routine. A new ackie that is chased around the cage learns to sprint, not to sit.

Heat you measure on the rock

An adult ackie is a 24 to 28 inch lizard with a 15 to 20 year life, and the quick facts enclosure of 4 by 2 by 2 feet is the floor plan to build before the animal arrives. Active, inquisitive monitors pace the glass if the only shelter is a plastic cave on a paper towel. Give a packed soil mix several inches deep, cork flats, and a couple of stones that will not tip when the lizard digs under them. The warm burrow and the cool burrow should both be tight enough that the body touches a wall.

The temperature line on this page is 80 to 150°F as a gradient, which is easy to misread as air temperature. Air on the cool side can sit near 80°F. The number that matters at the hot end is the surface the belly touches. Check that slate or paver with an infrared thermometer while the lamp has been on for an hour. Plug the basking lamp into a thermostat and set the probe on the stone, or the surface will sail past a safe basking zone on a warm afternoon. Night can fall back toward the bottom of the range. Leave a cooler retreat so the lizard can get off the heat.

Ackies bask. Mount a UVB tube over the hot end at the distance the bulb maker lists for a high-output basking lizard, and replace it when the maker says the UVB has faded, even if the light still looks bright. A glass lid between the tube and the animal blocks the useful wavelengths. Screen is fine. Humidity of 40 to 60 percent in the air, with the lower burrow kept slightly damp, lets sheds peel off toes and the tail tip. A bone-dry tunnel is how retained shed rings a toe.

Food, weight, and soft bone

The diet is insects and small prey: dubia roaches, crickets, and the occasional egg. Dust most insect meals with calcium. A habit of fatty rodents piles weight onto a lizard that should still be able to sprint into a burrow. Offer food in the evening or morning when the animal is out, and skip the hand that smells like a roach if the strike is wild. An inquisitive ackie learns the tongs. That habit is useful. It is still a monitor with a bite.

Metabolic bone disease is the watch-item for this basking lizard. A rubbery jaw, swollen limbs, or trembling when the animal climbs out to bask means UVB or calcium has been missing. Weigh the monitor every few weeks. An animal that stops clearing insects while the temperatures still read correctly needs a reptile veterinarian while the temperatures still look correct. Keep the cage at the 4-foot size even for a young pair of animals you might later separate. Two adults in a cramped box fight where you cannot see them, under the dirt.
