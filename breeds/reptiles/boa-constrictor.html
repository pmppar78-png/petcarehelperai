The adult is a length of muscle

Boa constrictor is one species with many localities. Common pets often land between 5 and 8 feet and a heavy body. Some Central American animals stay smaller. True red-tail types and a few localities pass 10 feet. Buy the locality you can house at the large end, in an enclosure at least 6 feet long for a big adult, with a warm side in the mid to high 80s and a hot spot near 90°F. Humidity around 60 to 70 percent. A tub that was fine at 3 feet is not fine at 7.

They are often calm if worked with on purpose. A hungry adult is still a constrictor. Feed in the enclosure or with a plan, thaw rodents fully, and do not drape a large boa around a neck. Two people for the big ones. House them alone.

Inclusion disease and a stuck shed

Boid inclusion body disease is the serious viral concern in boas: neurological signs, staring, regurgitation, wasting. There is no casual cure. Quarantine new snakes and do not mix collections casually. Respiratory infections show up as open-mouth breathing and mucus when the cage is cold and wet. Incomplete sheds mean the humidity crashed. Regurgitation is an emergency pattern, a medical event that needs a clinic even when the last mouse looked fine.

Food for an adult is a line item, often rats. ARAV lists snake vets. Repeated refusal or a vomit is the not-eating page and the checker.

Boa constrictor questions

How big will a boa get?

Common boa constrictors in the pet trade often finish around 5 to 8 feet. Some localities and true red-tails get larger, past 10 feet. Ask which animal you are buying. The 5 to 10 foot range is that spread, a band that includes snakes well past a cute juvenile size.

Are boa constrictors good for beginners?

Care is intermediate. A 6-foot muscled snake that can bite and constrict is a two-person handling problem. A corn snake is the usual first snake. A boa is the second, after the adult size is already in the house.

Are they legal everywhere?

No. Some states and cities restrict large boas. The federal injurious-wildlife list targets certain giant pythons and anacondas, and local rules still catch boas. Check before you pay.

A long cage and a warm hide

Boa constrictor on this page covers animals that often finish around 5 to 8 feet, with some localities and true red-tails past 10 feet. The quick facts size is 5 to 10 feet, lifespan is 20 to 30 years or more, and care is intermediate. Ask which animal you are buying before you trust a cage. The adult minimum listed here is 6 by 2 by 2 feet, and a larger locality needs a longer box. Give a warm hide, a cool hide, a heavy water bowl, and branches only if they are bolted down. A generally docile boa is still a length of muscle.

Temperature range is 78 to 90°F. Run the warm side on a thermostat and read the temperature inside the warm hide, where the snake actually lies. The cool side should sit at the bottom of that band. Overhead heat has to be guarded. A Boa Constrictor photoperiod can be a fluorescent tube. Basking heat, if any, has to stay inside 78-90°F at an adult size around 5 to 8 feet. Skip a desert basking chart. This is a snake that thermoregulates between hides, and a 120°F rock is a burn waiting for a heavy body.

Humidity of 60 to 70 percent supports a complete shed. Dry air leaves rings on the tail and retained eye caps. Wet, cold air is the other failure, and it shows up as respiratory noise. Keep the substrate damp in a humid hide during the blue-eye phase, and keep the rest of the cage from staying soaked. Ventilation matters in a 6-foot enclosure. Stale damp air is a husbandry problem even when the thermometer looks fine.

Prey size and the shed

The diet is carnivore: rodents, and rabbits for large adults. Prey should leave a modest bulge and then be digested before the next meal. Feed with tongs or in a routine that keeps hands out of the strike path. Two people should be available to handle a large adult. House boas singly unless you have a specific, experienced reason and separate feeding. Quarantine new animals because inclusion body disease is a collection-level problem with neurological signs, staring, regurgitation, and wasting.

The everyday health watch-item is the shed, with respiratory signs close behind if the cage is cool and wet. An incomplete shed means humidity crashed. A snake that breathes with its mouth open or shows mucus needs a reptile veterinarian promptly. Regurgitation is an emergency pattern: cool the handling, check temperatures, and call the clinic. A decades-long boa in a cage that cannot hold 78 to 90°F on a thermostat will fail the shed and the appetite long before old age.
