Bearded Dragon Overview

The Bearded Dragon, affectionately known as "beardie," is one of the most beloved reptile pets worldwide. Native to the arid regions of Australia, these charismatic lizards are known for their docile temperament, interactive personalities, and the distinctive "beard" of spiny scales under their chin that they can puff out when threatened or displaying.

Bearded dragons are diurnal (active during the day), making them perfect for keepers who want to observe their pet's activities. They're known for their dog-like personalities, often recognizing their owners and displaying behaviors like arm waving and head bobbing.

Bearded dragons are the golden retrievers of the reptile world. They actually seem to enjoy being handled, will sit on your lap while you watch TV, and develop recognizable routines around feeding and basking time. Many beardie owners swear their dragon knows its name, and while the science on that is debatable, there is no question that beardies learn to associate their owner with food, warmth, and positive experiences.

The care requirements are more involved than many pet stores let on. Bearded dragons need strong UVB lighting (not just any light bulb), a basking spot over 100 degrees Fahrenheit, and a diet that shifts from mostly insects as juveniles to mostly vegetables as adults. Getting the lighting wrong leads to metabolic bone disease, which is painful, disfiguring, and the single most common preventable health problem in captive beardies. A proper T5 HO UVB tube light is not optional -- it is the most important piece of equipment in the enclosure.

What surprises most new beardie owners is how much space an adult needs. A baby in a 40-gallon tank looks fine, but an 18-to-24-inch adult dragon in that same tank is cramped. Plan for a 4x2x2-foot enclosure from the start, or budget for the upgrade later. Beardies that have room to move, climb, and thermoregulate across a proper gradient are visibly healthier and more active than those kept in undersized setups.

Natural Habitat & Origin

Bearded dragons are native to the deserts, scrublands, and woodlands of central Australia: Your exotic veterinarian and experienced Bearded Dragon owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

  • Semi-arid climate: Hot days, cooler nights, low humidity
  • Open terrain: Rocky outcrops, fallen timber, and sparse vegetation
  • Basking behavior: Spends hours basking on rocks and branches
  • Diurnal activity: Active during daylight, sleeps at night
  • Note: Export from Australia is prohibited; all pet beardies are captive-bred

Enclosure Setup & Requirements

Bearded dragons require spacious enclosures with proper temperature gradients.

Tank Size

  • Juveniles (0-6 months): 40-gallon minimum
  • Sub-adults (6-12 months): 75-gallon minimum
  • Adults: 120-gallon / 4x2x2 feet minimum
  • Ideal: Larger is always better - 6x2x2 feet provides excellent space

Substrate Options

  • Recommended: Reptile carpet, slate tile, paper towels, excavator clay
  • Bioactive: Organic topsoil/sand mix (for adults with proper husbandry)
  • Avoid for juveniles: Loose substrates due to impaction risk

Essential Furnishings

  • Basking platform/branch under heat lamp
  • Hide on cool side
  • Climbing branches and rocks
  • Shallow water dish (many beardies won't drink from dishes)
  • Food dish for salads

Temperature & Lighting

Proper temperature and UVB lighting are critical for bearded dragon health: Understanding how this applies specifically to Bearded Dragon helps you avoid common pitfalls.

Temperature Requirements

ZoneTemperature
Basking Spot100-110°F (38-43°C)

Cool Side75-85°F (24-29°C)
Nighttime65-75°F (18-24°C)

UVB Lighting (ESSENTIAL)

Bearded dragons REQUIRE strong UVB lighting for proper calcium metabolism and overall health.

  • Tube lights: T5 HO 10.0 or 12% UVB, spanning 2/3 of enclosure length
  • Distance: 10-12 inches from basking spot (without screen) or 6-8 inches (with screen)
  • Duration: 12-14 hours per day
  • Replace: Every 6-12 months (UVB output diminishes)

UVB is Non-Negotiable

Without adequate UVB, bearded dragons will develop metabolic bone disease (MBD), a painful and often fatal condition. Do not skip or skimp on proper UVB lighting.

Humidity & Water

Bearded dragons are desert animals requiring low humidity: Owners who engage with Bearded Dragon-specific guidance, rather than generic pet advice, tend to spot problems sooner.

  • Ambient humidity: 30-40%
  • Hydration: Many beardies prefer drinking from movement (dripping water, baths)
  • Baths: Weekly lukewarm baths help with hydration and shedding
  • Misting: Not generally recommended, can raise humidity too high

Diet & Feeding

Bearded dragons are omnivores with changing dietary needs as they age.

Diet Ratio by Age

AgeInsectsVegetables
Juvenile (0-6 months)70-80%20-30%
Sub-adult (6-12 months)50%50%
Adult (12+ months)20-30%70-80%

Appropriate Feeder Insects

  • Staples: Dubia roaches, crickets, black soldier fly larvae (nutrigrubs)
  • Occasional: Superworms, hornworms, silkworms
  • Treats only: Waxworms, mealworms (hard to digest)
  • Size rule: No larger than the space between their eyes

Vegetables & Greens

  • Daily staples: Collard greens, mustard greens, turnip greens, butternut squash
  • Occasional: Bell peppers, carrots, green beans, snap peas
  • Treats: Fruits (berries, mango) - sparingly due to sugar content
  • Avoid: Lettuce (low nutrition), spinach (binds calcium), avocado (toxic)

Supplementation

  • Juveniles: Calcium with D3 at every feeding, multivitamin 2x weekly
  • Adults: Calcium with D3 3-4x weekly, multivitamin 1x weekly
  • With proper UVB: Can alternate calcium with and without D3

Feeding a bearded dragon changes dramatically as it grows. Juveniles are little insect-demolishing machines -- they need protein to fuel rapid growth and will eat 30 to 50 small crickets or roaches per day. By adulthood, the ratio flips: 70 to 80 percent of the diet should be leafy greens and vegetables, with insects as a smaller supplement. Many new beardie owners struggle with this transition because their dragon has spent months enthusiastically chasing bugs and now needs to learn to eat salad. Persistence is key -- keep offering the greens daily even if they go untouched at first.

Calcium supplementation is non-negotiable. Dust feeder insects with calcium powder at every feeding for juveniles and three to four times per week for adults. Without adequate calcium and proper UVB to metabolize it, bearded dragons develop metabolic bone disease -- soft, rubbery bones that bend and break under normal use. Once MBD progresses, the damage is often permanent. This is not a theoretical risk; it is the most common serious health problem vets see in captive beardies, and it is almost entirely preventable with proper lighting and supplementation.

Health Issues & Common Problems

With proper care, bearded dragons are robust, but watch for these conditions: Your exotic veterinarian and experienced Bearded Dragon owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD)

The most common and serious condition, caused by inadequate UVB and/or calcium. Signs include soft jaw, kinked spine/tail, tremors, lethargy, and difficulty walking.

Impaction

Intestinal blockage from substrate ingestion or improper feeding. Signs: bloating, lethargy, lack of bowel movements, dragging back legs.

Respiratory Infections

From improper temperatures or humidity. Signs: wheezing, mucus around nose/mouth, gaping, lethargy.

Parasites

Internal parasites common in captive beardies. Annual fecal exams recommended. Signs: weight loss despite eating, runny stool.

Yellow Fungus Disease

Serious fungal infection requiring immediate veterinary care. Signs: yellow/brown patches on skin that spread.

Brumation

Natural dormancy period (not illness). Beardies may sleep more, eat less during winter months. Normal if dragon maintains weight and is otherwise healthy.

Metabolic bone disease (MBD) is the condition that every beardie owner needs to understand. It happens when the dragon does not get enough calcium, enough UVB light, or both. Early signs include trembling limbs, difficulty climbing, and a jaw that feels soft when the dragon opens its mouth. Advanced MBD causes permanent skeletal deformities -- kinked tails, bowed legs, a rubbery jaw that cannot close properly. The good news is that MBD is almost entirely preventable with proper UVB lighting and calcium supplementation. If you do nothing else right, get the lighting right.

Internal parasites are common in bearded dragons and often go undetected until they cause weight loss or chronic loose stools. A fecal exam by a reptile vet should be part of your initial checkup and repeated annually. Pinworms, coccidia, and other parasites are treatable with medication, but they can become a serious problem in immunocompromised or stressed dragons. New beardies from pet stores are especially likely to carry parasites, so get that first vet visit done within the first couple of weeks.

Brumation is not a health problem, but it scares new owners every winter. Bearded dragons naturally slow down, eat less, and sleep more during the shorter days of fall and winter. This is normal. A healthy, well-fed dragon that starts sleeping more in November is almost certainly brumating, not sick. Let it happen -- reduce feeding, keep water available, and do not force the dragon to stay active. It will bounce back in spring with a renewed appetite and energy level. The key is making sure the dragon was healthy going into brumation; a sick dragon that stops eating is a different situation entirely.

Handling & Temperament

Bearded dragons are known for being one of the most handleable reptiles: Your exotic veterinarian and experienced Bearded Dragon owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

  • Acclimation: Allow 1-2 weeks to settle before regular handling
  • Approach: From the side, not above (predators come from above)
  • Support: Always support all four legs and the body
  • Duration: Can handle for extended periods once acclimated
  • Body language: Black beard and puffing = stressed; arm waving = submission/acknowledgment
  • Temperature: Provide warmth during handling, return when they get cool

Strong Bearded Dragon care plans prioritize enclosure conditions, stress reduction, and scheduled health observation instead of generic mammal care routines.

Bearded Dragon thrives when thermal gradient, humidity control, and enclosure hygiene are managed as a system, not as isolated checklist items.

Breeding Information

Breeding bearded dragons requires preparation and commitment.

  • Sexual maturity: 18-24 months, though they can breed younger (not recommended)
  • Sexing: Males have larger heads, hemipenal bulges, and femoral pores
  • Brumation: Cooling period helps trigger breeding behavior
  • Clutch size: 15-30 eggs per clutch, multiple clutches per season
  • Egg laying: Provide lay box with moist substrate
  • Incubation: 55-75 days at 82-86°F

Bearded Dragons Are Great For:

  • Those wanting an interactive, personable reptile
  • Families with children (supervised handling)
  • People who enjoy daytime activity from their pets
  • Keepers who want a handleable species
  • Those prepared for long-term commitment (10-15 years)

Bearded Dragons May Not Be Ideal For:

  • Limited space (adults need 4+ foot enclosures)
  • Tight budgets (proper lighting is expensive)
  • Those unwilling to feed live insects
  • People who travel frequently
  • Anyone not prepared for daily salad prep

Before committing to a Bearded Dragon, do the math on ongoing costs — not just the enclosure and the animal, but electricity for heating and lighting, replacement bulbs, food, substrate, and veterinary care from a specialist. These costs are real and they do not go away. If your budget can handle them comfortably, you are in a good position. If they feel tight, it is better to wait.

For those who are prepared, a Bearded Dragon offers a genuinely unique pet-keeping experience that most people never get to have. There is something deeply satisfying about creating a thriving habitat and watching a well-cared-for animal flourish in it.
