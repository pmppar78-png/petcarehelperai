The striped snake is a full pet with a real lifespan

Garter snakes, Thamnophis, are the striped snakes of North American ditches and gardens. Common captives are often eastern or checkered garters. They can live 10 to 15 years, and care is beginner. They are active, they musk, and the diet is the part people get wrong.

A garter does well around 72 to 85°F with humidity near 50 to 60 percent. A 20-gallon tank is only a minimum and small for a busy adult. Give floor space, a water bowl big enough to soak in, and a tight lid. They are slender escape artists. Earthworms, nightcrawlers, and thawed mice suit many individuals. Fish, if you feed it, should not be a goldfish diet. Thiaminase in common feeder fish leads to a vitamin B1 shortage.

Captive-bred, and a real menu

Wild garters are loaded with parasites and are protected in places. Buy captive-bred. Some keepers house similar-sized garters together. That can work. It fails when one animal takes all the food. Feed separately if you are unsure. Handling is fine once the snake is eating. Expect musk at the start.

A kinked spine, a refusal to eat, or a bad shed that will not clear needs a reptile vet. ARAV lists clinics that will see a small snake.

Garter snake questions

Can garter snakes eat goldfish?

Goldfish and other fatty feeder fish are a bad staple. They contain thiaminase, which breaks down vitamin B1. Earthworms, appropriately prepared fish, and rodents are the safer mix.

Do garter snakes live in groups?

They tolerate company better than kingsnakes when the animals are similar in size and food is plentiful. A hungry larger snake can still bite a smaller one. Watch feeding.

Why does my garter snake musk?

Fear. The smell is a defense. Less grabbing, and support under the body, reduces it. Wild-caught adults musk more and tame less.

A terrestrial cage that stays slightly damp

Garter snakes in the pet trade are active colubrids about 2 to 3 feet, with a lifespan around 10 to 15 years. Care is beginner for a keeper who can manage a varied diet. Temperament is active and hardy. A 20-gallon tank is a start, and a longer cage suits an adult that cruises. Give hides, a substrate they can burrow in lightly, and a water bowl large enough to soak. . They are escape artists relative to their size. The striped pattern is a full pet with a real lifespan, and the adult cage should be ready while the snake is still small.

Hold 72 to 85°F as a gradient on a thermostat. The warm hide can sit toward the top of that band and the cool end toward 72°F. Skip a desert basking layout. Humidity of 50 to 60 percent, plus the soak bowl, keeps sheds moving. A constantly soaked substrate under a cool hide is how belly scales rot, so damp and wet are different conditions.

Fish, worms, and breathing

The diet is fish, earthworms, and mice. Variety matters. Fish-only feeding, especially fatty or thiaminase-heavy fish as the whole menu, is a poor long-term plan. Earthworms and the occasional thawed mouse pink or fuzzy, sized to the snake, round the week out. Feed with tongs if the strike is eager. Some garters do better singly. Watch any group for a snake that never gets to the food.

The Garter Snake warm side at 72-85°F should be the first measurement if breathing looks labored at an adult size around 2 to 3 feet. Wheezing, mucus, or open-mouth breathing needs a reptile veterinarian. A cool, wet cage is a common setup error behind that picture. The shed still deserves a look: retained eye caps mean the 50 to 60 percent humidity or the soak bowl failed during blue eyes. An active, hardy garter in a measured 72 to 85°F gradient is a reasonable small snake. The diet is the part that takes more work than a mouse-only colubrid.
