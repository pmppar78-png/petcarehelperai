The hiss is the whole greeting

Bull snakes are Pituophis catenifer sayi, the big prairie gopher snakes. Adults are often 4 to 8 feet, and some pass 9. 20 to 30 years. Care for a 4-8 feet (1.2-2.4 m), some reach 9 ft animal at 75-85°F (24-29°C), basking to 90°F on a Carnivore (rodents) diet in a 6x2x2 feet minimum (adults) enclosure is beginner to intermediate. The beginner half is the diet of rodents and the dry cage. The intermediate half is the size and the defensive show.

They hiss hard, flatten, and buzz the tail in bedding so it sounds like a rattle. People kill wild ones for that impression. A captive-bred baby can learn that hands are not hawks. Some adults stay theatrical. Give a cage at least 6 by 2 by 2 feet, a warm side near 85 to 90°F, a cool side in the 70s, and humidity of 30 to 50 percent. They are strong. Latches matter.

Rodents, and room to stretch

Thawed mice and then rats are the food. A snake this large is a poor fit for a bedroom tub sold as a starter kit. Floor space beats gadgets. Handle with the body supported, and wait out the hiss instead of pinning the head every time. Pinned snakes stay defensive.

Buy captive-bred. Wild bull snakes are protected in some states and full of parasites besides. Weight loss or a refused meal in warm weather is a reason to use the reptile appetite page and then ARAV.

Bull snake questions

Are bull snakes venomous?

No. The hiss, the flattened head, and the vibrating tail copy a rattlesnake. The snake is a rodent eater, Pituophis catenifer sayi.

Why is my bull snake so loud?

Bluffing is the species. A loud hiss is a bluff, and the sound is the species, separate from pain and separate from a rattle. Calm handling reduces it. It may never go silent.

How big a cage does a bull snake need?

Give them at least 6 by 2 by 2 feet for adults. Some individuals reach 8 or 9 feet. Measure the snake you have and give it room to stretch.

Room to hiss, then settle

Bull snakes are large gopher snakes, about 4 to 8 feet, with some animals near 9 feet. Care sits at beginner to intermediate. Temperament is a defensive display that tames well: a loud hiss, a flattened neck, and a bluff that can include a closed-mouth strike. The sound is the greeting. Calm, repeated handling reduces it. Hold the bull snake at 75 to 85°F, and let one basking area reach about 90°F, with a thermostat on that warm end. Read the warm hide and the cool end. Night can drop toward the low end of the range. Rodents and a clean shed

The diet is rodents. Thawed mice or rats, sized to the thickest part of the body, on a schedule that lets the snake digest. These are enthusiastic feeders. Use a hook at the door and tongs for the prey so the hiss and the strike stay aimed at the food. House one snake. A bull snake that smells another animal on your hands may bluff harder.

The shed is the health watch-item in a low-humidity species. A complete shed in one piece means the moist hide and the water bowl did their job. Flakes and a retained eye cap mean the cage was too dry during the cycle, or the snake could not soak. Fix that before you pull at skin. Wheezing or open-mouth breathing is less common in a correctly dry, warm cage and still needs a reptile veterinarian if it appears. A 6-foot adult that hisses is a normal bull snake. A 6-foot adult in a 40-gallon tank with no thermostat is an unfinished setup.
