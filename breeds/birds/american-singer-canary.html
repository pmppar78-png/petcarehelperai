American Singer Canary Overview

The American Singer Canary is a small (5 in) canary that has captivated bird enthusiasts worldwide. Their moderate (melodious) noise level makes them better suited for homes where some vocalization is acceptable.

Their diet of seeds, greens, egg food requires consistent quality and variety.

The American Singer is a song-canary breed developed in the United States from roller and border-type birds. It is a domestic canary, about 5 inches long, with a listed lifespan of 10 to 15 years. It is not a color breed, and it is not a wild Atlantic canary. Serinus canaria, the island finch, is the ancestor. This page is the American breed kept for its song. Judges score that song. A yellow pet-shop bird chosen because the feathers looked bright is a different project from a bird whose family was selected for voice.

Noise is moderate and melodious. That phrase means a male's song, not a parrot contact scream and not silence. Care level is beginner, which is fair only when someone will learn the song calendar and the cage routine. The diet on the card is seeds, greens, and egg food. A seed stick alone leaves a gap. Egg food matters more in a molt and when a hen is breeding, and greens have to be washed and removed before they sour. Water cups in a 5-inch bird's cage foul quickly.

Housing starts at 24 by 12 by 18 inches, and a longer flight cage is the better song room. Horizontal space lets the singer hop and flutter between perches. A tall narrow cage with one swing is a poor song studio. Several perch diameters protect the feet. The cage belongs out of drafts, out of the kitchen, and away from a constantly running television if the point of the breed is a clean song. Canaries are not shoulder pets. Handling is a stress for most of them, and the relationship is listening and husbandry.

Temperament & Behavior

  • Noise: Moderate (melodious) - can be vocal, especially at dawn and dusk.

Song training is repetition and example, often with a tutor recording or an adult male, during the season when the bird is already inclined to sing. Forcing noise at a bird that is quiet, fluffed, or molting does not build a better voice. A quiet night matters. Cover or dim the cage so the singer gets a long uninterrupted dark period. Household clatter through the late evening leaves a bird that rehearses at the wrong hour and rests badly. Hens are not the show singers. Expect the sustained song from the male.

Ten to 15 years is long enough for several molt cycles and for the song to change as the bird ages. A young male in full voice and a seven-year-old with a shorter phrase can both be healthy. Weight loss, a soiled vent, or a bird that stops eating are the changes that need a clinician who sees small birds. Ordinary dog-and-cat clinics often decline canaries. Find that person before the first quiet week, because a 5-inch bird has very little reserve.

This breed's reputation sits on the song score, not on a photograph. Nothing here depends on a particular Commons image or on a color name. Type, stance, and the way the bird delivers its tours are what a song judge is listening and looking for. A keeper who wants red feathers should be reading the red-factor page. A keeper who wants speech should not be buying a canary at all. American Singers do not mimic human talk. They sing, and the household has to be willing to let that song happen in daylight and stop at night.
