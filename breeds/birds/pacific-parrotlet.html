Size and temperament

Pacific parrotlets, Forpus coelestis, are about 4.5 to 5 inches and an ounce, with a life often 15 to 20 years. They are bold, loud for the size, and affectionate on their terms. A cage that would satisfy a pair of finches is a jail. Give length to fly or at least to sprint, and hours on a play stand. Pellets, vegetables, and a little seed are the diet. Millet as the whole bowl fattens a parrotlet and leaves it short of vitamins.

They nest in pockets and drawers if you let them. Hormonal biting follows a dark hut and a lot of petting on the back. Keep hands off the back. Keep the cage in the room where people spend the day. A spare bedroom is a poor place for it.

Bites and illness

The beak bruises. Children should watch, not handle. A fluffed parrotlet crashes faster than a macaw because there is no body reserve. Weigh the bird. AAV is the vet search. The quiet bird page is what a puffed-up, silent parrotlet looks like.

Dry coast of Ecuador and Peru

Forpus coelestis lives in arid scrub and deciduous woodland along the Pacific slope of Ecuador and northwestern Peru, and in the Marañón valley. Birds of the World describes a bird that eats berries, cactus and tamarind fruit, and grass seed, and that nests in fence posts, tree holes, and old mud nests of other birds. Captive birds are often a little heavier than the wild 24 to 28 gram range. The ounce on the quick-facts table is the wild weight. Do not fatten the bird up to that number on millet. Males carry blue through the rump and the wing; females stay greener. Either sex will bite, nest in a pocket, and outlive a childhood.

Cage size and flight space

Bar spacing at or under half an inch keeps the head from passing through. Width matters more than a tall narrow tower. A cage about 30 inches wide is a start for one bird that also spends hours on a play stand in the room where people are. Left in a spare bedroom, a parrotlet learns to scream for company and then bites the person who finally walks in. Skip the enclosed hut and the nest box unless you are deliberately breeding, which a pet household should not do by accident. Covering the cage on a schedule helps. Petting the back and the tail does not. That contact is courtship, and courtship in a bird with no mate turns into eggs, biting, and a bird that guards the couch.

Daily diet

A measured tablespoon of a small-parrot pellet in the morning, a pile of chopped vegetable about that size (pepper, carrot top, kale, a little apple), and a short sprig of millet as a training reward is a day. Seed as the bowl pushes the ounce toward an ounce and a half, and the keel disappears under fat. Fresh water daily. No avocado, no chocolate, no fruit pits, no alcohol, and nothing caffeinated. Pull soft food after a few hours so it does not sour. A gram scale once a week tells you more than a glance. Avian practice treats a loss around a tenth of the bird's usual weight as already worth a call.

When to call a veterinarian

Fluffed feathers, a tail bob, sitting on the cage floor, or a bird that is quiet when it is usually an opinion are same-day problems. There is no spare fat to burn. The quiet bird page is the picture. Find a clinic through the Association of Avian Veterinarians before you need the night number. Egg binding in females looks like straining, a wide stance, or a bird that cannot perch. That is an emergency. Take the bird in the same day. Psittacosis, a chlamydial infection, can pass to people as a flu-like illness. If the bird is sick and a person in the house is sick, tell the physician there is a parrot in the home. Do not start a leftover antibiotic and do not ask a forum for a milligram dose. Bleeding feathers, a bite wound on you or the bird, and vomiting are also same-day calls. Courtship regurgitation is different: the bird bobs and offers seed to a person.

Baths, nails, and the beak

Offer a shallow dish or a spray of lukewarm water. A parrotlet that bathes will soak the stand, which is the point. Nails that catch in fabric can be filed or trimmed by someone who can see the quick. A beak that is lined up wears on the pellet and on a mineral block or cuttlebone. A beak that is crossed or overgrown needs an avian vet. The cause is often liver disease or a bite injury. Sandpaper will not correct it. Do not use a dog nail dremel on a bird this size.

Foraging toys and supervised time out

Paper cups, a leaf of kale clipped to the bars, and a foot toy they can throw are enough. Rotate two toys rather than filling the cage until there is no flight path. Out-of-cage time is supervised. They crawl into drawers, under couch cushions, and into the gap behind the stove. Teach a step-up with a millet seed, in sessions of a few minutes, and stop before the bite. A second parrotlet may fight the first. The questions below already say pairs often fight. If you keep two, you need a second cage on the day they do.

Costs in October 2026

A US buyer in October 2026 should plan on a flight cage and stand somewhere between $150 and $400, pellets and produce around $20 to $35 a month, and an avian exam commonly $75 to $180 before tests. The bird from a breeder who will show you the parents is often $200 to $500. A pet-store bird at the low end of that range, hand-fed in a back room and already biting, costs less up front and more once the biting is yours. Budget the exam in the first month, before anything goes wrong.

CITES and imports

Parrots are listed on CITES Appendix II unless a species sits on Appendix I. Forpus coelestis is in that Appendix II group: trade is regulated because the birds could become threatened, not because the species is already rare. The US Wild Bird Conservation Act sharply limits imports of wild-caught exotic birds. Buy a captive-bred parrotlet. Ask for a closed band or for the breeder's name and the hatch date. A seller who says the bird was "imported last month" is offering a recent import, and the Wild Bird Conservation Act sharply limits wild-caught birds. Apartment rules and some cities restrict birds even when the state does not. Hawaii restricts a list of parrots and all lories; a parrotlet is not automatically on it, and a move there still starts with the Plant Quarantine animal guidelines.

Suitability

The table says bold, feisty, and affectionate, with limited talking and a noise level marked low. The noise is low compared with a macaw and high compared with a finch. They bond hard to one person and nip everyone else, including the child who was promised a pocket parrot. Other pets are a hazard: a dog's mouth and a cat's paw are larger than the bird. A beginner who has already kept a budgie, and who will keep this bird on a stand for part of the day, can do this. A beginner who wants a quiet colorful object cannot.

Before you buy

Watch the bird eat. Feel, or ask to see, a weight written down. Eyes and nostrils should be clear, the vent dry, the keel present but not sharp as a knife. Ask what pellet is in the cup so you do not switch brands on the drive. Ask who the bird steps up for, and believe the bite you see more than the word "sweet." Confirm you may return a bird that is sneezing on day two. Have the cage, the gram scale, and the avian clinic's weekday number before the deposit.

Pacific parrotlet questions

Are parrotlets good for children?

No. They bite hard for their size and they bond to one person. A child who wants a pet to hold will be bitten, and the bird will be scared.

Can two parrotlets share a cage?

Often they fight. One bird with a person is the safer plan unless you have a true bonded pair and a cage large enough to separate them the day it goes wrong.

Do they talk?

A few words, sometimes. Buy one because you want a tiny parrot, not because you want a lecture.

Where do Pacific parrotlets come from?

The Pacific slope of Ecuador and northwestern Peru, plus the Marañón valley. They are dry-country cavity nesters. Pet birds should be captive-bred, not wild-caught imports.

How much does a parrotlet eat in a day?

About a tablespoon of small-parrot pellets, a similar pile of chopped vegetables, and millet only as a training sprig. Seed left in the bowl all day fattens a one-ounce bird.

Are Pacific parrotlets legal in the United States?

They are CITES Appendix II parrots, and the Wild Bird Conservation Act limits wild-caught imports. Captive-bred birds are the ordinary legal pet. Hawaii restricts some parrots, so check Plant Quarantine before a move.
