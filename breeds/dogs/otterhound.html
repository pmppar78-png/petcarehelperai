Otterhound Overview

The Otterhound is one of the most endangered dog breeds in the world, with fewer than 1,000 individuals globally. This large, shaggy hound was developed in medieval England for the specific purpose of hunting otters, which were depleting fish stocks in rivers and streams. With their webbed feet, waterproof double coat, and exceptionally keen nose (considered one of the most sensitive among all dog breeds), Otterhounds are superbly adapted for aquatic tracking. When otter hunting was banned in England in 1978, the breed lost its original purpose and numbers declined dramatically. Despite their rarity, Otterhounds are wonderful family companions - they are good-natured, playful, and possess a dignified charm that endears them to everyone they meet.

Diet & Nutrition

Large breed formula with controlled calories. Feed 3-4 cups daily in two meals. Use slow-feeder bowls to reduce bloat risk. Avoid exercise immediately after meals.

Housing & Environment

Needs a large, securely fenced yard. Not suitable for apartments. Their deep, resonant bark may disturb close neighbors. They need access to water for swimming.

Exercise & Activity Requirements

60 minutes of moderate exercise daily. Loves swimming and tracking games. Long walks and hikes are ideal. Not suited for repetitive fetch games.

Grooming Requirements

Brush the rough double coat 2-3 times weekly. Professional grooming recommended every 6-8 weeks. Clean ears frequently. The beard needs regular washing.

Socialization & Early Training

Early socialization is one of the most important investments you can make in your Otterhound. Between 3-14 weeks of age, puppies are most receptive to new experiences. Expose your Otterhound puppy to a wide variety of people, animals, sounds, surfaces, and environments in positive, controlled ways.

These classes provide controlled socialization opportunities while teaching basic manners. Continue socialization throughout your Otterhound's life with regular outings, new experiences, and positive interactions with people and other animals.

Training an Otterhound requires consistency, patience, and positive reinforcement. Their moderate trainability means they benefit from short, engaging training sessions that keep their interest.

Finding a Reputable Otterhound Breeder

When searching for an Otterhound, prioritize finding a responsible breeder who health-tests their breeding stock, socializes puppies from birth, and answers all your questions.

Alternatively, consider adoption through breed-specific rescue organizations. Many wonderful Otterhounds are looking for second chances.

Daily Life with an Otterhound

Living with an Otterhound is a rewarding experience that requires a consistent daily routine. Throughout the day, your Otterhound will appreciate having access to chew toys and puzzle feeders that provide mental enrichment while you attend to daily activities.

Otterhounds thrive on routine and predictability. Remember that every dog is an individual - pay attention to your specific Otterhound's signals and adjust the routine to match their energy levels and preferences as they age.

Are Otterhounds good family dogs?

Otterhounds can make good family dogs with proper socialization and training. They do best with families who understand their temperament and energy needs.

How much exercise does an Otterhound need?

Otterhounds require 60 minutes of moderate exercise daily.

What are the most common health problems in Otterhounds?

The most common health concerns in Otterhounds include Hip Dysplasia, Bloat (GDV), Epilepsy. Regular veterinary checkups (as recommended by the AVMA for all companion animals), maintaining a healthy weight, and age-appropriate exercise all contribute to a longer, healthier life for your Otterhound.

How much does it cost to own an Otterhound?

The initial purchase price for an Otterhound typically ranges from $1800 to $3500 from a reputable breeder. Emergency veterinary care and breed-specific health issues can add additional costs. Pet insurance is strongly recommended to help manage unexpected medical expenses.

Is an Otterhound Right for You?

Otterhound May Be Great For:

  • A keeper who accepts a rare hound that swims, smells, and may seize
  • Someone who will tell a surgeon about Glanzmann thrombasthenia before an operation
  • An owner with a yard, heavy-ear cleaning, and a neurologist if seizures start

Otterhound May Not Be Ideal For:

  • A suburban family who wanted a non-shedding rare dog
  • Anyone who will skip ear drying after every swim
  • A home that cannot house a large, vocal hound

Otterhounds are rare enough that every breeding is a genetic decision. Glanzmann thrombasthenia, a platelet disorder, is documented: bleeding that does not match the size of the wound. Tell the surgeon the breed. Idiopathic epilepsy is also more common here than in a random large dog. The heavy ears infect if they stay wet, the feet are webbed, and the coat will smell because these dogs find water. Bloat is the surgical emergency. A seizure log and a clinic that has seen the breed are more useful than another wash of the coat.
