A small dog with primitive opinions

Shiba Inus are Japanese spitz dogs of about 17 to 23 pounds and 13 to 17 inches. Lifespan is often 13 to 16 years. They are clean, sharp, and fond of their own people on their own schedule. The template line that calls every breed eager to please is wrong here. A Shiba cooperates when the deal is good and leaves when it is not. Prey drive is high: cats, squirrels, and a plastic bag can all trigger a chase. Fences need to be higher than they look, because these dogs climb and squeeze.

Children are a moderate fit. A Shiba that is grabbed will scream and may bite. Same-sex dogs can be tense. Socialization helps. It does not delete the breed. Exercise is a brisk walk and a chance to sniff, not endless fetch. They settle indoors better than a husky if the walk actually happened.

Skin, eyes, joints

Atopy and other allergies are the everyday disease: itchy feet, ear gunk, a coat that looks moth-eaten outside of a normal blow. Glaucoma and other eye disease show up; a cloudy or red eye is same-day. Patellar luxation and hip dysplasia are both on the list despite the modest size. Teeth need brushing. Seasonal shedding is normal twice a year. Year-round baldness is not.

Routine costs are listed in a wide band, about $1,350 to $4,000, because skin workups get expensive. Merck is the place to read about allergic skin disease without a shampoo ad. Itch that breaks the skin uses the itching page. A red eye or a limp uses the checker if you are unsure it can wait.

Shiba Inu questions

What is the Shiba scream?

A real, loud protest when a Shiba is restrained, bathed, or told no. It sounds like an emergency. Neighbors will think it is one. It is communication, and it does not mean the dog is injured every time, but pain still has to be ruled out the first time you hear it.

Are Shiba Inus easy to train?

No. They are smart and not eager to please. House training can be slow. Recall is unreliable off leash. If you wanted a dog that repeats a trick for praise, look at a sporting breed.

Do Shibas shed?

Hard. The coat blows twice a year and the house fills with fur. Allergies and skin disease are also common, so a bald patch is a vet visit, not only the seasonal shed.

There is no universal "right owner" for a Shiba Inu — people from all kinds of backgrounds and living situations make it work. What they tend to share is patience, consistency, and a genuine interest in learning about their dog's needs as those needs evolve over time. If that describes you, a Shiba Inu is likely to be a rewarding companion.

The satisfaction of sharing a home with a Shiba Inu is unlike that of any other dog — it is earned rather than freely given. Shibas do not hand out affection indiscriminately, which makes the moments when one chooses to curl up against you, or follows you from room to room just to keep you in sight, feel genuinely meaningful. Their cat-like self-sufficiency and obsessive personal grooming habits make them surprisingly easy housemates once basic rules are established, and owners who respect the Shiba's independent streak rather than fighting it tend to develop a quiet, durable trust with their dog.
