Norwegian Lundehund Overview

The Norwegian Lundehund is one of the most anatomically unique dog breeds in the world. Developed on the remote Lofoten Islands of Norway, this small spitz-type dog was bred specifically to hunt puffins nesting in steep, narrow cliff crevices. To accomplish this remarkable task, the Lundehund evolved extraordinary physical adaptations: six functional toes on each foot for superior grip, extremely flexible joints that allow the front legs to extend to the sides at 90-degree angles, the ability to tilt the head backward to touch the spine, and ears that can fold shut to protect against debris. These features make the Lundehund a true marvel of canine evolution. Despite nearly going extinct during World War II (the entire breed was rebuilt from just five surviving dogs), the Lundehund persists as a rare and fascinating companion breed.

The Norwegian Lundehund is a breed that commands attention not just for its physical appearance but for the depth of personality and capability it brings to a household. With a lifespan averaging 12-15 years, the decision to welcome a Norwegian Lundehund into your family is one that will shape your daily routine, activity levels, and emotional life for well over a decade. This breed's alert, energetic, loyal temperament is the product of generations of selective breeding for specific traits—understanding this heritage provides valuable insight into why your Norwegian Lundehund behaves the way it does and what it needs from you as an owner to truly thrive.

Temperament & Personality

The Norwegian Lundehund is known for being alert, energetic, loyal.

Their moderate exercise needs mean they require owners who can commit to regular physical activity and mental stimulation.

The alert, energetic, loyal nature of the Norwegian Lundehund is not a simple personality label—it is a complex behavioral profile shaped by breed history, individual genetics, early socialization experiences, and ongoing environmental factors. What this means in practice is that two Norwegian Lundehund from different lines, raised in different environments, can display meaningfully different behavioral tendencies while still sharing core breed characteristics.

Your veterinarian knows your Norwegian Lundehund best — always verify dietary choices with them, especially if your dog has existing health conditions.

Common Health Issues

Norwegian Lundehunds are generally healthy dogs, but like all breeds, they can be prone to certain conditions.

Socialization & Early Training

Early socialization is one of the most important investments you can make in your Norwegian Lundehund. Between 3-14 weeks of age, puppies are most receptive to new experiences. Expose your Norwegian Lundehund puppy to a wide variety of people, animals, sounds, surfaces, and environments in positive, controlled ways.

These classes provide controlled socialization opportunities while teaching basic manners. Continue socialization throughout your Norwegian Lundehund's life with regular outings, new experiences, and positive interactions with people and other animals.

Training a Norwegian Lundehund requires consistency, patience, and positive reinforcement. Their moderate trainability means they benefit from short, engaging training sessions that keep their interest.

Finding a Reputable Norwegian Lundehund Breeder

When searching for a Norwegian Lundehund, prioritize finding a responsible breeder who health-tests their breeding stock, socializes puppies from birth, and answers all your questions.

Alternatively, consider adoption through breed-specific rescue organizations. Many wonderful Norwegian Lundehunds are looking for second chances.

Daily Life with a Norwegian Lundehund

Living with a Norwegian Lundehund is a rewarding experience that requires a consistent daily routine. Throughout the day, your Norwegian Lundehund will appreciate having access to chew toys and puzzle feeders that provide mental enrichment while you attend to daily activities.

Norwegian Lundehunds thrive on routine and predictability. Remember that every dog is an individual - pay attention to your specific Norwegian Lundehund's signals and adjust the routine to match their energy levels and preferences as they age.

Are Norwegian Lundehunds good family dogs?

Norwegian Lundehunds can make good family dogs with proper socialization and training. They do best with families who understand their temperament and energy needs.

How much exercise does a Norwegian Lundehund need?

Norwegian Lundehunds require 30-45 minutes of daily exercise.

What are the most common health problems in Norwegian Lundehunds?

The most common health concerns in Norwegian Lundehunds include Lundehund Syndrome (intestinal disorder), Protein-Losing Enteropathy, Small Intestinal Disease. Regular veterinary checkups (as recommended by the AVMA for all companion animals), maintaining a healthy weight, and age-appropriate exercise all contribute to a longer, healthier life for your Norwegian Lundehund.

How much does it cost to own a Norwegian Lundehund?

The initial purchase price for a Norwegian Lundehund typically ranges from $1200 to $2800 from a reputable breeder. Emergency veterinary care and breed-specific health issues can add additional costs. Pet insurance is strongly recommended to help manage unexpected medical expenses.

Norwegian Lundehund May Be Great For:

  • Someone who wants the extra toes and the flexible neck and will not hike the dog like a sturdy spitz
  • An owner who will treat chronic diarrhea and weight loss as the breed disease until proven otherwise

Norwegian Lundehund May Not Be Ideal For:

  • A first dog bought for hiking and a thick coat
  • Anyone who will switch foods from a forum instead of doing blood protein and a GI workup
  • A home that cannot afford a specialist diet and repeated lab work

The Lundehund's extra toes and bendy neck are the visible traits. The disease is the intestine. Lundehund syndrome is a protein-losing enteropathy: chronic diarrhea, vomiting, weight loss, and low blood protein. Diet and a veterinarian who has read about the breed are the treatment, not a random grain-free bag from a forum. They are rare and independent, and they are a poor choice if you wanted a sturdy little spitz for hiking. Gastrointestinal signs in this breed are the breed disease until a workup says otherwise.
