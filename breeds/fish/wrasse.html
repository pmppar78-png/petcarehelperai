Wrasse Overview

The Wrasse is a varies (3-12 in) saltwater species that is well-suited for hobbyists with some experience. With a lifespan of 5-10 years, this varies species requires a minimum tank size of 50+ gal.

Wrasses thrive in water temperatures of 72-82°F with a pH range of 8.1-8.4. As a carnivore, they prefer meaty foods and live or frozen offerings.

This page groups wrasses that range about 3-12 inches and often live 5-10 years when the species is hardy and fed. That span is the problem. A 3-inch six-line and a foot-long tamarin do not share a tank size. Read the scientific name, then size the tank for the adult, not for the juvenile in the bag. A lifespan of 5-10 years is for a hardy species that eats, not for a cleaner wrasse that starves.

Temperament varies with the genus. Six-lines harass shy fish and eat pest snails, including pyramidellids. Fairy wrasses are peaceful plankton feeders. Coris and Halichoeres eat shrimp, crabs, and small fish. Do not add a wrasse to a reef whose cleanup crew is the thing you wanted to keep. Watch the fish at night. A wrasse missing at lights-out may be under the sand, not dead. Plan the adult size, the vet, and the daily work for the wrasse before it comes home.

Marine wrasses need 72-82°F, pH 8.1-8.4, and salt around natural seawater. This page lists a chemistry minimum of 30 gallons and an overview minimum of 50+ gallons. Use the larger number for any wrasse that swims in the open or shares the tank with other fish. Sand-sleepers need a sand bed deep enough to dive into. Every wrasse tank needs a lid. The chemistry block's minimum 30 gallons is the small-species floor. The overview's 50+ gallons is the safer plan for an open-swimming wrasse.

Common Health Issues

  • Marine Velvet: Dusty gold appearance; highly contagious and often fatal if untreated.
  • Parasites: Internal and external parasites; quarantine new additions.

The foundation of Wrasse health management is water quality — full stop. Regular testing and diligent maintenance are your best tools.

Diet and Nutrition Tips

Proper nutrition is the foundation of good health for your Wrasse.

  • Primary diet: Feed a high-quality staple food formulated for your Wrasse's dietary type (herbivore, omnivore, or carnivore). Look for foods with whole ingredients and minimal fillers.
  • Variety ensures complete nutrition and encourages natural foraging.
  • Feeding frequency: Feed small amounts 1-2 times daily.
  • Avoid overfeeding: Overfeeding is the most common nutritional mistake in fishkeeping. It leads to obesity, water pollution, and secondary health problems.

Tank Maintenance Essentials

  • Water changes: Perform 20-30% partial water changes weekly.

Cost of Ownership

These figures are averages, not guarantees. Some Wrasse owners spend less; others spend more due to health complications or premium product preferences. Where you live matters too — urban vet costs tend to run higher. The point is to go in with a realistic financial picture, not an optimistic one.

Budget a covered display of at least the overview minimum of 50+ gallons if the chemistry line's minimum 30 gallons is only enough for a small six-line kept carefully. Equipment failure is an inevitable aspect of aquarium keeping that should be factored into financial planning. Heaters, filters, lights, and pumps all have finite lifespans, and their failure—particularly heater or filter failure—can have catastrophic consequences for Wrasse if not addressed promptly. Maintaining a small emergency fund specifically for aquarium equipment replacement, or keeping backup equipment on hand for critical systems like heating and filtration, is a wise investment. Many experienced Wrasse keepers also invest in battery-operated air pumps and backup heaters as insurance against power outages, which can be lethal to tropical species within hours depending on ambient room temperature.

The cost-benefit analysis of quality versus budget equipment deserves careful consideration for Wrasse keepers. Higher-quality filters, heaters with accurate thermostats, and reliable lighting systems cost more upfront but typically last longer, perform more consistently, and are less likely to fail catastrophically. In aquarium keeping, equipment failure can result in livestock losses worth far more than the savings from choosing budget equipment. This is particularly true for Wrasse, where the animals themselves, along with any tank mates, plants, and established biological filtration, represent a significant investment of both money and time that is worth protecting with reliable equipment.
