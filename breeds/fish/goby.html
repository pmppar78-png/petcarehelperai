Overview

Gobies are one of the largest fish families, with thousands of species ranging from tiny nano fish to robust sand-sifters. They're beloved in the marine aquarium hobby for their peaceful nature, interesting behaviors, and symbiotic relationships with pistol shrimp. Many species are perfect for nano reefs and beginner marine tanks.

From the hovering firefish to the industrious sand-sifting gobies, this diverse group offers something for every aquarist. Their small size, hardy nature, and entertaining behaviors make them staples in reef aquariums worldwide.

Goby, on a marine label, is the family Gobiidae, not one fish. The animals in the trade run from about 1 inch to 6 inches and live 3 to 8 years, in tanks from 10 gallons up past 55, depending on the species. They need 72 to 82°F, pH 8.1 to 8.4, and specific gravity 1.020 to 1.025. Most of them are carnivores. Temperament is generally peaceful. Care runs from easy to moderate. The spread is the point. A neon goby and a sand-sifting goby do not share a care sheet just because both perch on their pelvic fins.

Watchman gobies, the Cryptocentrus and related fish, pair with a pistol shrimp. The shrimp digs. The goby watches. A watchman in a tank with no shrimp and no deep sand will sit in a PVC pipe and never show you the behavior you bought it for. Give them a sand bed several inches deep and a rubble pile the shrimp can brace. Do not put two pistol-shrimp pairs on top of each other in a 10-gallon tank. The shrimp will fight, and the gobies will have no burrow left.

Neon gobies and their Elacatinus relatives are the small cleaners. They pick at fish and they also need frozen foods and small pellets. They are a poor choice as the only cleaner in a tank full of ich, and they are a good choice as a small peaceful fish in a reef that already has a lid. They jump less than a firefish, and they still fit through a hole around a cable. A 10-gallon minimum can hold a neon goby and should not also hold a hawkfish that eats shrimp and small gobies.

Natural Habitat

Gobies inhabit diverse marine environments.

  • Range: Tropical and temperate seas worldwide
  • Environment: Sand flats, coral reefs, rubble zones
  • Behavior: Bottom-dwelling; many burrow or hover near substrate
  • Symbiosis: Many species partner with pistol shrimp

Popular Species

Many goby species are kept in aquariums.

  • Firefish Goby (Nemateleotris magnifica): White/red gradient; hovers mid-water; 3 inches
  • Yellow Watchman Goby (Cryptocentrus cinctus): Yellow; pairs with pistol shrimp; 4 inches
  • Diamond Goby (Valenciennea puellaris): Sand-sifter; 6 inches; keeps sand clean
  • Clown Goby (Gobiodon spp.): Tiny at 1.5 inches; lives in coral branches
  • Neon Goby (Elacatinus oceanops): Blue stripe; cleaner fish; 2 inches
  • Engineer Goby (Pholidichthys leucotaenia): Eel-like; burrows; 13 inches

Tank Requirements

Requirements vary by species type.

  • Tank Size: 10 gallons for tiny species; 30-55+ for sand-sifters
  • Reef Safe: Most species are completely reef safe
  • Substrate: Fine sand essential for sand-sifting species
  • Burrows: Provide caves or allow room for burrow construction
  • Lid: Essential for firefish and other jumpers
  • Filtration: Standard reef filtration

Diet & Feeding

Most gobies are carnivorous.

  • Staple Food: Frozen mysis shrimp, brine shrimp
  • Prepared Foods: Marine pellets (some species accept these)
  • Live Foods: Copepods, amphipods from refugiums
  • Sand-Sifters: Filter food from sand; need active sand beds
  • Feeding Challenge: Some are shy feeders; target feed if needed

Feeding Schedule: Feed 2 times daily; small amounts.

Most gobies on this card are carnivores, and the food has to land where that species lives. A sand-sifting diamond goby needs fine foods on the sand bed in a tank large enough for a 6-inch fish, often the upper end of the 10 to 55 gallon range. A neon goby will take small meaty bites in the rocks. A firefish hovers and picks from the water. Hold the reef at 72 to 82°F, pH 8.1 to 8.4, and specific gravity 1.020 to 1.025, and feed a little mysis, brine, or a tiny marine pellet once or twice a day. Leftovers buried in sand push nitrate past 20 ppm. A vegetarian flake meant for a tang does not count as feeding a carnivore goby.

Match the rotation to the job the goby does. A sand-sifter still needs targeted frozen food after the bed is clean, or it starves in a spotless tank. A watchman goby sharing a burrow with a pistol shrimp may eat at the entrance and ignore food on the far glass. If a goby that used to dart for mysis now sits in the hole, check salinity and whether a tankmate has taken the burrow before you change brands. Three to 8 years, depending on species, is long enough for one food to become a nutritional rut. Refusal of a known favorite is the early health flag. The family label carnivore is the clue, and the species is the menu.

Goby-Shrimp Symbiosis

  • Partners: Watchman gobies pair with pistol shrimp
  • Relationship: Shrimp digs/maintains burrow; goby acts as lookout
  • Communication: Goby alerts shrimp to danger via tail signals
  • Observation: Fascinating to watch their partnership
  • Pairing: Not all goby/shrimp combinations work; research compatibility

Behavior & Personality

Gobies display varied behaviors.

  • Bottom Dwellers: Most stay near substrate
  • Hovering: Firefish hover mid-water near bolt holes
  • Burrowing: Many excavate and maintain burrows
  • Sand-Sifting: Some continuously filter sand for food
  • Timid: Many are shy initially; need hiding spots
  • Jumping: Firefish especially are notorious jumpers

Sand-sifting gobies, Valenciennea and similar genera, eat tiny animals out of the sand. They starve on a bare glass bottom under a layer of sugar-sized sand that has no life in it, and they starve in a new tank. They also shovel sand onto corals and into overflows. A mature deep sand bed and regular frozen food, offered where the goby is actually sifting, is the combination that keeps one alive. The carnivore line on this card is doing real work. An algae wafer meant for a snail does not feed these fish.

Three to 8 years covers a small cleaner that lives a shorter life and a larger watchman that can pass half a decade if the burrow stays intact. Moderate care is the sand-sifter and the shrimp pair. Easy care is a neon goby in a stable reef with a lid and a little meaty food. Read the species on the tag before you trust the family name. A goby is not a blenny, it does not scrape film algae for a living, and it should not be bought to replace a lawnmower that starved.

Feeding is diagnostic because gobies do not all eat in the same place. A yellow watchman should pop from the burrow, grab a morsel, and back in, often with a pistol shrimp maintaining the hole. A firefish should hover and pick. A clown goby stays in coral branches. If you only watch the open water, you will declare a healthy burrow goby a failed eater. Feed the spot the species actually uses, and feed enough times that a peaceful fish is not outcompeted by wrasses. Two gobies that want the same hole will not share a meal politely. Give each a burrow. Appetite at that burrow, at 72 to 82°F and the right salinity, tells you more than color under actinic light.

Compatibility

Gobies are peaceful community fish: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced Goby owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

Compatible Tank Mates

  • Most peaceful reef fish
  • Clownfish, chromis, anthias
  • Blennies (usually)
  • Wrasses (non-aggressive species)
  • Corals and invertebrates
  • Pistol shrimp (for watchman types)

Incompatible Species

  • Aggressive fish that bully them
  • Other gobies of same species (usually)
  • Large predatory fish
  • Very competitive feeders (may outcompete shy gobies)

  • Clown Gobies: Can spawn in aquariums

  • Male Care: Males typically guard eggs

Common Health Issues

Gobies are generally hardy: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced Goby owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

  • Marine Ich: Common parasite; quarantine prevents introduction
  • Jumping: Many species jump; secure lids essential
  • Starvation: Shy feeders may not compete well for food
  • Stress: From lack of hiding spots or aggression
  • Sand-Sifter Issues: May starve in tanks without sufficient sand fauna

The foundation of Goby health management is water quality — full stop. Regular testing and diligent maintenance are your best tools.

A goby that abandons its burrow, breathes fast, or shows white specks against the sand is having a bad day you should not sleep on. Marine ich and velvet move quickly at 72 to 82°F, and a fish already thin from losing the food fight has less margin. Learn the normal: sand-sifters work the bed, watchmen guard the hole, firefish hover. A dusty coat or a fish on its side on the sand is a different picture. Do not put copper in a reef. Invertebrates, including a pistol shrimp partner, die from copper. Move the goby to a fish-only hospital tank if you can, and involve a veterinarian. This page has no dose to copy.

Quarantine a new goby in marine water at 72 to 82°F and specific gravity 1.020 to 1.025, with sand or a PVC burrow so a watchman or a shrimp goby will eat. A firefish needs a lid on the quarantine tank too, because they jump. Several weeks of watching the species feed in its normal spot reveals ich and velvet before the fish meets a reef you cannot treat. Never dose copper in the display. If copper is used, it is a veterinarian's call in a fish-only tank, and the pistol shrimp stays behind. A sand-sifter starved in a bare cup during quarantine is not a successful observation. Give it something to sift and a pinch of food on that sand.

Is This Fish Right for You?

Of the many small parts of Goby care, this is the one households most often postpone and most often regret postponing.

Gobies Are Great For:

  • Reef aquarium enthusiasts
  • Nano reef keepers (smaller species)
  • Those wanting the goby-shrimp symbiosis
  • Peaceful community tanks

Gobies May Not Be Ideal For:

  • Tanks without tight-fitting lids (jumpers)
  • Aggressive fish communities
  • Bare-bottom tanks (for sand-sifters)
  • Highly competitive feeding environments

Thriving is a goby doing the job you bought it for, in a tank sized to the species. A neon goby picking pests in 10 gallons, a watchman at a burrow with its shrimp, or a diamond goby actually sifting in a bed deep enough, at 72 to 82°F and specific gravity 1.020 to 1.025, can live toward the 3 to 8 year span. Surviving is a firefish on the carpet because the lid was off, or a sand-sifter starving on a bare-bottom reef. If you will match the footprint inside the 10 to 55 gallon range, feed a carnivore diet, and keep a lid on the jumpers, the fish is a fair project. A single care level cannot cover every genus in Gobiidae. Read the species.

Cost of Ownership

Goby consistent chemistry, controlled feeding, and deliberate quarantine sit at the centre of sustained aquatic welfare; these factors drive outcomes more than brand-name products.

Costs follow the species and the box. A tiny clown goby in 10 gallons still needs salt, tests, and meaty frozen food. A 6-inch sand-sifter needs a deep sand bed and a tank toward 55 gallons, and the salt and electricity scale up. People budget the fish and then skip water changes, so nitrate climbs past 20 ppm and the goby stops sifting. Plan frozen mysis as a repeating purchase and a spare pump before a long weekend. A reef at 72 to 82°F does not wait while a part ships. The pistol shrimp, if you keep that partnership, is part of the livestock value a cheap failure will take with the goby.

Heater failure hits small goby tanks first. Ten gallons can leave 72 to 82°F in an afternoon if the heater sticks on, and evaporation at the same time drives specific gravity above 1.025 if nobody tops off with fresh water. A larger sand-sifter tank moves more slowly and is easier to ignore until the goby has left the burrow. A failed return pump is worse for a fish that lives in a hole: the burrow goes stale, oxygen drops, and the goby declines while midwater fish still look passable. Read the thermometer and the refractometer. Keep a spare heater matched to the gallons you run, and a small pump if the main one dies. Do not top off a hot tank with saltwater.

The lid, the heater, and the pump that keeps a burrow oxygenated are the purchases that protect a goby. Most of these species are reef-safe, so the corals and the pistol shrimp around them are the value a bargain heater destroys. Three to 8 years is long enough to wear out a pump. Buy the replacement while the first one still runs, and match it to a tank inside the 10 to 55 gallon span rather than to a generic reef kit. A firefish lid is not optional equipment. It is the difference between a hovering fish and a fish on the floor. Spend there, and on a thermometer you can read without moving the rock that holds the burrow.
