Overview

Killifish are among the most colorful freshwater fish in the world, rivaling marine species in vibrancy. This diverse group includes over 1,200 species ranging from tiny nano fish to larger predatory species. They're particularly popular among advanced hobbyists and breeders.

Killifish are divided into two main groups based on their life cycle: "annual" species that live in temporary pools and complete their entire life cycle in months, and "non-annual" species that live in permanent water bodies and have longer lifespans. Understanding this distinction is crucial for proper care.

Killifish in the aquarium trade are annual and non-annual egglayers, chiefly Nothobranchius, Aphyosemion, and Fundulopanchax. They are not the North American banded killifish, Fundulus, even when a shop uses the same common name for both. Adults span 1 to 4 inches, and the listed lifespan of 1 to 5 years is species-dependent because a fish from a drying pool and a fish from a permanent forest stream age on different clocks. Care is rated moderate to advanced for that reason. The animal is only as straightforward as the species that was actually in the bag.

Many Nothobranchius live about a year because the wild pools they use disappear in the dry season. A heated aquarium does not cancel that calendar. Give the smaller annuals a tank in the 5-gallon part of the listed 5 to 20 gallon range, and move larger Fundulopanchax or a group toward the 20-gallon end. Temperature on the card is 68 to 80°F, pH 6.0 to 7.5, and hardness 2 to 15 dGH. Those are envelopes, not one ideal number. A peat-swamp Aphyosemion and a soda-pool Nothobranchius should not be forced into the same bucket of parameters.

Annuals are bred on peat. A shallow container of boiled, rinsed peat goes on the bottom, the pair drives eggs into it, and the moss is lifted out to be dried until it is damp rather than wet. Stored that way, the eggs wait out a resting period and are then wetted to hatch. That drying step is the usual breeding method for annuals. Leaving the peat submerged until it sours wastes the spawn. Non-annuals do not use this rest. Their eggs stay in water.

Natural Habitat

Killifish inhabit diverse environments worldwide.

  • Africa: Temporary pools, swamps, and streams (many annual species)
  • Americas: Streams, ditches, and coastal brackish waters
  • Asia: Rice paddies and slow-moving waters
  • Water Types: From soft acidic to hard alkaline; some tolerate brackish

Popular Species

Numerous killifish species are kept in aquariums.

  • Clown Killifish (Epiplatys annulatus): Tiny, peaceful; great for nano tanks
  • Gardneri Killifish (Fundulopanchax gardneri): Colorful; good beginner species
  • Lyretail Killifish (Aphyosemion australe): Beautiful finnage; peaceful
  • Golden Wonder Killifish (Aplocheilus lineatus): Larger surface dweller
  • Nothobranchius species: Stunning annuals; short-lived but spectacular
  • American Flagfish (Jordanella floridae): Hardy; eats algae

Tank Requirements

Requirements vary by species, but general guidelines: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced Killifish owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

  • Tank Size: 5-10 gallons for small species; 20+ for larger ones
  • Lid: Essential; many killifish are excellent jumpers
  • Plants: Dense planting provides security and spawning sites
  • Substrate: Fine sand or peat for egg-burying species
  • Filtration: Gentle; many prefer still or slow-moving water
  • Lighting: Subdued; floating plants help diffuse light

Diet & Feeding

Killifish are carnivorous and often prefer live foods.

  • Live Foods: Brine shrimp, daphnia, bloodworms, white worms
  • Frozen Foods: Bloodworms, brine shrimp, daphnia
  • Prepared Foods: Some accept high-quality micro pellets (training needed)
  • Variety: Essential for health and breeding condition
  • Important: Many killifish refuse dry foods entirely

Feeding Schedule: Feed 2-3 times daily; small amounts they consume quickly.

Proper nutrition for Killifish requires understanding not just what to feed, but how feeding strategies affect health, behavior, and water quality. A disciplined approach to feeding, where your Killifish receives the right amount of appropriate food at consistent intervals, is one of the most impactful things you can do for both the fish and the overall aquarium ecosystem.

Diet variety is essential for optimal Killifish health because no single commercial food provides the complete nutritional profile these fish require. Observing your Killifish's response to different food types also provides useful health information, as reduced interest in normally preferred foods is often an early indicator of illness.

Behavior & Social Structure

Killifish behavior varies by species.

  • Males: Often brilliantly colored; can be territorial with each other
  • Females: Usually plainer; more peaceful
  • Ratio: One male to 2-3 females reduces aggression
  • Surface Oriented: Many species spend time near the surface
  • Jumpers: Most killifish jump; secure lids mandatory

Aphyosemion australe, the lyretail, is the longer-lived non-annual most keepers meet. It spawns on a yarn mop or fine plants, the eggs incubate wet, and the adults are kept for years rather than a single season. Treating a lyretail with a Nothobranchius drying routine dries out viable eggs. The reverse mistake, holding annual eggs underwater for months, is just as costly. Read the genus before you set up a spawning dish.

The diet is carnivorous. Baby brine shrimp, daphnia, mosquito larvae, and similar frozen or live foods keep the belly rounded in a way flake rarely does. Temperament runs from peaceful to semi-aggressive, and males of one species will spar if the tank is a bare tray. Broken sight lines from leaf litter, oak leaves, and floating cover matter more than a bright display if several males share the water. Ammonia has to stay at 0 ppm, nitrite at 0 ppm, and nitrate under 20 ppm. These are small fish, and a dirty sponge filter shows up as clamped fins within a day.

Feeding behavior in Killifish reveals much about their ecological role and can be leveraged by keepers to promote natural behaviors and reduce aggression. Observing when, where, and how your Killifish feeds provides diagnostic information about their comfort level, health status, and social standing. Many successful Killifish keepers use varied feeding strategies including target feeding, scatter feeding, and enrichment feeders to promote natural foraging behaviors and reduce competition-related stress.

Compatibility

Tank mate selection depends on species: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced Killifish owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

Potentially Compatible

  • Small, peaceful fish (small tetras, rasboras)
  • Corydoras catfish
  • Otocinclus
  • Other killifish (with caution; males may fight)
  • Dwarf shrimp (with smaller, peaceful species only)

Generally Incompatible

  • Large or aggressive fish
  • Fin nippers
  • Very active fish that cause stress
  • Fish that outcompete for food

Breeding

Killifish are popular breeding subjects.

Non-Annual Species

  • Spawn in plants or spawning mops
  • Eggs can be left with parents or collected
  • Hatch in 14-21 days
  • Multiple spawnings over time

Annual Species

  • Spawn in substrate (peat moss)
  • Eggs require dry incubation period
  • Incubation: 2-6 months depending on species
  • Add water to trigger hatching

Common Health Issues

Killifish can be sensitive to certain conditions.

  • Velvet: Common parasite; appears as gold dust on body
  • Ich: White spot disease; treat with temperature and medication
  • Bacterial Infections: Often from poor water quality
  • Internal Parasites: Common in wild-caught specimens
  • OOD (Old Age): Annual species have naturally short lifespans

The foundation of Killifish health management is water quality — full stop. Regular testing and diligent maintenance are your best tools.

Recognizing early signs of disease in Killifish requires a trained eye and consistent observation. Establishing a daily observation routine—even just a few minutes of focused attention during feeding—helps you develop a baseline understanding of what normal behavior looks like for your specific Killifish, making deviations immediately apparent.

Quarantine protocols represent one of the most impactful health management practices available to Killifish keepers. This single practice prevents the majority of disease outbreaks in established aquariums and is considered essential by virtually all experienced Killifish keepers.

Is This Fish Right for You?

People often underestimate how much this piece of a Killifish's routine influences later health outcomes.

Killifish Are Great For:

  • Hobbyists seeking colorful, unique fish
  • Those interested in breeding projects
  • Nano tank enthusiasts (many small species)
  • Aquarists willing to provide live foods

Killifish May Not Be Ideal For:

  • Complete beginners to fishkeeping
  • Those unable to provide live/frozen foods
  • Community tanks with incompatible fish
  • Hobbyists wanting long-lived pets (annual species)

The best way to decide if a Killifish is right for your tank is to talk to people who already keep them. That kind of practical insight is more valuable than any care sheet.

A well-maintained Killifish is one of the more satisfying species to keep, and most owners who take the time to get it right have no regrets.

Cost of Ownership

Buy a named species, decide whether you are keeping a peat-spawning annual or a mop-spawning non-annual, and size the tank to that fish. A mixed cup of unidentified killifish will either fight or fail to spawn, and an annual kept like a community tetra simply reaches the end of its year while the tank still looks new. The moderate-to-advanced rating is about that planning, not about exotic equipment.

The true cost of keeping Killifish extends well beyond the initial purchase price and basic equipment. Many aspiring Killifish keepers underestimate these ongoing costs, which can lead to cut corners that ultimately compromise fish health and result in even greater expenses. A realistic budget that accounts for both routine maintenance and occasional replacement of equipment provides a more accurate picture of what Killifish keeping actually costs.

Heaters, filters, lights, and pumps all have finite lifespans, and their failure—particularly heater or filter failure—can have catastrophic consequences for Killifish if not addressed promptly. Many experienced Killifish keepers also invest in battery-operated air pumps and backup heaters as insurance against power outages, which can be lethal to tropical species within hours depending on ambient room temperature.

The cost-benefit analysis of quality versus budget equipment deserves careful consideration for Killifish keepers. This is particularly true for Killifish, where the animals themselves, along with any tank mates, plants, and established biological filtration, represent a significant investment of both money and time that is worth protecting with reliable equipment.
