Corydoras Overview

Corydoras catfish, affectionately known as "cories" or "cory cats," are among the most popular and beloved bottom-dwelling aquarium fish. With over 170 described species (and likely many more awaiting scientific description), corydoras come in a delightful variety of sizes, patterns, and colors. Their constant foraging behavior, adorable "winking" (when they roll their eyes), and peaceful nature make them favorites among aquarists of all experience levels.

Corydoras are often recommended as "cleanup crew" fish, though this somewhat undersells their value. While they do scavenge uneaten food from the substrate, they require their own dedicated feeding and are fascinating fish in their own right. Their social nature means they should always be kept in groups of 6 or more of the same species.

Corydoras are armored South American catfish, a genus rather than one fish. Depending on the species they reach 1 to 3 inches and live 5 to 10 years. The tank minimum is 20 gallons for a school. Temperament is peaceful and social. Diet is omnivore. The mistake that defines bad cory care is keeping one. A single cory is a nervous fish that never quite settles, even in a pretty tank.

They are built to root in a group. Six or more of the same species, not a mix of leftovers that happen to share a family, is the school. Bronze corys do not fill the social need of a panda cory. Sterbai, aeneus, paleatus, and pygmaeus each school with their own. The barbels on the snout are sensory. Sharp gravel wears them down until the fish cannot find food and the mouth looks eroded. Sand, or a smooth fine substrate, is the bottom that matches the animal. They are not decoration for a cutting gravel bed.

Corydoras breathe at the surface. A dash up, a gulp, and a dive back down is normal, not a sign that the filter has failed. It is also why a lid matters and why a film of dust or oil on the water is a problem. They still need oxygenated water. A hot, still tank at the top of their range, packed with fish, makes those dashes frantic. Their preferred band tops out at 78°F. A discus tank in the mid-80s is too warm for the common species on this card.

Natural Habitat & Origin

Corydoras are native to South American waterways.

  • Native Range: Throughout South America, from Trinidad to Argentina
  • Habitat: Slow-moving rivers, streams, marshes, and pools
  • Environment: Sandy or muddy bottoms with leaf litter
  • Behavior: Schooling fish that forage together in large groups
  • Adaptation: Can gulp air at the surface for supplemental oxygen

Tank Requirements & Setup

Creating an ideal cory habitat is straightforward.

Tank Size

  • Minimum: 20 gallons for a school of 6
  • Recommended: 30+ gallons for larger groups
  • Footprint: Floor space matters more than height
  • School Size: Always keep 6+ of the same species

Essential Equipment

  • Filter: Gentle to moderate flow
  • Heater: Maintain stable temperatures
  • Lighting: Moderate lighting
  • Air Pump: Good oxygenation appreciated

Substrate (Critical!)

  • Best: Fine sand (allows natural sifting behavior)
  • Acceptable: Very smooth, rounded gravel
  • Avoid: Sharp gravel that damages barbels
  • Plants: Appreciate planted tanks with hiding spots
  • Cover: Driftwood, caves, and leaf litter

Some corydoras species (like C. sterbai) tolerate warmer temperatures and are suitable for discus tanks, while others (like C. paleatus) prefer cooler water. Research your specific species' needs.

Diet & Feeding

Corydoras are omnivorous scavengers that need dedicated feeding: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced Corydoras owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

Primary Foods

  • Sinking Pellets/Wafers: Formulated bottom-feeder food
  • Live Foods: Blackworms, tubifex (occasional)
  • Vegetables: Blanched zucchini, cucumber
  • Algae Wafers: Supplemental food

Feeding Guidelines

  • Feed sinking foods after lights out
  • Ensure food reaches the bottom
  • Don't rely on "leftover" food from other fish
  • Feed daily or every other day
  • Watch for rounded bellies (indicates good feeding)

Feed them on the sand. A sinking pellet, a tablet, and frozen bloodworms or daphnia after the lights dim reach the fish that wait until the tetras are done. Flakes that never leave the surface do not count as a cory diet. They are omnivores and they will pick through mulm. They are not algae eaters, and they will not clean a tank in the sense of replacing a water change. What looks like cleaning is foraging. If the only food in the mulm is rotten, the foraging makes the water worse.

Popular Corydoras Species

There are many wonderful cory species to choose from.

Common Species

  • Bronze Cory (C. aeneus): Hardy, bronze/green coloration
  • Peppered Cory (C. paleatus): Mottled pattern, cooler water tolerant
  • Panda Cory (C. panda): Black and white pattern, smaller
  • Albino Cory: Albino variant of bronze or peppered
  • Julii/False Julii Cory (C. trilineatus): Spotted pattern
  • Sterbai Cory (C. sterbai): Spotted with orange fins, heat tolerant
  • Pygmy Cory (C. pygmaeus): Tiny (1"), mid-water swimmer
  • Emerald Cory (Brochis splendens): Larger, iridescent green

Behavior & Temperament

Corydoras display charming, endearing behaviors.

  • Social: Must be kept in groups of 6+ of same species
  • Active: Constantly sifting and foraging
  • Peaceful: Never aggressive toward other fish
  • Surface Gulping: Occasionally dart to surface for air (normal)
  • Winking: Rapidly roll eyes - a unique behavior
  • Resting: Often rest together in groups

Five to 10 years is a real span if the barbels stay intact and the group stays together. A cory with red, worn barbels and a sunken belly is usually on the wrong substrate or losing every meal to faster fish. Beginner is a fair label for a sand-bottom 20-gallon tank with a school of one species and a filter intake they cannot get stuck in. It is a poor label for a single fish on crushed coral in a Rift Lake tank. The armor plates protect them from some bites. They do not protect them from bad company or from gravel.

Corydoras Catfish care quality tracks three controllable habits — parameter stability, feeding discipline, and quarantine protocol — more than anything else; these factors drive outcomes more than brand-name products.

Breeding Information

Corydoras can breed readily in home aquariums: Every time you adjust for something the Corydoras actually does, rather than what breed profiles predict, results improve.

  • Trigger: Cool water changes often simulate rainy season
  • Spawning: T-position mating behavior (female holds eggs in pelvic fins)
  • Egg Placement: Adhesive eggs placed on glass, plants, decorations
  • Egg Count: 10-20 eggs per spawning session
  • Hatching: 3-5 days depending on temperature
  • Fry Care: Remove eggs or adults; feed infusoria, then baby brine shrimp
  • Growth: Fry grow relatively slowly

Common Health Issues

Corydoras are hardy but can face some issues: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced Corydoras owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

Barbel Erosion

  • Symptoms: Shortened or missing barbels
  • Causes: Sharp substrate, poor water quality
  • Prevention: Use sand substrate, maintain clean water

Ich White Spot

  • Treatment: Raise temperature, half-dose ich medication (sensitive to meds)

Red Blotch Disease

  • Symptoms: Red patches on belly
  • Causes: Bacterial infection from dirty substrate
  • Treatment: Antibacterial medication, clean substrate

Before committing to a Corydoras Catfish, think less about whether this species is "easy" or "hard" and more about whether your setup matches its specific needs.

Corydoras are schooling catfish. Keep six or more of a species that actually matches, because shops mix bronze, albino, and panda corys and call them all corys. Sharp gravel wears the barbels down. Use sand or a smooth rounded substrate. They dash to the surface to gulp air, so leave access to the surface and do not let the water line and a tight lid make that impossible. They are not algae eaters. Salt and copper doses aimed at other fish can hurt them. A cory with no barbels and red skin is a substrate and water-quality problem.

Cost of Ownership

The true cost of keeping Corydoras Catfish extends well beyond the initial purchase price and basic equipment. While the upfront investment in a properly equipped aquarium (minimum 20 gallons for a school) represents a significant portion of the total cost, ongoing expenses for water treatment chemicals, filter media, electricity for heating and lighting, and food constitute the majority of long-term spending. Many aspiring Corydoras Catfish keepers underestimate these ongoing costs, which can lead to cut corners that ultimately compromise fish health and result in even greater expenses.
