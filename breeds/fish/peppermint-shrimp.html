Peppermint Shrimp Overview

The Peppermint Shrimp is a small (2 in) saltwater invert invertebrate that is an excellent choice for both beginners and experienced aquarists. With a lifespan of 2-3 years, this peaceful species requires a minimum tank size of 10 gal.

Peppermint Shrimps thrive in water temperatures of 72-82°F with a pH range of 8.1-8.4. As an omnivore, they accept a wide variety of foods.

Three jobs keep a Peppermint Shrimp going: water that stays put, omnivore grazing, plus targeted feedings two or three times a week if the reef is too clean in finished portions, and quarantine before the display. Water Quality Warning

Saltwater species are sensitive to parameter fluctuations. Use an RO/DI unit for water changes and test regularly. Never add Peppermint Shrimps to an uncycled or newly set up tank.

Common Health Issues

  • Marine Ich (Cryptocaryon): White spots on body and fins. Treat with copper-based medication in quarantine tank.
  • Marine Velvet: Dusty gold appearance; highly contagious and often fatal if untreated.
  • Molting Issues: Ensure proper water parameters and nutrition during molting.

The shrimp people want under this name are Lysmata in the peppermint complex, often kept because they pick at Aiptasia. Not every red-striped shrimp in a shop cup is that animal. Behavior & Temperament

Peppermint Shrimp are characterized by a peaceful disposition that influences their care requirements and compatibility.

  • General Disposition: Peaceful nature that defines daily interactions
  • Social Behavior: Specific social needs that owners should understand and accommodate
  • Activity Patterns: Natural activity cycles that influence care scheduling

A 10-gallon tank can hold one of these shrimp if it is mature enough to offer film, detritus, and a frozen-food feeding. It is a tight home once fish are added. Peppermint shrimp are slow, nocturnal eaters and they get picked off by hawkfish, large wrasses, and anything else that hunts crustaceans. Give them rockwork with real gaps, feed mysis or a small pellet after the lights dim, and do not house them with the perching predators that treat a 2-inch shrimp as the meal.

They molt. An empty shell on the sand is not a dead shrimp until you have counted the live animal. The new shell is soft, and that is when tankmates do the most damage. Iodine and trace elements in a normal reef mix, stable salinity inside 1.020 to 1.025, and no copper in the water are what get them through a molt. Copper treatments aimed at fish parasites are lethal to this shrimp. Move it out, or do not use copper in its tank at all.

Peaceful does not mean they ignore every sessile animal. A hungry peppermint can sample polyps. Feed them so pest-eating is a bonus rather than the whole diet. Beginner care is fair only if the tank is already mixed, aged, and free of shrimp-eaters. A new 10-gallon with a swinging salinity and a damselfish is how a 2-to-3-year lifespan becomes a weekend.
