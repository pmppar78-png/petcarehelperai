Overview

African Cichlids are among the most colorful and fascinating freshwater fish available to aquarium hobbyists. Originating from the great lakes of Africa, these fish display vibrant colors that rival marine species, making them extremely popular for home aquariums.

There are hundreds of species divided primarily by their lake of origin: Lake Malawi cichlids (Mbuna, Peacocks, Haps), Lake Tanganyika cichlids (shell dwellers, frontosa), and Lake Victoria cichlids. Each group has distinct characteristics, care requirements, and behavioral patterns that make them unique.

An African cichlid, on this page, means a rift-lake fish from Malawi, Tanganyika, or Victoria. It does not mean an oscar, an angelfish, or any other South American cichlid that shares the family name. Adult size runs from about 3 inches in a small mbuna to 12 inches in a large hap or Tanganyikan predator, and the lifespan line is 8 to 15 years. That range is why one bag labeled assorted African cichlid so often becomes a fight. The fish in the bag are not one species with one adult size.

The water is hard and alkaline. Hold the tank at 76 to 82°F, pH 7.8 to 8.6, general hardness 12 to 20 dGH, and carbonate hardness 10 to 18 dKH. Soft, acidic water that keeps a cardinal tetra happy will leave these fish washed out and stressed. A 55-gallon tank is the minimum on this page. It is already small once several mbuna start claiming caves. Ammonia and nitrite stay at 0 ppm. Nitrate belongs under 20 ppm, which means the filter has to match the bioload of fish that dig and eat all day.

Natural Habitat

The African Great Lakes are ancient, deep bodies of water with very specific water chemistry.

  • Lake Malawi: Rocky shorelines with sandy bottoms; alkaline, hard water
  • Lake Tanganyika: Rocky reefs, shell beds, and sandy areas; highest pH and hardness
  • Lake Victoria: Varied habitats from rocky to open water; slightly softer water

Understanding their natural habitat is crucial for recreating appropriate aquarium conditions and ensuring your cichlids thrive.

Tank Requirements

African Cichlids require thoughtfully designed aquariums to thrive.

  • Tank Size: Minimum 55 gallons for most species; larger is always better due to aggression
  • Substrate: Aragonite sand or crusite gravel helps maintain pH and hardness
  • Rockwork: Abundant rock formations create territories and reduce aggression
  • Filtration: Powerful filtration (aim for 8-10x turnover per hour); canister filters recommended
  • Lighting: Moderate lighting; avoid very bright lights without caves for retreat
  • Decorations: Avoid sharp decorations; use natural rocks like Texas holey rock or lava rock

Water Chemistry Note

African Cichlids require hard, alkaline water. If your tap water is soft or acidic, you'll need to buffer it using cichlid-specific buffers, aragonite substrate, or limestone rocks. Never mix African Cichlids with fish requiring soft, acidic water.

Diet & Feeding

Diet varies significantly by species type.

  • Mbuna (Malawi): Primarily herbivorous; feed spirulina-based foods, vegetables, avoid high-protein foods
  • Peacocks/Haps: Omnivorous; balanced diet of pellets, frozen foods, occasional live foods
  • Tanganyikan: Varies by species; research specific requirements

Feeding Schedule: Feed 2-3 small meals daily rather than one large feeding. Remove uneaten food within 2 minutes to maintain water quality.

Malawi Bloat Warning

Mbuna cichlids are prone to "Malawi Bloat" when fed high-protein foods like bloodworms or brine shrimp. Stick to vegetable-based foods for herbivorous species.

Mbuna are the rock dwellers. They graze aufwuchs, so the diet leans herbivore even though the group as a whole is listed as omnivore or herbivore depending on species. A pile of holey rock, and more than one cave per fish, is the layout. Haps and utaka want open sand and a longer swim. Tanganyikan shell-dwellers live in snail shells on sand and can be kept on a different plan than a mbuna pile, but they still want the same hard alkaline water. Mixing those strategies in one tank is how a shell-dweller ends up homeless.

Behavior & Social Structure

African Cichlids are known for their complex social behaviors.

  • Territorial: Males establish and defend territories, especially during breeding
  • Hierarchy: Clear pecking order develops; dominant males display brightest colors
  • Aggression: Can be highly aggressive; overstocking slightly helps diffuse aggression
  • Intelligence: Recognize their owners and display distinct personalities

Temperament runs from semi-aggressive to aggressive. One male of a given mbuna species, several females, and enough rock to break lines of sight is the usual pattern. Two males of the same species in a 55-gallon tank usually means one fish pinned in a corner. Overstocking a long Malawi tank can spread the chasing out, but only when the filter keeps up. Adding tetras, angelfish, or rams to calm the tank puts those fish in water they cannot live in, and the cichlids will harass them anyway.

Victoria cichlids are the third lake, not a color morph of a yellow lab. Many of the fish sold from that lake are captive lines of species that are rare or gone in the wild, and they still need the hard water and the space. Buy a named fish when the shop can name it. A juvenile that looks like a 4-inch neighbor may be a species that doubles that length and changes the whole stocking. Eight to 15 years is long enough for the largest fish in the bag to become the only one left.

African Cichlid stable water parameters, appropriately measured feeding, and a consistent quarantine protocol carry most of the welfare signal; these factors drive outcomes more than brand-name products.

Compatibility

Choosing compatible tank mates is crucial for a peaceful aquarium: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced African Cichlid owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

Compatible Tank Mates

  • Other African Cichlids from the same lake (with research)
  • Synodontis catfish (African catfish species)
  • Plecos (larger species that can hold their own)

Incompatible Species

  • Small, peaceful community fish (tetras, guppies, etc.)
  • Slow-moving fish with flowing fins
  • South American cichlids (different water requirements)
  • Fish requiring soft, acidic water

Breeding

African Cichlids are mouthbrooders with fascinating breeding behaviors: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced African Cichlid owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

  • Mouthbrooding: Females carry fertilized eggs in their mouths for 3-4 weeks
  • Egg Spots: Males have egg spots on anal fins to trigger spawning behavior
  • Breeding Setup: Provide flat rocks or caves for spawning sites
  • Fry Care: Females may continue protecting fry after release
  • Ratio: Keep 1 male to 3-4 females to reduce female stress

Common Health Issues

African Cichlids are hardy but susceptible to certain conditions: For real-world African Cichlid decisions, breed-specific advice routinely outperforms generic pet-care templates.

  • Malawi Bloat: Caused by improper diet; symptoms include swelling, loss of appetite, white feces
  • Ich (White Spot): Common parasite; raise temperature and use ich medication
  • Hole in the Head: Often caused by poor water quality or nutritional deficiencies
  • Fin Rot: Bacterial infection often from aggression injuries or poor water quality
  • Swim Bladder Issues: Can result from overfeeding or constipation

Is This Fish Right for You?

African Cichlids Are Great For:

  • Hobbyists wanting colorful freshwater fish
  • Those interested in observing complex behaviors
  • Aquarists ready for an intermediate-level challenge
  • People who enjoy species-specific biotope tanks

African Cichlids May Not Be Ideal For:

  • Beginners new to fishkeeping
  • Those wanting a peaceful community tank
  • Aquarists with soft, acidic tap water (without buffering commitment)
  • Anyone with limited space for a large tank

The question to ask yourself is not whether you can keep an African Cichlid alive, but whether you can keep one thriving. If you are willing to invest in those consistently, you and your African Cichlid will both benefit.
