Hermit Crab (Marine) Overview

The Hermit Crab (Marine) is a small (1-2 in) saltwater invert invertebrate that is an excellent choice for both beginners and experienced aquarists.

Hermit Crab (Marine) thrive in water temperatures of 72-82°F with a pH range of 8.1-8.4.

The hermit crab on this page is a marine hermit, a saltwater scavenger, not a land hermit of the Coenobita kind sold in souvenir shops. Adults in the trade are about 1 to 2 inches and live 2 to 5 years. A marine tank of 10 gallons is the smallest this card allows. Standard reef salt applies here too: 72 to 82°F, pH 8.1 to 8.4, specific gravity 1.020 to 1.025, nitrate under 10 ppm. Diet is omnivore and scavenger. Temperament is listed as peaceful. Care is beginner only if you already run a marine tank and you understand the shell problem.

A marine hermit dies in a land-crab cage, and a land hermit dies in this tank. The water has to be full-strength seawater, not brackish and not fresh. A 10-gallon reef or fish tank with live rock, a lid, and stable salinity is the minimum shape. They need to be submerged. They also need shells. A hermit in a shell that is too small, or a hermit with no spare shells in the tank, will kill a snail and take that shell. Sometimes it will kill a snail it does not even need, because the snail is in the way of the search.

Peaceful means they do not hunt fish. It does not mean they are safe with every invertebrate. Small hermits such as some Calcinus and Clibanarius are the usual reef janitors and still pick at snails, at tube worms, and occasionally at coral tissue if they are hungry. Larger hermits, including big Dardanus, are not a 10-gallon animal and will rearrange rock and eat what they can catch. Read the species. A crab already near 2 inches with a heavy claw may be at the top of this card's size and a poor fit for a nano reef full of astrea snails.

Common Health Issues

Feed them on purpose. Algae, leftover frozen mysis, and a sinking pellet are the scavenger diet. A hermit in a spotless new tank with no film and no target feeding will go hunting. That hunt is the snail deaths people blame on a mean crab. A fed crab in a tank with several empty shells of the next size up is a much calmer animal. Replace shells as the crab grows. A decorative painted shell from a craft shop can be toxic and is often the wrong shape. Plain empty marine shells are the ones to offer.

Recognizing early signs of disease in Hermit Crab (Marine) requires a trained eye and consistent observation. Behavior & Temperament

Hermit Crab (Marine) are characterized by a peaceful disposition that influences their care requirements and compatibility.

Two to 5 years is the span if salinity stays put and the crab can molt. Molting hermits bury or hide and should not be pulled out for inspection. An empty shell with no crab in it is a clue to go looking, not proof the animal died. They are sensitive to copper and to a lot of reef medications. A tank you plan to dose with copper is a tank that should not have a hermit as a permanent resident. Beginner care is a stable nano reef, a spare shell, and a little food. It is not a cup of fresh water, and it is not a dry terrarium with a sponge.

Keep the number small. Several hermits in 10 gallons will fight over the best shell even if each one has a spare, and the fights end with a torn abdomen, which is fatal because the abdomen is soft. One or two small hermits, shells available, snails you are willing to risk or no snails at all, is the stocking that matches the peaceful line. If the goal is algae control, a snail that cannot be evicted is the more peaceful tool. The hermit is a scavenger with a housing problem, and the housing problem is the whole hobby of keeping it.

Feeding behavior in Hermit Crab (Marine) reveals much about their ecological role and can be leveraged by keepers to promote natural behaviors and reduce aggression. Observing when, where, and how your Hermit Crab (Marine) feeds provides diagnostic information about their comfort level, health status, and social standing. Many successful Hermit Crab (Marine) keepers use varied feeding strategies including target feeding, scatter feeding, and enrichment feeders to promote natural foraging behaviors and reduce competition-related stress.

Marine hermit crabs need fully salt water, a spare shell assortment, and food that reaches the bottom: a marine pellet, algae, and a meaty bite. A freshwater hermit kit is a different animal. Copper medications will kill a hermit crab, and a crab that has abandoned its shell is already in trouble.
