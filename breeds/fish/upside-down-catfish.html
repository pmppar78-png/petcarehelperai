Upside-Down Catfish Overview

The upside-down catfish is a mochokid, Synodontis nigriventris, and it is not a pictus cat. Adults reach about 3 to 4 inches and the lifespan on this card is 15 years, which is long for a fish this size and long enough to make the housing decision matter. A group needs 20 gallons or more under the leaves they rest beneath. Temperament is peaceful and nocturnal. Difficulty is beginner. The feature everyone notices is real: they swim inverted, belly toward the ceiling, and they will rest under a leaf or a piece of wood in that posture.

One upside-down catfish is a wasted purchase. It hides until you forget you own it. Several fish in a 20-gallon tank with broad leaves and dark wood will use the underside of the decor in the open, which is the point of keeping them. They are most active when the room is dim. A tank blasted with light and stripped of cover will look empty at noon and busy after dinner. Feed sinking and midwater foods in the evening: small pellets, frozen bloodworms, and similar omnivore fare. Daytime leftovers from the other fish are not a feeding plan for a 15-year nocturnal group.

They are peaceful toward fish that are not small enough to be eaten. A 4-inch catfish can take a tiny fry or a shrimp. Do not house them with a breeding colony of dwarf shrimp and expect both projects to thrive. Ordinary community tetras of a decent size, peaceful schooling fish, and other calm residents are a better match. They do not have the long sharp spines and the 55-gallon requirement of a pictus, and they should not be stocked as if they did. They also will not clean the glass.

The card's costs are the usual wide band: setup about 100 to 500 dollars or more, each fish about 5 to 100 dollars or more, annual maintenance about 200 to 500 dollars, and food about 50 to 150 dollars a year. Spread across a 15-year lifespan, the annual line matters more than the purchase price. A 20-gallon tank is the floor. A group that will live into its second decade is more comfortable in something longer, with a filter you are willing to maintain after the novelty of the inverted swimming has worn off.

Temperament & Behavior

Beginner care fits a cycled tank, a group, and evening feedings. It does not fit a brand-new aquarium and one fish bought as a curiosity. They can be startled into the decor and they appreciate a lid. Provide shade. A pale, skittish animal that never inverts is often a fish with nowhere to tuck under, or a fish that is not S. nigriventris at all. Other Synodontis species swim the right way up and get larger. Buy the one that is already demonstrating the belly-up posture in the shop, and give that group the years the card allows.

Because they live under the wood, a dead fish is easy to miss and a 15-year plan is easy to neglect in year six. Look for every member of the group at feeding time. Gravel vacuums should avoid collapsing the caves they use by day. Peaceful and nocturnal is not the same as invisible and maintenance-free. The inverted swim is the display. The 20-gallon minimum and the decade-plus lifespan are the part you have to keep showing up for.

Daily Care Routine for Upside-Down Catfishs

What size tank do Upside-Down Catfishs need?

Upside-Down Catfishss need a starting tank of 20+ gallons or more. Larger systems are more forgiving on water chemistry and far easier on tankmate compatibility.

What fish are compatible with Upside-Down Catfishs?

With their peaceful nocturnal temperament, Upside-Down Catfishs are compatible with many community fish of similar size and temperament.

How often should I feed Upside-Down Catfishs?

Feed Upside-Down Catfishs 1-2 times daily, offering only what they can consume within 2-3 minutes.

Are Upside-Down Catfishs good for beginners?

Yes, Upside-Down Catfishs are excellent for beginning fishkeepers.

Keeping an Upside-Down Catfish is one of those experiences that gets better with time.

Upside-down catfish, Synodontis nigriventris, swim belly-up on purpose and feed at the surface that way. A group needs a planted tank, driftwood, subdued light, and a sinking or slow pellet they can reach. The belly is darker than the back, which is the camouflage for a fish that lives inverted. They are poor roommates for tiny shrimp.
