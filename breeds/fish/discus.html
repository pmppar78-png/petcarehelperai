Discus Overview

The Discus fish, often called the "King of the Aquarium," is widely considered one of the most beautiful freshwater fish in the world. Their distinctive disc-shaped bodies, vibrant colors, and intricate patterns have made them highly prized among serious aquarists. Discus are available in countless color varieties including turquoise, red, blue, leopard, pigeon blood, and many more.

While discus have a reputation for being difficult to keep, understanding their specific requirements makes success achievable. They demand pristine water conditions, warm temperatures, and a protein-rich diet. For those willing to meet their needs, discus reward keepers with stunning beauty and fascinating social behaviors.

Discus are Symphysodon, the round, laterally flat cichlids of the Amazon basin. Adults reach 8 to 10 inches across that disc and live 10 to 15 years. The tank minimum is 55 gallons for a group. Care is advanced. They want 82 to 88°F, pH 6.0 to 7.0, hardness only 1 to 4 dGH, and TDS around 50 to 150 ppm. Nitrate is listed under 10 ppm and ideally near 0. Ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm are marked critical. Temperament is peaceful but hierarchical. They are not a first community fish.

The group is the social unit. One discus in a community of mixed tetras spends its life dark, tucked behind the heater, and slowly thin. Five or more, in a tall tank with room to turn a 10-inch body, is when a pecking order forms and everyone still eats. The hierarchy is real. The smallest fish will be kept off the food if you feed in one spot. Spread the offering, and watch the fish at the bottom of the order. A discus that is always the last to the pile and always hiding needs a different group, not another medication.

Heat and soft water are the daily work. A tank at 84°F holds less oxygen and grows bacteria faster, so the filter and the water-change routine have to be sized for that, not for a cool livebearer tank. Large, frequent water changes are how keepers hold nitrate near the bottom of the range. Hard tap water has to be cut with reverse osmosis water and remineralized lightly. Guessing with peat and hoping is how the pH crashes overnight in a tank with almost no KH. Test before you celebrate a number.

Natural Habitat & Origin

Discus originate from the Amazon River basin where they inhabit specific environments: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced Discus owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

  • Native Range: Amazon River basin in Brazil, Peru, and Colombia
  • Habitat: Slow-moving tributaries, flooded forests, and quiet backwaters
  • Water Type: Very soft, acidic blackwater with tannins
  • Environment: Sheltered areas among submerged roots and vegetation
  • Wild Varieties: Heckel, Green, Brown, and Blue discus

The warm, soft, acidic waters of the Amazon explain why discus require these specific conditions in captivity.

Tank Requirements & Setup

Proper housing is critical for discus success.

Tank Size

  • Minimum: 55 gallons for a small group (5-6 fish)
  • Recommended: 75-125 gallons for optimal conditions
  • Group Size: Keep 5-6 minimum to distribute aggression
  • Rule of Thumb: 10 gallons per discus

Essential Equipment

  • Heaters: Quality heater (or two for redundancy) - temperature stability is critical
  • Filtration: Canister filter or sump with gentle flow
  • Thermometer: Digital thermometer for accurate readings
  • RO/DI System: Often needed for proper water parameters
  • Backup Heater: Temperature drops are dangerous

Decoration & Setup Options

  • Bare Bottom: Easier to keep clean, common for breeders
  • Sand Substrate: Fine sand if decorated
  • Plants: Hardy plants like Amazon swords, anubias (at warmer temps)
  • Driftwood: Provides cover and releases tannins
  • Minimal Decor: Less is often more for cleanliness

Water Changes Are Essential

Discus require frequent, large water changes - typically 25-50% daily for juveniles or at least 50% twice weekly for adults. Use RO/DI water remineralized to proper parameters. Test water frequently with a reliable API Master Test Kit.

Diet & Feeding

Discus require a high-protein diet for optimal health and color.

Primary Foods

  • Beefheart Mix: Traditional discus food (homemade or commercial)
  • High-Quality Pellets: Discus-specific formulas
  • Frozen Foods: Bloodworms, brine shrimp, mysis shrimp
  • Live Foods: Blackworms, white worms, brine shrimp
  • Commercial Discus Food: Freeze-dried and granules

Feeding Guidelines

  • Feed juveniles 4-6 times daily for growth
  • Feed adults 2-3 times daily
  • Remove uneaten food promptly (water quality)
  • Vary diet for complete nutrition
  • Color-enhancing foods improve vibrancy

Discus eat often, and the water has to survive that habit. Symphysodon in a group need 82 to 88°F, pH 6.0 to 7.0, and very soft water, about 1 to 4 dGH, with nitrate held near 10 ppm and ideally closer to zero. Offer a protein-focused omnivore ration, a quality discus granule plus frozen or gel food, several small meals rather than one dump, in a tank of at least 55 gallons for five or six fish. Pull leftovers immediately. Ammonia and nitrite are marked critical, and a warm tank turns waste into those readings faster than a cool community. Ten gallons per adult is the working rule once the fish are no longer juveniles.

Rotate proteins so a discus group does not stall on a single frozen blend. A granule in the morning, a meaty frozen or gel feeding later, and a lighter day when the water is already due for a change covers an omnivore that still leans on protein. Watch the pecking order. A healthy group of five or six all comes forward. The fish that hangs at the back and refuses a food it ate yesterday is your early signal, often before the eye darkens or the body darkens. Do not answer that by adding three new foods. Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and temperature. Appetite in a hierarchical, peaceful species is a status report.

Behavior & Temperament

Discus display complex social behaviors: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced Discus owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

  • Social: Prefer groups of 5-6 or more
  • Hierarchical: Establish pecking orders
  • Shy: Can be skittish, especially when new
  • Intelligent: Recognize owners and feeding times
  • Territorial: May establish territories, especially when breeding
  • Stress-Sensitive: React to environmental changes

The diet is omnivore with the emphasis on protein. A quality discus pellet or granule, frozen bloodworms, and brine shrimp are the ordinary foods. Beefheart was an old staple and is not a requirement. A fish that eats only one frozen food and refuses pellets is a fragile fish to travel with. Offer prepared foods while they are young and still learning. Color forms, pigeon blood and blue diamond and the rest, are the same genus with the same water needs. A fancy strain is not a hardier strain. Many of the most selected lines are thinner and more easily spooked.

They are poor roommates for cool-water fish, for nippy barbs, and for any cichlid that claims a cave and bites. A planted tank at 86°F is also a hard place to keep plants and a hard place to keep shrimp. Cardinal tetras are the classic dither because they can take the heat and the soft water. They still need a school and a tank bigger than a cramped 55 if the discus are fully grown. Ten to 15 years is the span if the group stays stable and the nitrate stays low. A discus that darkens, clamps, and stops eating is telling you the water or the hierarchy moved. Fix those before you shop for a cure.

Feed where you can see every fish. Discus are midwater cichlids from slow Amazon backwaters, and they should ease forward together at 82 to 88°F. Scatter the meal so the subordinate animal gets a share. A dominant fish that shoulders the others off a single pile is the social stress the group size is meant to dilute. Five or six is the minimum on this card for that reason. Target feeding with a pipette helps a thin fish without turning the tank into a competition. If the whole group refuses food and darkens, stop the feeding tricks and check the heater. At this temperature a failed thermostat shows up first as a missed meal.

Compatibility with Other Fish

Discus tank mates must tolerate high temperatures and peaceful conditions.

Compatible Tank Mates

  • Cardinal tetras (classic combination)
  • Rummy-nose tetras
  • Sterbai corydoras (heat-tolerant)
  • Bristlenose plecos
  • German blue rams
  • Clown loaches
  • Other discus

Incompatible Species

  • Aggressive fish
  • Fast, active fish that may stress discus
  • Fish that require cooler temperatures
  • Fin nippers
  • Boisterous species

Breeding Information

Discus breeding is challenging but rewarding: Your aquatic veterinarian and experienced Discus owners can offer perspective tailored to your situation.

  • Pair Formation: Let discus choose mates from a group
  • Spawning Site: Vertical surfaces like cones, pipes, or broad leaves
  • Egg Laying: 100-400+ eggs laid in rows
  • Parental Care: Both parents guard and fan eggs
  • Hatching: 48-60 hours at 84°F
  • Unique Fry Feeding: Fry feed on parental slime coat for first days
  • Fry Development: Begin eating baby brine shrimp after 1-2 weeks

Common Health Issues

Internal Parasites

  • Symptoms: White stringy feces, weight loss, darkening
  • Treatment: Metronidazole, praziquantel, improve conditions

Hole-in-Head Disease (HITH)

  • Symptoms: Pitting around head and lateral line
  • Causes: Poor water quality, hexamita, nutritional issues
  • Treatment: Metronidazole, improve water quality and diet

Gill Flukes

  • Symptoms: Heavy breathing, scratching, mucus
  • Treatment: Praziquantel, formalin baths

Discus Plague

  • Symptoms: Darkening, excess mucus, rapid death
  • Prevention: Quarantine all new fish, maintain pristine conditions

For Discus, prevention is always better than treatment.

Learn the group at feeding: eyes clear, bodies round without a pinched forehead, fins open, color even for the strain you keep. A discus that turns dark, clamps, breathes fast, or separates from the school has left that picture. White specks, frayed fins, and stringy feces are reasons to act while the fish still eats. Warm water at 82 to 88°F speeds both metabolism and disease. Look daily. This page is not a treatment recipe. If water tests show ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm and the fish still declines, call a veterinarian who sees discus. Do not run a home brew of remedies through a 55-gallon group.

Quarantine new discus at the same heat as the display, 82 to 88°F, in a cycled tank large enough for the group you bought. A cool hospital tub is a shock for a fish that lives this warm, and a single discus isolated from a hierarchical group often stops eating, which hides the problem. Several weeks of normal color and appetite is the bar before they join a resident group of five or six. Do not medicate on a calendar. If illness shows in quarantine, keep it out of the main tank and get veterinary guidance. A display at this temperature will spread whatever you add.

Discus Are Great For:

  • Experienced aquarists seeking a challenge
  • Those willing to commit to demanding maintenance
  • Keepers with space for large tanks
  • Hobbyists wanting the ultimate display fish
  • Breeders interested in challenging projects

Discus May Not Be Ideal For:

  • Beginners to fishkeeping
  • Those unable to maintain frequent water changes
  • Budget-conscious keepers (fish and setup are expensive)
  • Those wanting low-maintenance aquariums
  • Small tank setups

The useful opinion is from someone who has kept a discus group through a heater failure and a full set of water changes, not from a photo of a juvenile in a bare rack. Ask what temperature they actually hold, whether they can stay inside 82 to 88°F, and how they manage nitrate near 10 ppm. Advanced on this card is that workload plus soft water and a 55-gallon minimum. If the keeper you trust describes a fish that only colored up after the water got clean and the group got larger, you have a better decision tool than a price list.

Discus are satisfying when the group is calm, colored, and eating in soft water you test every day. They are a long disappointment when the tank cannot hold 82 to 88°F or when nitrate sits far above the 10 ppm line. Ten to 15 years is possible for Symphysodon that get a 55-gallon or larger home, five or six fish, and a protein-focused diet with the leftovers removed. Peaceful but hierarchical means you keep enough of them, not that any tankmate is welcome. Get those pieces right and the fish is as good as its reputation. Skip them and the reputation is the only thing left.

Cost of Ownership

A decade with discus tracks the water changes and the heater, not the fixture you bought first. Hold 82 to 88°F, pH 6.0 to 7.0, and nitrate as close to the 10 ppm ceiling as you can manage, ideally nearer zero. Replace filter media on a schedule so a warm, heavily fed tank does not creep up in ammonia, which this card marks critical. The group of five or six in 55 gallons or more is the husbandry. A new light will not repair a fish that has been living in the wrong heat. In year eight the same boring tests are still the job.

The true cost of keeping Discus extends well beyond the initial purchase price and basic equipment. While the upfront investment in a properly equipped aquarium (minimum 55 gallons for a group) represents a significant portion of the total cost, ongoing expenses for water treatment chemicals, filter media, electricity for heating and lighting, and food constitute the majority of long-term spending. Many aspiring Discus keepers underestimate these ongoing costs, which can lead to cut corners that ultimately compromise fish health and result in even greater expenses. A realistic budget that accounts for both routine maintenance and occasional replacement of equipment provides a more accurate picture of what Discus keeping actually costs.

Heater failure is the emergency this species is built around. Discus are listed at 82 to 88°F, and a single heater that dies during a cool night can drop a 55-gallon tank out of that band before morning. The fish darken, clamp, and stop eating, and ich finds them while they are down. A heater that sticks on and pushes past 88°F is the other failure, and it is just as serious in a tank you are also feeding heavily. Run two heaters, each able to hold the tank if the other quits, and a thermometer you read at feeding. If the filter stops, ammonia is critical at this temperature within hours, not days. A battery air pump buys oxygen during an outage. It does not hold 84°F or process the waste of a discus group.

Buy the second heater and the canister or sump before you buy a higher grade of discus. The fish are advanced, long-lived, and expensive to replace, and they share the tank with a bacteria colony that crashes if flow dies in 82 to 88°F water. A bargain heater with no backup is how a 10 to 15 year project ends in a weekend. Reliable gear costs less than a new group and a new cycle in a 55-gallon or larger tank. Service the o-rings and the impellers while the fish are healthy. The animals, the plants, and the mature filter are the value sitting in that warm water.
