Cardinal Tetra Overview

The Cardinal Tetra is a small (2 in) freshwater species that is an excellent choice for both beginners and experienced aquarists. With a lifespan of 4-5 years, this peaceful species requires a minimum tank size of 10 gal.

Cardinal Tetras thrive in water temperatures of 73-81°F with a pH range of 4.6-6.2. As an omnivore, they accept a wide variety of foods.

Cardinal tetras are Paracheirodon axelrodi, the neon's larger, redder cousin from the upper Rio Negro. Adults are about 2 inches and live 4 to 5 years. The tank minimum is 10 gallons, which is enough for a small school and tight once you keep the group size these fish actually need. They want 73 to 81°F and a pH of 4.6 to 6.2. That pH is more acidic than a standard community tank. Hard, alkaline tap water is why a school looks brilliant in the shop and fades at home.

The color split from a neon tetra is the whole body, not a red patch behind the dorsal. A cardinal carries the blue line and the red stripe from nose to tail. Neons stop the red about halfway. People buy cardinals, house them like neons in neutral water, and then blame the breeder when the red shrinks. Soft water, dim light, and a dark substrate are what keep the stripe. Leaf litter or a little botanicals can pull a hard tap-water tank down into the range. Chasing 4.6 with acid buffers in a tank you cannot test is how you crash it.

Cardinal tetras are a blackwater school, and the husbandry is the water they came from, not a hardy community label. Keep them at 73 to 81°F and pH 4.6 to 6.2, in at least 10 gallons, with ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm and nitrate near 20 ppm or under. They are peaceful omnivores of about 2 inches, living 4 to 5 years when the school is intact and the chemistry stays soft and acidic. A new group should be quarantined together. Splitting them into a bare cup stops them eating, and you learn nothing. Stable parameters plus a measured pinch of fine food beat any brand of conditioner poured in after the fish are already gasping.

Water Quality Warning

Finish the cycle before you even look at a school of cardinals. These tetras show the full neon-and-red stripe only in finished water, and an ammonia spike in a new 10-gallon tank wipes a group that shops still market as beginner. Test until ammonia is 0 ppm, nitrite is 0 ppm, and nitrate has appeared but stays near 20 ppm, at 73 to 81°F and a pH inside 4.6 to 6.2. Do not chase that pH with a powder the same day the fish arrive. Age the water, add leaf litter if you want the blackwater look, and only then acclimate the school. A newly filled tank is not a slightly inconvenient home. It is the wrong home.

  • Primary Diet: Omnivore.
  • Foods: High-quality flakes/pellets, frozen brine shrimp, mysis shrimp, and blanched vegetables.
  • Variety: Rotate different food types for balanced nutrition.

Thriving for a cardinal tetra is a tight school, a red stripe that runs the whole body, and soft acidic water that does not swing every time you top off. Surviving is one pale fish in a hard-water community at the edge of 73 to 81°F, hiding while louder tankmates eat the food. Give them at least the 10-gallon minimum, more length if the school is large, and an omnivore flake or micro pellet they can finish. If you will test pH and nitrate and keep the group together, the 4 to 5 year lifespan is realistic. If the tank is a new, uncycled, alkaline box, choose a different fish.

Diet and Nutrition Tips

  • Primary diet: Feed a high-quality staple food formulated for your Cardinal Tetra's dietary type (herbivore, omnivore, or carnivore). Look for foods with whole ingredients and minimal fillers.
  • Variety ensures complete nutrition and encourages natural foraging.
  • Feeding frequency: Feed small amounts 1-2 times daily.
  • Avoid overfeeding: Overfeeding is the most common nutritional mistake in fishkeeping. It leads to obesity, water pollution, and secondary health problems.

Tank Environment and Stimulation

  • Tank size: Always choose a tank that meets or exceeds the minimum recommended size for Cardinal Tetra. Larger tanks are more stable and forgiving of water quality fluctuations.
  • Water parameters: Maintain consistent temperature, pH, and hardness levels appropriate for Cardinal Tetra. Test water weekly and perform regular partial water changes.
  • Filtration: Use a quality filter rated for your tank size.
  • This reduces stress and aggression.

Tank Maintenance Essentials

  • Water changes: Perform 20-30% partial water changes weekly.
  • Filter maintenance: Rinse filter media in old tank water monthly.
  • Algae control: Some algae is normal, but excessive growth indicates nutrient imbalance. Adjust lighting duration and consider adding algae-eating species.
  • Water testing: Test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and pH at least weekly. Keep a log to track trends and catch problems early.

Living Environment

  • A stable location reduces stress and prevents temperature fluctuations.
  • Compatible tankmates: Research compatibility before adding new species. Aggression, different water parameter requirements, and size mismatches are common problems.
  • Substrate choice: Select substrate appropriate for your Cardinal Tetra's natural behavior, whether that is sand for digging species or planted substrate for aquascaping.

Behavior & Temperament

Cardinal Tetra are characterized by a peaceful disposition that influences their care requirements and compatibility.

  • General Disposition: Peaceful nature that defines daily interactions
  • Social Behavior: Specific social needs that owners should understand and accommodate
  • Activity Patterns: Natural activity cycles that influence care scheduling

They are peaceful omnivores and a schooling fish. Six is a bare group. Ten or more in a longer tank than the 10-gallon minimum is when they use the open water instead of hiding in one plant. A small pellet, crushed flake, and frozen baby brine or daphnia match a 2-inch mouth. They feed in the middle of the tank. Long-finned tankmates that nip, and cichlids large enough to eat a 2-inch tetra, are the wrong company. Angelfish and cardinals are a classic pair only when the angels are not yet large enough to swallow them.

Wild-caught fish still make up much of the trade, and a new school is sensitive to a fresh tank. Add them after the filter is stable, not the week the aquarium is filled. Nitrate belongs under 20 ppm, and any ammonia or nitrite reading means the school waits. A cardinal that clamps its fins and loses the blue line in the first three days is usually ammonia or a pH the fish was not acclimated toward. Drip them over from the bag. Dumping a soft-water fish into a hard tank all at once is a reliable way to lose a few.

Four to 5 years is the life if the school stays together and the pH stays soft. A lone cardinal in a community of boisterous barbs will not reach it. They are listed as beginner fish, and a captive-bred school in a planted tank with soft water behaves like one. A wild school in an uncycled 10-gallon tank with a high pH does not. The beginner label assumes the water is already in the range on the card.

Cost of Ownership

A cardinal tetra is a soft, acidic-water schooling fish from the Rio Negro, and it fades in a small, alkaline, unplanted tank kept too warm and too dirty. Keep them in a group of at least eight, in a cycled tank, and do not confuse them with the hardier neon if your water is liquid rock. They are not a beginner fish just because they are small and cheap. A single cardinal hiding in a corner is a stressed fish.
