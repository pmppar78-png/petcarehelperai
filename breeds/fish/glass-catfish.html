Glass Catfish Overview

Glass catfish in the hobby are Kryptopterus vitreolus, the transparent midwater catfish from Southeast Asian streams. The card lists adult size at 4 to 5 inches, lifespan at 7 to 8 years, and a tank of at least 30 gallons. They are peaceful schooling fish and the care level is intermediate. The intermediate part is the school. A glass catfish kept alone starves or fades. The body goes opaque, the fish hides, and it stops eating while the rest of the tank looks normal.

They are not bottom catfish. The body hangs in the middle of the water, angled, often with the tail down, and a healthy group moves together over open substrate and between plants. A school of six or more in a 30-gallon tank is the minimum social unit that lets them rest in the open. One fish behind the filter is a dying display. They do not become bold if you wait long enough. They become thin. Add the group on the same day, into a tank that is already planted and calm.

Feed them where they swim. A small pellet, frozen brine shrimp, and daphnia in the water column reach a mouth that will not grub in the gravel the way a cory does. Fast barbs and large tetras steal that food. Glass cats are slow, deliberate feeders, and they lose. Keep them with other peaceful midwater fish that are not in a feeding frenzy, or feed after the lights drop, when the glass cats come forward and the other fish have calmed down. A sunken belly on a transparent fish is easy to see and easy to miss because people look through the animal instead of at it.

They want gentle flow, subdued light, and plants that give a visual break without turning the tank into a current. A powerhead aimed through the middle of the tank exhausts them. Sharp gravel is a poor match for a fish that sometimes rests low. Sand or smooth substrate, and no fish that nips, because the fins are long and the skin is the whole appeal. Fin-nippers ruin a glass cat quickly. So do rough nets. Move them in a container of water when you can, rather than in a mesh that strips the slime.

Temperament & Behavior

Seven to 8 years is a long life for a fish people treat as a curiosity and buy singly. The 30-gallon minimum assumes the school. A larger, longer tank is where a group of adults can hover without stacking on top of each other. They are poor tankmates for boisterous cichlids and for any fish that eats small moving things at night. The transparency is health. A milky, patched, or suddenly dark glass cat is stressed, sick, or dying. Isolate a fish that turns opaque and check the group size and the feeding before you decide it is just the light.

Intermediate care is mostly social and dietary. The water itself is an ordinary soft, slightly acidic community tank, kept clean and quiet. They do not need blackwater so dark you cannot see them, and they do need enough light for you to notice a thin fish. Buy captive-bred animals when they exist in the shop, and refuse the offer of a single specimen as a centerpiece. There is no centerpiece. There is a school, or there is a fish that fades.

Housing & Environment Requirements

A minimum tank size of 30+ gallons is recommended for Glass Catfishs. Provide appropriate substrate, hiding places, and peaceful tankmates. A quality filtration system and heater are essential.

Diet & Nutrition

Feed Glass Catfishs a varied diet appropriate for their species. Overfeeding leads to water quality issues. Vary the diet to ensure complete nutrition.

Weeks of real use will make it obvious which elements are pulling weight and which can be pruned.

Common Health Issues

Common health issues in Glass Catfishs include ich (white spot disease), fin rot, bacterial infections, and stress-related conditions. Maintaining excellent water quality is the best prevention. Quarantine new fish for 2-4 weeks before adding to the main tank. Have aquarium salt and broad-spectrum medications on hand for emergencies.

Grooming & Maintenance

Remove uneaten food promptly. Trim live plants as needed. Monitor equipment function daily. Replace filter media according to manufacturer recommendations.

Beginner-Friendly Rating: Intermediate

Glass Catfishs are suitable for keepers with some experience.

Before acquiring a Glass Catfish, research thoroughly and prepare the habitat in advance. Join online communities and forums dedicated to fishkeeping to connect with experienced keepers who can offer guidance specific to Glass Catfish care.

Socialization & Enrichment

Rearranging decorations periodically provides novelty. For Glass Catfishs specifically, consider their natural habitat when designing your aquascape - this helps encourage natural behaviors and reduces stress.

Feeding enrichment is another important aspect. Live and frozen foods provide hunting stimulation that dry foods cannot. This knowledge helps you detect health issues early and optimize care.

Where to Find Glass Catfishs

Purchase Glass Catfishs from reputable local fish stores (LFS) that maintain clean, well-cared-for aquariums. Online sellers with strong reviews and live arrival guarantees are another option.

What size tank do Glass Catfishs need?

Glass Catfishss need a starting tank of 30+ gallons or more. Larger systems are more forgiving on water chemistry and far easier on tankmate compatibility.

What fish are compatible with Glass Catfishs?

With their peaceful schooling temperament, Glass Catfishs are compatible with many community fish of similar size and temperament. Good tankmates include other peaceful species that share similar water parameter requirements. Always research specific compatibility before adding new species to your aquarium.

How often should I feed Glass Catfishs?

Feed Glass Catfishs 1-2 times daily, offering only what they can consume within 2-3 minutes. Fasting one day per week can benefit digestive health.

Are Glass Catfishs good for beginners?

Glass Catfishs are suitable for fishkeepers with some experience.

Long-Term Commitment & Responsible Ownership

Owning Glass Catfishs is a commitment that spans their full lifespan of 7-8 years. Aquariums require daily attention, weekly maintenance, and ongoing expense. Many fishkeeping communities have members willing to help in emergencies.

Glass Catfish May Be Great For:

  • A keeper who will school at least six Kryptopterus vitreolus in a dim, planted tank
  • Someone who leaves open swimming room and peaceful tankmates
  • An aquarist who can tell this small species from larger glass cats that do not belong in a community

Glass Catfish May Not Be Ideal For:

  • One or two glass catfish. They fail alone
  • Bright, bare tanks with fast nippy barbs
  • A keeper who wants a centerpiece predator
  • Plan the adult size, the vet, and the daily work for the glass catfish before it comes home.

The decision to keep a Glass Catfish should be based on practical readiness, not just enthusiasm. Can you maintain the habitat consistently? Can you handle the costs?
