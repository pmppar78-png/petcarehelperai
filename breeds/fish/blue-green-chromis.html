Overview

The Blue Green Chromis (also known as the Green Chromis) is one of the most popular and beginner-friendly saltwater fish in the hobby. Their peaceful nature, hardiness, stunning iridescent blue-green coloration, and active schooling behavior make them perfect for both reef aquariums and fish-only setups.

These damselfish are an exception to the typically aggressive damselfish family, remaining peaceful throughout their lives. They're often recommended as "starter" fish for cycling new marine aquariums due to their hardiness, though fishless cycling is preferred.

Blue green chromis are Chromis viridis, a damselfish from Indo-Pacific reefs and the Red Sea. Adults reach 3 to 4 inches and the lifespan line is 8 to 15 years, which is long for a fish people treat as a cheap school. They need 72 to 82°F, pH 8.1 to 8.4, specific gravity 1.020 to 1.025, and a tank of at least 30 gallons. Nitrate under 20 ppm, with ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm, keeps the color from muddying. They are peaceful schooling fish, and a single animal is the wrong social unit.

On the reef they hang above coral heads and pick zooplankton from the current. In a tank that means a small marine pellet and frozen mysis or brine shrimp, not a diet of nori meant for a tang. They are omnivores, so an algae-based flake can be part of the rotation, but meaty frozen food is what puts weight on a fish that arrived thin from a wholesale system. Feed them where the school is, in the open water, or the food hits the sand and the wrasses get it.

Color is the husbandry tell. A healthy school reads blue-green and shifts as the fish turn. A chromis that has gone olive-brown is stressed, underfed, kept alone, or sitting in rising nitrate. The change is easy to blame on the light. It is usually the group size or the water. Buy at least six, and buy them together. A pair will harass a third fish that was added a month later. The peaceful label assumes a real school with enough room to spread out over the rock.

Diet & Feeding

Blue Green Chromis are easy to feed.

  • Staple Food: High-quality marine flakes or pellets
  • Frozen Foods: Mysis shrimp, brine shrimp, chopped seafood
  • Live Foods: Copepods, brine shrimp (occasional treat)
  • Variety: Varied diet maintains health and coloration
  • Feeding Style: Mid-water feeders; catch food as it floats

Feeding Schedule: Feed 2-3 times daily; small amounts they consume in 2 minutes.

Chromis viridis feeds in the open water above coral, so flake that falls straight into the sand never becomes the meal. In a reef of at least 30 gallons, held at 72 to 82°F, pH 8.1 to 8.4, and specific gravity 1.020 to 1.025, offer a small marine pellet plus frozen mysis or brine shrimp where the school actually swims. These omnivores can take an algae-based flake as part of the rotation, but a thin fish from a wholesale system puts weight on meaty frozen food. Feed a little, twice, and net what they miss so nitrate stays near the 20 ppm ceiling. A single chromis eating in a corner is not a school being fed.

Vary the omnivore menu across the week and watch who reaches it. Mysis one evening, a marine pellet the next morning, and a rinse of brine shrimp later will show you the fish that has stopped competing. A blue green chromis that hangs back and turns olive while the others stay blue-green is an early warning, often hunger, solitude, or nitrate, not a lighting effect. Keep at least a small group, the card's 3 to 5 fish in 30 gallons, so one animal is not picked off the food. If the whole school suddenly ignores a food they rushed yesterday, test salinity and temperature before you open another jar.

Behavior & Social Structure

Blue Green Chromis are active schooling fish: Health and behavior metrics for a Blue Green Chromis. A blue-green chromis is a schooling marine damselfish. One fish in a nano tank is the wrong plan. They need a group, swimming room, and stable saltwater parameters. They are hardy for a marine fish and they still die in a new, uncycled tank. Aggression shows up if the group is too small or the tank is too short. Buy the school, quarantine them, and do not add them to a tank that is still cycling.

  • Schooling: Best kept in groups of 5+ for natural behavior
  • Peaceful: Non-aggressive toward other species
  • Active: Constantly swimming in open water
  • Hierarchy: Establish pecking order within the school
  • Attrition: Numbers may dwindle over time due to aggression within the group

School Dynamics

Blue Green Chromis can be aggressive toward each other, and weaker individuals may be bullied to death over time. Start with larger groups (6+) and provide ample space and hiding places. This is normal damselfish behavior despite their generally peaceful reputation.

They are reef-safe in the ordinary sense. They do not eat coral or ornamental shrimp. They do jump when the school spooks, so the lid or the mesh screen has to be on. A 30-gallon tank holds a small group and gets tight once the fish are a full 4 inches and sharing the rock with larger fish. They occupy the upper half of the tank. A goby on the sand and a chromis school above it is a better split than two midwater species fighting for the same open water.

Eight to 15 years is the part the price tag leaves out. A two-dollar fish that lives a decade still needs a stable reef for that decade. Replace losses in the school promptly. A chromis left alone after its group dies does not become a centerpiece. It fades, hides, and stops eating. If the shop tank has one brilliant fish and five pale ones, the pale ones are the husbandry lesson, not a bargain.

On the reef these fish pack above branching coral and pick plankton from the current. Copy that in the tank with open water over rockwork, and feed in that upper band rather than at the substrate where wrasses and hermits win. A school of Chromis viridis should face the same direction and surge together. If one fish is pinned behind the rock during every feed, the group is too tight for the 30-gallon minimum or a newcomer was added late and is being harassed. Peaceful describes a real school with room, not three fish in a cube with no swimming lane. Scatter the food so the rear of the group gets a share.

Breeding

Breeding Blue Green Chromis is possible but challenging.

  • Spawning: Males prepare nest sites on rock or rubble
  • Courtship: Males display and attract females to nest
  • Eggs: Female deposits eggs; male guards and fans them
  • Hatching: Eggs hatch in 2-3 days
  • Fry Challenge: Larvae are tiny and difficult to raise; require rotifers

Keeping a Blue Green Chromis healthy means prioritizing water quality above everything else.

Color is the daily chart for this species. A healthy blue green chromis shifts between blue and green as it turns. Olive-brown, rapid breathing, white specks, or a dusty gold film means you look closer the same day. Marine ich and marine velvet both spread in water at 72 to 82°F, and a schooling fish that leaves the group is often the first to show it. Compare the loner to the fish still packing over the coral. Do not treat the display with copper if shrimp or corals live there. Move the sick fish to a fish-only hospital tank and get veterinary help. No dose belongs on this page.

Quarantine the school together. A lone Chromis viridis in a bare box often stops eating, which hides the very illness you are trying to see. Match 72 to 82°F and specific gravity 1.020 to 1.025, give them open water and a coral skeleton or rock to hover over, and feed mysis where they swim. Several weeks of watching the group eat and hold blue-green color is the point of the wait. Ich and velvet show here, before the fish meet a reef you cannot medicate. If copper is used at all, a veterinarian directs it in a fish-only tank. It never goes into the display with invertebrates.

Aquarium keeping is a hobby of specifics, and Blue Green Chromis is no exception.

Eight to 15 years is a long contract for a fish that shops sell as a cheap dither. A 30-gallon reef at 72 to 82°F with specific gravity held at 1.020 to 1.025, and a school large enough to rest above the rock, is what makes Chromis viridis worth walking over to see. Replace losses so the last fish is not left alone and fading. The peaceful schooling temperament on the card assumes that group. Commit to salt mix, testing, and a lid, because this species spooks and jumps. Do that, and the blue-green pack over the coral is still there years after the receipt has faded.

Cost of Ownership

Your actual costs for Blue Green Chromis care will depend on where you live, your animal's health, and the choices you make.

A school that can live 8 to 15 years quietly consumes salt, test kits, frozen mysis, and replacement parts for a reef of 30 gallons or more. People who price one fish forget that nitrate control, a protein skimmer where you run one, and electricity for 72 to 82°F continue whether the chromis were inexpensive or not. Cutting water changes to save salt is how color muddies and disease finds a thin fish. Budget the frozen food and the salt mix as repeating purchases, and keep a spare heater before you need it on a weekend when the display is already at the edge of the temperature band.

Small reef tanks punish a stuck heater. In 30 gallons, a failed-on heater can push Chromis viridis above 82°F while evaporation raises salinity if the top-off is neglected at the same time. A failed-off heater in a cool room drops a tropical school out of 72°F overnight, and the fish pack tighter, darken, and stop taking mysis. Either swing is when marine ich shows up. Check the thermometer against a second one. Size a spare heater to this tank, not to a guess, and keep a small pump or air stone ready if the return fails. A schooling fish in stale, hot, salty water declines as a group, which looks dramatic and is usually just equipment.

The equipment that protects a chromis school is boring: a heater that holds 72 to 82°F, a lid or mesh they cannot jump through, and flow that keeps plankton-style food in the water long enough to eat. Corals and invertebrates sharing that 30-gallon reef are worth more than the fish and are the reason a bargain heater is a poor place to save. Pumps and heaters wear out inside an 8 to 15 year lifespan. Buy the replacement while the first unit still runs. A specific gravity of 1.020 to 1.025 is easier to hold with gear that does not dump water on the cabinet.
