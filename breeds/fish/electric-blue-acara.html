Electric Blue Acara Overview

Electric blue acara are a blue captive line of Andinoacara pulcher, or a closely related electric-blue strain. They are not a wild electric species from a lake, and they are not a naturally occurring fish someone discovered already neon. Adults reach 6 to 7 inches and live 8 to 10 years. One fish or a young pair starts at 30 gallons. Temperament is peaceful for a cichlid, and the difficulty line says beginner to intermediate. The peaceful part holds until they pair and spawn. Then they harass any small tankmate that comes near the eggs.

The blue was selected in aquaculture. Some lines are sturdy and some are thin, shy, and prone to sitting on the bottom with clamped fins. Buy a fish that is already eating and that has a full body, not the palest juvenile in a bare wholesale tank. A 30-gallon tank is the floor for one fish or a young pair and gets small once two 7-inch cichlids are guarding a brood. A longer tank, with sand they can sift and a flat stone or a piece of wood to spawn on, matches the way a pair actually lives.

They are South American cichlids and they belong in neutral to slightly acidic community water, not in a hard alkaline Malawi tank and not with mbuna. The electric color does not make them an African cichlid. They also are not Jack Dempseys, green terrors, or oscars, even though a juvenile can look like a generic blue cichlid in a bad photo. Those fish get larger and meaner. An electric blue acara that is starting to terrorize a tank is usually spawning, not turning into a terror. The behavior drops after the fry are gone, and it comes right back at the next spawn.

A pair will claim a patch of substrate, clean it, and drive tetras, shrimp, and smaller cichlids out of that half of the tank. In a 30-gallon community the driven fish have nowhere to go. Plan for that before you add a male and a female. One acara in a tank of robust mid-sized fish, such as larger tetras, a school of Congo tetras, or peaceful catfish, is the calmer stocking. Two of them are a future pair even if they ignore each other as juveniles. They dig. Plants rooted in the spawn site will be uprooted. Anchor the plants you mean to keep, or put them in pots.

Feed a cichlid pellet and frozen or fresh meaty foods. They are eager eaters when they are healthy, and a fish that refuses food for more than a day or two while looking dark is a fish to isolate and watch. Eight to 10 years is a long life for a color form that some hatcheries push out weak. The beginner half of the care label fits a single healthy fish in a quiet 30-gallon tank. The intermediate half is the pair, the digging, and the week they spend trying to kill every neon tetra you thought was fine.

Temperament & Behavior

Do not buy them as a rare wild import. If a seller describes an electric species from a named lake, the story is wrong. The color is a line. Treat the animal as a medium cichlid with a temper that changes when the eggs appear, give it sand and a solid place to lay those eggs, and keep the small, fragile tankmates in a different aquarium. A blue acara that spends its life with its fins clamped in a mbuna tank is in the wrong water and the wrong company, no matter how good the color looked under the shop light.

Electric blue acaras feed in the middle of the tank and along the bottom, with less of the nonstop sifting a ram does and less of the lunging a big terror does. In 30 gallons or more, offer a pellet and a frozen food where a 6 to 7 inch cichlid can take it without being mobbed, and watch whether both fish in a pair eat. They are peaceful for a cichlid, not peaceful next to a frantic school that steals every cube. Scatter part of the meal so a subordinate animal gets some. If one acara stops coming up while the other guards a corner, you are seeing pair stress or illness, and the feeding pattern is the clue. Live and frozen foods keep them searching. A single pile under a decoration only feeds the bolder fish.

Housing & Environment Requirements

A minimum tank size of 30+ gallons is recommended for Electric Blue Acaras. Provide appropriate substrate, hiding places, and peaceful tankmates. A quality filtration system and heater are essential.

Diet & Nutrition

Feed Electric Blue Acaras a varied diet appropriate for their species. Overfeeding leads to water quality issues. Vary the diet to ensure complete nutrition.

Portions should match a 6 to 7 inch omnivore, not a pile meant for a fish twice that length. A cichlid pellet they can chew, plus bloodworm or brine shrimp, in amounts finished within a few minutes, keeps a 30-gallon tank from climbing in nitrate. The card's food range of about 50 to 150 dollars a year is plenty for that rotation. Overfeeding is the usual failure with a fish this calm, because it looks like it is always hungry and the keeper keeps pouring. A sunken belly is the other failure, usually a fish losing food to a tankmate. Feed so you can see the blue fish swallow. Eight to 10 years is a long time to get the amount wrong.

Use more than one food so a missed meal means something. Pellet, frozen brine, and an occasional earthworm or bloodworm give an electric blue acara a varied omnivore diet and give you a favorite item to test appetite against. A fish that refuses the worm it usually takes, and fades from bright blue toward gray, is an early health signal in a species that can look dramatic even when it is declining. Do not answer with a new jar every day. Note the refusal, test the water, and look at the fins. Variety is a tool for observation on a beginner-to-intermediate cichlid, not a shopping project.

Common Health Issues

Ich, fin rot, bacterial infections, and stress show on an electric blue acara as white grains on the blue, ragged fins, redness, or a fish that has gone dark and stopped coming to food. Clean water and a calm tank are the prevention that fits a cichlid listed as peaceful for its family. A 30-gallon tank with constant harassment will produce the stress half of that list. Quarantine new fish for a few weeks before they meet a resident pair. Keep medications out of the plan until you know what you are treating. This card does not list a dose, and aquarium salt is not a complete answer. If the fish declines in clean water, ask a veterinarian.

For Electric Blue Acara, prevention is always better than treatment.

Watch the blue at feeding. A healthy electric blue acara should be bright, fins spread, taking food in the open of a 30-gallon or larger tank. Your baseline is that brightness and the way the fish moves, not a generic cichlid posture. A sudden dark wash, a clamped fin, rapid breathing, or a refusal over several days is the deviation. White spot stands out on this color if you actually look, which is an advantage over a muddy green fish. Use the daily feed as the exam. A fish that has stopped eating among decorations is hard to catch later, so act while it still comes forward, and do not invent a treatment dose.

Quarantine electric blue acaras in a cycled tank with a cave and open space, held near the same conditions as the display, before they go into a 30-gallon community. They are peaceful for a cichlid and still territorial enough that a sick newcomer in the display means rearranging rocks to net it. Several weeks of bright color and steady eating is the observation you want. Do not medicate because the fish is new and blue fish sometimes arrive stressed. If ich appears, it stays in the quarantine tank, and a veterinarian sets any treatment. The display, especially with other species, should not be your experiment.

Grooming & Maintenance

Remove uneaten food promptly. Trim live plants as needed. Monitor equipment function daily. Replace filter media according to manufacturer recommendations.

Beginner-Friendly Rating: Beginner-Intermediate

Electric Blue Acaras are suitable for keepers with some experience.

Before acquiring an Electric Blue Acara, research thoroughly and prepare the habitat in advance. Join online communities and forums dedicated to fishkeeping to connect with experienced keepers who can offer guidance specific to Electric Blue Acara care.

Socialization & Enrichment

Lay out a South American cichlid tank, not a rift-lake pile. Electric blue acaras use open sand, a cave, wood, and plants that can take some digging, in 30 gallons or more. They are peaceful for a cichlid, so the scape should give a pair a corner without turning the whole tank into a fortress that traps timid tankmates. Shift a single piece if a fish is pacing a reflection. Do not rebuild the layout weekly. A pair that knows where the cave is spends its time in the blue you bought it for, instead of re-digging a new map every Sunday.

Scatter frozen food across the sand and let an electric blue acara search, then offer a pellet it can take in the water column. That mix matches an omnivore of 6 to 7 inches and gives you two ways to see appetite. Live foods from a clean source extend the hunt. They are not required every day. A fish that will no longer move for a scattered feeding has left the behavior you are using as a health check. On an 8 to 10 year cichlid, that change is worth a water test the same evening.

Where to Find Electric Blue Acaras

Buy electric blue acaras from a shop that can show you the fish eating and that keeps the tank clear, or from a seller with a live-arrival guarantee. The card's range of about 5 to 100 dollars per fish means color and size vary, and a cheap faded fish is not a bargain if it will not eat. Avoid tanks with white spots and torn fins. Quarantine on arrival. A beginner-to-intermediate cichlid still deserves a healthy start, and the blue only stays interesting when the animal was in good condition on day one.

Setup runs about 100 to 500 dollars or more, the fish about 5 to 100 dollars, yearly upkeep about 200 to 500 dollars, and food about 50 to 150 dollars, for a 6 to 7 inch cichlid that needs 30 gallons and can live 8 to 10 years. People who buy the color and delay the filter service end up with ich, fin rot, and a second purchase. Add media, a spare heater, and test kits to the yearly number. A peaceful cichlid is not a low-waste fish. The blue you are paying for fades in a dirty tank just as fast as any other color.

A 30-gallon tank cools and heats faster than the big cichlid tanks people compare it with. If the heater fails off, an electric blue acara drops out of a stable tropical range overnight and often stops eating. If it fails on, the same volume overshoots and the fish darkens and gasps. Either swing is a common door into ich and bacterial trouble for a fish that otherwise behaves well. Read the thermometer daily. Keep a backup heater sized for 30 gallons and a way to aerate if the filter quits. An air pump during a power cut helps oxygen. It does not process the waste of a 7-inch cichlid, so plan a water change once power returns if the filter has been off for hours.

A controllable heater and a filter you can open without destroying the aquascape are the gear that fits this fish. Electric blue acaras can be modest in price and still sit in a planted 30-gallon tank that took months to settle, alongside tankmates that are part of the same cost. A no-name heater is a weak place to save across an 8 to 10 year life. Buy equipment you can get parts for, and replace a noisy impeller before it stops. The fish is peaceful for a cichlid, which means the community around it is also yours to protect when a pump fails.

How often should I feed Electric Blue Acaras?

Feed Electric Blue Acaras 1-2 times daily, offering only what they can consume within 2-3 minutes. Fasting one day per week can benefit digestive health.

Is Electric Blue Acara Right for Your Aquarium?

The best way to evaluate whether a Electric Blue Acara fits your aquarium is to match three factors: the animal's baseline care demand, your household's realistic capacity, and the environmental constraints of your space. Households that get this match wrong usually do so on one specific dimension — undersized space, undersupplied daily time, or undermatched experience level — rather than across the board. Identifying which dimension is the limiting factor lets you decide whether to adapt the household or choose a better-fit animal.

A Electric Blue Acara rewards households whose routines already include the care cadence the species requires. It becomes stressful for households that must reorganise their schedule to accommodate care. Evaluate honestly rather than optimistically before committing.

Electric Blue Acara May Be Great For:

  • A cichlid keeper who wants the line-bred blue Andinoacara and will not buy a dyed fish
  • Someone with 30 to 55 gallons and tankmates too big to become food
  • An aquarist who knows electric blue is a color line, not a promise of a calm community fish

Electric Blue Acara May Not Be Ideal For:
