Longnose Hawkfish Overview

The Longnose Hawkfish is a small (5 in) saltwater species originating from the Indo-Pacific. Known for being semi-aggressive, perching, this species is rated beginner care level, making it an excellent choice for aquarists new to saltwater keeping.

Distinctive elongated snout used to extract prey from crevices; sits on coral branches surveying territory; red and white checkerboard pattern; lacks swim bladder

The hawkfish on this page is the longnose, Oxycirrhites typus. Adults reach about 5 inches and live 5 to 7 years. A single longnose needs 30 gallons at minimum. The care line says beginner because the saltwater recipe is ordinary. The diet is carnivore, built from mysis, brine shrimp, and small pellets. Temperament is semi-aggressive and perching. Reef safe is caution because the fish eats ornamental shrimp. It comes from the Indo-Pacific, and the shop price is about 30 to 60 dollars against a setup of roughly 200 to 400. The long snout is for pulling small animals out of crevices, not for show.

They perch on gorgonians and on any thin projection that looks like one. A tank with a sea fan, a tall rock spine, or a piece of branching structure gives them the seat they use in the ocean. From that seat they watch shrimp. A cleaner shrimp, a sexy shrimp, or a small crab is a meal, not a coworker. The caution is the whole invertebrate stocking plan. Corals are usually ignored. The cleanup crew is not.

From a water chemistry perspective, maintaining a saltwater aquarium for Longnose Hawkfish demands consistent attention to parameters including temperature (72-82°F), pH (8.1-8.4), and tank capacity (minimum 30 gallons). These parameters are not merely guidelines—they represent the range within which Longnose Hawkfish's metabolic processes, immune function, and reproductive behaviors operate optimally. Successful Longnose Hawkfish keeping therefore requires not just the right equipment, but a disciplined approach to monitoring and maintaining water quality over the long term.

Temperament & Compatibility

The Longnose Hawkfish is described as semi-aggressive, perching and is considered reef safe with caution monitor closely in reef tanks as individual specimens may develop coral-nipping behavior.

  • Compatible tankmates: Semi-aggressive community fish of similar size. Introduce simultaneously when possible to reduce territorial aggression.
  • Stocking density: Marine fish require more space per individual than freshwater fish.

Small fish can disappear for the same reason. A 5-inch longnose has a mouth that matches the snout, and a tiny goby is not safe because the hawkfish looks slender. Choose tankmates with enough body that they are not a lunge away from being eaten. One longnose per 30 gallons. A second hawkfish, even a different species, competes for the perch and the argument is one-sided. Beginner water care does not cancel the predatory part.

Hold the tank at 72 to 82°F and a salinity of 1.020 to 1.025. pH should stay at 8.1 to 8.4, ammonia at 0 ppm, and nitrite at 0 ppm. Nitrate under 10 ppm is the reef line and under 20 ppm is the fish-only line. Calcium at 380 to 450 ppm, alkalinity at 8 to 12 dKH, and magnesium at 1250 to 1350 ppm are there for the corals and the gorgonian you may have added as a perch. The fish itself needs the meaty foods. It will not graze film the way a tang does, and a pellet-only diet that it refuses should be swapped for mysis it will actually take.

Thirty gallons is the minimum for a single 5-inch fish that spends its life on a stalk of coral or rock. Give it that perch on day one so it is not pacing the glass. The 5-to-7-year lifespan is a reasonable expectation for a longnose that is fed and kept away from a second hawk. It is a poor expectation for the ornamental shrimp in the same aquarium. If the reef's point is the shrimp, this is the wrong perch predator. If the reef's point is corals and larger fish, the longnose is a beginner fish with a clear menu.

Tank Setup & Requirements

Tank Specifications

  • Minimum size: 30 gallon. Larger is strongly recommended for parameter stability.
  • Sump systems recommended for tanks over 50 gallons.
  • Water flow: Moderate to strong flow using powerheads or wavemakers. Provide areas of both higher and lower flow within the tank.
  • Lighting: Standard marine lighting adequate for fish-only systems.
  • Aquascaping: Live rock providing caves, overhangs, and swim-through passages. Ensure open swimming space for active species.

Diet & Nutrition

The Longnose Hawkfish is classified as carnivore. Proper nutrition is essential for maintaining health, coloration, and immune function.

  • Primary diet: Carnivore: mysis, brine shrimp, small pellets.
  • Feeding frequency: 2-3 small feedings per day. Small frequent meals are better than one large feeding.
  • Variety: Rotate between different food types to ensure complete nutrition. Offer frozen mysis shrimp, brine shrimp, and high-quality pellets.
  • Avoid: Overfeeding is the most common feeding mistake.

Proper nutrition for Longnose Hawkfish requires understanding not just what to feed, but how feeding strategies affect health, behavior, and water quality. A disciplined approach to feeding, where your Longnose Hawkfish receives the right amount of appropriate food at consistent intervals, is one of the most impactful things you can do for both the fish and the overall aquarium ecosystem.

Diet variety is essential for optimal Longnose Hawkfish health because no single commercial food provides the complete nutritional profile these marine fish require. Observing your Longnose Hawkfish's response to different food types also provides useful health information, as reduced interest in normally preferred foods is often an early indicator of illness.

Grooming & Maintenance

  • Equipment maintenance: Clean protein skimmer cup 2-3 times weekly. Replace filter media monthly. Calibrate refractometer regularly.
  • Algae management: Magnetic algae scrapers for glass cleaning. Clean-up crew (snails, hermit crabs) helps control nuisance algae.
  • Water testing: Test salinity, pH, ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate weekly. Test alkalinity, calcium, and magnesium for reef systems.

Is Longnose Hawkfish Right for Your Aquarium?

The best way to evaluate whether a Hawkfish fits your marine aquarium is to match three factors: the animal's baseline care demand, your household's realistic capacity, and the environmental constraints of your space. Households that get this match wrong usually do so on one specific dimension — undersized space, undersupplied daily time, or undermatched experience level — rather than across the board. Identifying which dimension is the limiting factor lets you decide whether to adapt the household or choose a better-fit animal.

A Hawkfish rewards households whose routines already include the care cadence the species requires. It becomes stressful for households that must reorganise their schedule to accommodate care. Evaluate honestly rather than optimistically before committing.

Longnose Hawkfish May Be Great For:

  • A fish-only or shrimp-free reef keeper who understands hawkfish have no swim bladder and perch
  • Someone with a lid and tankmates too large to be eaten
  • An aquarist who will feed meaty foods and skip ornamental shrimp

Longnose Hawkfish May Not Be Ideal For:

  • A reef stocked with sexy shrimp, small gobies, or nano fish
  • An open-top tank. Hawkfish jump from the perch
  • A keeper who wanted a peaceful community cleaner
  • Plan the adult size, the vet, and the daily work for the hawkfish before it comes home.

Keeping a Longnose Hawkfish is one of those experiences that gets better with time. The first few weeks involve a steep learning curve, but once you establish a routine, the daily care becomes second nature. Most long-term Longnose Hawkfish keepers say they wish they had started sooner.
