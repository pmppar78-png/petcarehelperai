Overview

The is a medium (7 in) saltwater species originating from the Hawaii. Known for being peaceful, active algae grazer, this species is rated intermediate care level, making it well-suited for hobbyists with some marine aquarium experience.

One of the best algae-eating tangs; unique bristle-like teeth scrape film algae from glass and rock; more peaceful than many tang species; distinctive yellow eye ring

The kole tang, Ctenochaetus strigosus, is a Hawaiian bristletooth tang of about 7 inches with a lifespan of 5 to 7 years. A single kole needs 75 gallons of swimming length. Intermediate is a fair rating for one bristletooth with a daily nori clip. It is a peaceful, active algae grazer, reef safe on this card, and a herbivore that should get nori, spirulina, algae, and the film it is built to comb. The price runs about 50 to 120 dollars, with a setup around 300 to 500. The mouth is the detail that separates it from a yellow tang. A kole has bristle-like teeth for brushing film off rock. It does not carry a scalpel at the base of the tail in the same way a surgeonfish such as a yellow tang does. It still has a caudal spine. Treat the tail with the same caution you would give any tang when you net it.

One tang per tank unless the tank is huge. A kole in 75 gallons owns the grazing circuit. A second tang, kole or otherwise, in that footprint is a chase that ends with torn fins and a fish that will not come out to eat. Seventy-five gallons is the minimum for a single 7-inch grazer, not a starter size for a tang collection. They swim more than they perch. Length matters. A tall tank without a lap to swim will show as a fish that only circles the heater.

Temperament & Compatibility

The is described as peaceful, active algae grazer and is considered reef safe, making it compatible with coral-dominated aquariums.

Temperature on this card is 72 to 78°F, cooler at the ceiling than a general reef that is allowed to reach 82. Salinity for this Hawaiian tang is 1.020 to 1.025. Keep the kole inside that cooler band rather than letting a summer tank sit at the top of some other species' range. Keep the kole's pH inside 8.1 to 8.4. Nitrate under 10 ppm is appropriate because this fish belongs on a reef with film to harvest, and under 20 ppm is the fish-only fallback. Alkalinity of 8 to 12 dKH, calcium of 380 to 450 ppm, and magnesium of 1250 to 1350 ppm support the corals it is rated to live with. Ammonia and nitrite at 0 ppm are non-negotiable for a tang, which will show stress on the skin before a tougher fish does.

Feed the film and then feed as if the film is not enough. Nori on a clip, spirulina, and algae-based foods every day keep a kole from starving in a tank you have scraped clean because you wanted the rocks purple. They graze constantly. A single daily pinch of flake is a yellow-tang myth applied to the wrong mouth. Reef safe here means the kole is not a coral predator. It can still knock loose frags while it combs, and the caudal spine can catch your hand, a net, or another fish during a scare.

Feeding behavior in reveals much about their ecological role and can be leveraged by keepers to promote natural behaviors and reduce aggression. Observing when, where, and how your feeds provides diagnostic information about their comfort level, health status, and social standing. Many successful keepers use varied feeding strategies including target feeding, scatter feeding, and enrichment feeders to promote natural foraging behaviors and reduce competition-related stress.

Intermediate care is the 75-gallon minimum, a single tang, constant herbivore food, and stable water in the 72 to 78°F band. The 5-to-7-year lifespan is typical for a kole that was given that grazing life rather than a bare quarantine tank extended into a permanent home. Do not buy it as a yellow tang that stays smaller. The bristle mouth, the Hawaiian distribution, and the spine at the tail are a different animal with a different tool kit and the same need for a large, quiet territory.

A new kole often arrives thin and pale. Give it live rock with real film, a nori clip on the first day, and no second tang to fight. Quarantine is wise for any tang because ich finds them easily, and a 75-gallon display is a bad place to discover spots. The card's peaceful label assumes the fish is the only surgeonfish in the system. It is peaceful toward dissimilar fish. It is not peaceful toward a rival with the same job.

The true cost of keeping extends well beyond the initial purchase price and basic equipment. Many aspiring keepers underestimate these ongoing costs, which can lead to cut corners that ultimately compromise fish health and result in even greater expenses. A realistic budget that accounts for both routine maintenance and occasional replacement of equipment provides a more accurate picture of what keeping actually costs.

Heaters, filters, lights, and pumps all have finite lifespans, and their failure—particularly heater or filter failure—can have catastrophic consequences for if not addressed promptly. Many experienced keepers also invest in battery-operated air pumps and backup heaters as insurance against power outages, which can be lethal to tropical species within hours depending on ambient room temperature.
