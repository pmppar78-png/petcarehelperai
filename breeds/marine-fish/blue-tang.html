Ground the care plan in the animal's observable traits rather than a breed summary; the personalisation is what drives the difference in outcomes.

Blue tangs are Indo-Pacific surgeonfish, bright as juveniles and still electric as adults, with a tail spine they can cut you with during a bad netting. They reach about 12 inches and can live 8 to 20 years. Temperature on this card is a slightly cooler 72 to 78°F, salinity 1.020 to 1.025, reef-safe, and active enough to need length. Care level is advanced.

A long tank and a grazing diet

One hundred eighty gallons is the minimum listed here, and the shape should let the fish swim, not hover in a column. Live rock for grazing, strong filtration, and nitrate kept low. Feed nori and other algae foods every day. A blue tang on flakes gets thin, pale, and sick. They are semi-aggressive and do not belong with a shy, slow tankmate in a tank that is already borderline small.

Marine ich shows up when these fish are stressed by capture, shipping, or a dirty bag-to-display transfer. Quarantine. A display reef is the wrong hospital. Captive-bred juveniles exist and cost more than the $40 to $80 sometimes listed for a tiny wild-caught juvenile. The cheaper number is often a small wild fish with a long medical bill attached.

Who should buy one

Someone who already runs a large marine system and wants a tang that will use it. Not a first saltwater fish, and not a gift because a child liked a film. The setup cost is the tank you already own or a multi-thousand-dollar build. Yearly salt, food, and electricity are hundreds of dollars.

Scratching on rocks, faded color, or rapid gill movement is a quarantine and water-chemistry problem first. Read the gasping guide, use the checker only as a triage reminder, and call an aquatic vet when the animal is worth more than another bottle of copper you cannot use in a reef.

Blue tang adult size

How big do blue tangs get?

About 12 inches. The juvenile in a store tank is not the adult size. This page's minimum is 180 gallons, and many keepers would rather see a longer tank than that.

What do they eat?

Algae first: nori, spirulina, and herbivore foods, with some mysis. They are Paracanthurus hepatus, a surgeonfish, not a cartoon omnivore.

Are they beginner fish?

No. The care level is advanced. They are active, semi-aggressive, prone to marine ich when stressed, and expensive to house properly.

Is the fish in the movie a fair model?

The species is real and the housing in a home is a six-foot swimming lane plus a reef-keeper's maintenance schedule. A small tank because a character carried one is how these fish die.
