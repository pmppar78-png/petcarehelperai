How Our Guides Are Created

Every guide on this site follows a structured editorial process designed to produce accurate, useful content that serves pet owners — not search engines.

Research Foundation

Each guide begins with veterinary literature review, drawing from sources like the Merck Veterinary Manual, AVMA guidelines, breed health databases (OFA, CHIC), and peer-reviewed journals. We do not rely on anecdotal sources or unverified claims.

Review Cycle

Health-critical content is reviewed quarterly. General care content is reviewed semi-annually. All guides are checked against updated AVMA, AAHA, and WSAVA standards. When veterinary consensus changes, affected guides are updated within 30 days.

What We Don't Do

We do not provide specific dosage recommendations, diagnose conditions, or claim to replace veterinary care. We do not fabricate expert credentials or invent endorsements. Affiliate relationships are disclosed and do not influence health guidance.

Limitations We Acknowledge

Online guides describe population-level trends, not individual predictions. Breed predispositions reflect statistical averages — your animal may differ significantly. No guide can account for your pet's specific health history. Always consult your veterinarian for individual care decisions.

Quick Answers: Most Common Pet Care Questions

Experienced pet owners consistently report that paying attention to this detail early on prevents larger problems down the road. Start with the fundamentals and refine your approach as you learn your pet's individual preferences and needs.

How Do I Stop My Puppy From Biting Everything?

Puppy biting is a normal developmental stage, but it needs to be redirected to appropriate outlets:

  • When puppy bites: Say "ouch" in a high pitch, immediately stop play, and redirect to an appropriate chew toy
  • Be consistent: Every family member must respond the same way
  • Ensure adequate sleep: Overtired puppies bite more (puppies need 18-20 hours of sleep daily)
  • Provide appropriate outlets: Frozen Kongs, teething toys, and supervised chew items
  • Never punish: Physical correction increases fear and anxiety, making biting worse

Most puppies significantly reduce biting by 5-6 months of age with consistent redirection.

What Is the Best Food for a Senior Dog?

Senior dogs (7+ years for large breeds, 10+ for small breeds) have different nutritional needs:

  • High-quality protein: Maintains muscle mass - seniors need MORE protein, not less (unless kidney disease present)
  • Fewer calories: Slower metabolism and reduced activity means fewer calories needed
  • Joint support: Look for glucosamine, chondroitin, and omega-3 fatty acids
  • Easy digestibility: Older digestive systems need easily absorbed nutrients
  • Appropriate fiber: Supports healthy digestion and regular bowel movements

Consult your vet before switching foods, especially if your senior has health conditions that may require prescription diets.

Why Is My Cat Peeing Outside the Litter Box?

Inappropriate urination is one of the most common cat behavior concerns, and it usually has a solvable cause:

Medical causes (rule out first):

  • Urinary tract infection
  • Bladder crystals or stones
  • Kidney disease
  • Diabetes (increased urination)
  • Arthritis (hard to climb into box)

Behavioral/environmental causes:

  • Dirty litter box (scoop at least once daily)
  • Wrong litter type or depth (most cats prefer unscented, fine-grained, 2-3 inches deep)
  • Box location (too exposed, near loud appliances, hard to access)
  • Not enough boxes (rule: one per cat, plus one extra)
  • Stress from household changes
  • Territorial marking (especially intact males)

Important: Always see a vet first to rule out medical causes before assuming the issue is behavioral.

How Often Should I Take My Pet to the Vet?

Dogs:

  • Puppies (under 1 year): Every 3-4 weeks until 16 weeks old for vaccine series
  • Adults (1-7 years): Annual wellness exam and vaccinations
  • Seniors (7+ years): Every 6 months with blood work recommended

Cats:

  • Kittens: Every 3-4 weeks until 16 weeks for vaccines
  • Adults (1-10 years): Annual exams
  • Seniors (10+ years): Every 6 months with blood work and urine testing

Other pets: Annual exams for birds, reptiles, and fish (for fish, focus on water testing rather than individual fish exams unless obviously ill).

How Much Should I Feed My Dog?

Feeding amounts depend on several factors and package guidelines are just a starting point:

  • Start with package guidelines for your dog's IDEAL weight (not current weight if overweight)
  • Divide into 2 meals for adults, 3-4 meals for puppies
  • Body condition check: You should be able to easily feel (but not prominently see) your dog's ribs
  • Adjust based on results: If gaining weight, reduce by 10-20%; if losing, increase
  • Account for treats: Treats should be no more than 10% of daily calories

Note: Overweight dogs may need 20-30% less food than package suggests. Consult your vet for weight management advice.

How Do I Introduce a New Cat to My Existing Cat?

Slow, gradual introductions are essential for success. Rushing leads to stress and aggression that's difficult to reverse.

Introduction timeline (2-4 weeks typical):

  1. Week 1: Keep new cat in separate room with own litter box, food, water, and bedding. Swap bedding between cats to exchange scents.
  2. Week 2: Feed cats on opposite sides of closed door, gradually moving bowls closer. They should associate each other's scent with positive experiences.
  3. Week 3: Crack the door or use a baby gate for visual contact during feeding. Watch for hissing (normal initially) vs. aggressive behavior.
  4. Week 4+: Short, supervised face-to-face sessions. Increase duration gradually. Never leave unsupervised until interactions are consistently positive.

Signs to slow down: Prolonged hissing, growling, or aggressive posturing means you're moving too fast. Go back to the previous step.

What Vaccinations Does My Puppy Need?

Core vaccines (required for all dogs):

  • DHPP: Distemper, hepatitis, parvo, parainfluenza - given at 6-8 weeks, 10-12 weeks, 14-16 weeks, then yearly or every 3 years
  • Rabies: Required by law - first dose at 12-16 weeks, then as required (usually every 1-3 years depending on state law)

Non-core vaccines (based on lifestyle/risk):

  • Bordetella: Recommended if boarding, grooming, dog parks, or daycare
  • Leptospirosis: Recommended in areas with wildlife and standing water
  • Lyme: Recommended in tick-endemic areas
  • Canine influenza: Recommended for dogs with high social exposure

Important: Puppies aren't fully protected until 2 weeks after their final puppy shot (typically 18 weeks). Limit exposure to unvaccinated dogs and high-risk areas until then.
